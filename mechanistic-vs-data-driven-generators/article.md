# Mechanistic and Data-Driven Synthetic Data Generators: The Trade-Offs, Measured on a Simulated Contact Centre

*Two Erlang queues and four tabular synthesizers, fitted to the same interval table and put to the same seven questions by a world whose truth is known.*

Synthetic data is produced in two ways that share a name and not much else. A data-driven generator, whether a Gaussian copula, a sequence of classification and regression trees, a generative adversarial network or SMOTE, estimates the distribution of the data you already have and samples from the estimate. What comes out is what was in the data, smoothed a little and wrong by the amount of the estimation error. A mechanistic generator starts somewhere else, from equations for how the data come about, with parameters and noise added on top. A susceptible-infected-recovered model of an epidemic, a compartment model of how a drug is absorbed and cleared, a queue, a stochastic differential equation for an interest rate, a market of interacting agents and a physics engine all belong to this second family.

The difference is where the information comes from. A mechanism carries information that is not in the data, namely the theory it encodes, and that buys three things a data-driven generator cannot supply. Every parameter and every causal link inside the generator is known exactly, so a method can be scored against a ground truth. Conditions the data never covered can be simulated, because the equations do not care whether a region of the input space was ever visited. And a parameter can be changed and the model rerun, which gives a counterfactual rather than a conditional. The price is that a mechanistic generator's errors are systematic rather than random. Running more simulations shrinks the Monte Carlo error and leaves the model error where it was. Roboticists call that residual the reality gap; an early use of the term is in the title of a 1995 paper by Jakobi, Husbands and Harvey on evolving robot controllers in simulation.

None of this is new. Econometrics has estimated structural models by matching simulated and observed moments since McFadden and Pakes and Pollard in 1989, and by indirect inference, where an auxiliary model fitted to real and simulated data is made to agree, since Gouriéroux, Monfort and Renault and Smith in 1993. Population genetics produced approximate Bayesian computation at the end of the same decade: Pritchard and co-authors' 1999 study of Y chromosomes already accepted simulated parameter draws whose summary statistics landed close to the observed ones, and by the time Beaumont, Zhang and Balding published their 2002 paper the method had its name. Machine learning has since replaced the accept-reject step with neural conditional density estimators, and the area now goes by the name simulation-based inference, surveyed by Cranmer, Brehmer and Louppe in 2020. Roboticists train controllers in simulators whose parameters are randomised so that the real world looks like one more draw, and engineering firms keep digital twins of plants and fleets, which a 2024 report of the US National Academies defines by the two-way traffic between the virtual model and its physical counterpart. On the data-driven side, statisticians have released synthetic microdata for privacy reasons since Rubin proposed it in 1993, and tabular synthesizers have become a product category.

What follows does not argue for either family. It lays out the trade-offs between them, and wherever a trade-off can be measured, it measures it. Measuring needs a world in which the truth is known, which means the referee has to be a simulator as well. I chose a contact centre for two reasons. Queueing models of call centres have a long record that has been checked against real data, so the mechanistic side is not a straw man. And contact-centre platforms export exactly the kind of interval table that tabular synthesizers are built for, so the data-driven side is not one either.

All code, results and figures are in the folder [mechanistic-vs-data-driven-generators](https://github.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/tree/main/mechanistic-vs-data-driven-generators) of my Medium-Blog repository. Every number below is printed by one script into `results/summary.txt`.

## A world whose truth is known

The centre in the simulation is open from eight in the morning to eight at night on weekdays, and everything about it is reported in half-hour intervals. Fresh calls arrive as a Poisson process within each interval, but the rate is not fixed. It follows an intraday profile with peaks in mid-morning and mid-afternoon; it is 18% higher on Mondays and 12% lower on Fridays than midweek; it rises by 30% on campaign days, which are a fifth of all days and are announced in advance; and on top of all that it carries a shock common to the whole day, with a coefficient of variation of 10%, and a smaller one for each interval. Handle times are log-normal with a mean of five minutes and a coefficient of variation of one. Patience is a mixture: a fifth of callers hang up after an exponentially distributed twenty seconds on average, and the rest have log-normal patience with a median of 200 seconds, which puts the overall mean at 224 seconds. Six in ten callers who hang up call again, on average ten minutes later, and the platform counts the second attempt as a new offered call. The planners staff each half hour by the square-root rule on their forecast, revise the plan from the fourth interval onwards when the calls seen so far run above or below forecast (by at most five extra agents or three fewer), and lose about one agent in twenty to absence in any interval. Calls are answered first come, first served, and the whole thing is simulated call by call. On an ordinary day the centre takes about 3,600 calls, answers about 80% of them within twenty seconds and loses about 5% to abandonment.

None of those details reach the analyst. What reaches the analyst is the table the platform exports: one row per day and half hour, with the weekday, the interval, the campaign flag, the number of offered calls, the number of agents, the share of calls abandoned, the service level (the share answered within twenty seconds, the most common target in the industry), the mean time waited per offered call, and the mean handle time of the calls that were answered. Ninety days of it, 2,160 rows, is the training set for every generator below unless I say otherwise.

The two mechanistic generators are the two textbook models of a call centre. Erlang C, the formula telephone engineers have used for about a century, treats the centre as a queue with Poisson arrivals, exponential service and callers who never give up. Erlang A, the Palm/Erlang-A queue that Garnett, Mandelbaum and Reiman analysed in 2002, adds exponential patience with rate θ. Both are calibrated from the table alone. The service rate comes from the mean handle time. The patience rate comes from the number of abandoned calls divided by the total time callers spent waiting, which is the estimator Mandelbaum and Zeltyn give for Erlang A: if patience is exponential, the share of callers who abandon equals θ times the mean wait, and the ratio is the maximum-likelihood estimate of θ. To produce whole tables, both queues are handed a Poisson demand model fitted to the offered calls, multiplicative in interval, weekday and campaign, and the company's own staffing rule applied to the planners' own forecast. The rule and the forecast belong to the company, so giving them to the analyst is fair. What the analyst does not have is the day-level shock, the log-normal handle times, the patience mixture or the redials.

The four data-driven generators are written out in full rather than called from a library, so that what they do at the edge of the data is visible in the code. The Gaussian copula maps each column to normal scores through its empirical distribution, estimates the correlation matrix of the scores and inverts by interpolating the observed order statistics; the Synthetic Data Vault was built around one. The CART synthesizer follows synthpop: it draws the first column from its observed values, then takes the columns in order, grows a regression tree for each on the columns before it, and draws a value from the observed values in the leaf a synthetic row falls into, which is the approach Reiter proposed for partially synthetic public-use files in 2005. SMOTE, originally a way to oversample a minority class, is used here without classes: pick a row, pick one of its five nearest neighbours, and return a random point on the segment between them. The Gaussian mixture is fitted to the standardised columns with full covariance matrices and a number of components chosen by BIC. All but SMOTE can also be asked for the remaining columns given fixed values of some of them, which is how a joint synthesizer is asked a what-if question. Neural synthesizers such as CTGAN are left out. The properties tested below are about support and conditioning, and in those respects a neural density estimator behaves like the mixture: smooth, able to produce values beyond the observed range, and with nothing but the training data to say what those values should be.

## What each generator believes before it sees a row

It is tempting to say that a data-driven generator makes no assumptions and a mechanistic one makes many. The first half is wrong, and the way it is wrong matters for everything that follows.

Every data-driven generator has an inductive bias, and it is a bias that nobody chose for a reason connected with the problem. The Gaussian copula assumes that once each column has been squashed through its own marginal distribution, the dependence between columns is that of a multivariate normal: linear in the normal scores, symmetric, and with no extra dependence in the tails. The relationship between how loaded a half hour is and what share of calls are answered in twenty seconds is nothing like that. It is flat near one while agents have slack and then drops steeply once the load per agent approaches one, and a Gaussian copula can only blur that bend. Because its marginals are inverted by interpolating the observed order statistics, the copula also cannot produce a value outside the range of each column. CART cannot produce any value it has not seen: every synthetic cell is a donor from a leaf. SMOTE's rows lie on segments between real neighbours, so they stay inside the convex hull of each neighbourhood. The mixture is smooth and will extrapolate, but how it extrapolates is decided by whichever Gaussian components happen to sit at the edge of the data.

The mechanistic generators make their assumptions in a different place. Erlang A says that calls arrive as a Poisson process at a rate that depends on the calendar, that handle times are exponential, that patience is exponential, and that a caller who hangs up is gone. Every one of those statements is false in the world I built, and every one of them is written down. That is the real asymmetry. Both families are wrong, but the mechanistic model is wrong in sentences a domain expert can read, and the data-driven model is wrong in the geometry of an estimator.

## Fidelity where the data are

The first question is the one the synthetic-data literature usually asks: how hard is it to tell the generated rows from real ones? I use the propensity-score idea of Snoke and co-authors with a boosted classifier. For each generator, 960 synthetic rows (forty days' worth) are pooled with forty held-out real days, and a gradient-boosted classifier is trained by five-fold cross-validation to tell them apart. An out-of-fold ROC AUC of 0.5 means it cannot; 1.0 means every synthetic row is recognisable. Two independent samples of forty real days give an AUC of 0.540 (standard deviation 0.035 over six replicate histories), which is the floor at this sample size: a classifier with this much capacity finds some spurious separation even between real and real.

![Six generators against forty held-out days](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/mechanistic-vs-data-driven-generators/figures/fig1_fidelity.png)

*Figure 1. Service level against offered load per agent for forty held-out real days (top left, black) and for 960 rows from each generator trained on ninety days (colour, with the real rows in grey behind). Bottom right: discriminator AUC by length of training data, mean and standard deviation over six replicate histories; the black line is two independent real samples.*

The scatter plots show the two failure styles at a glance. Erlang C is badly wrong. With nobody hanging up, a half hour in which the load per agent exceeds one builds a queue that only clears after the peak, so its service levels collapse to zero in the region where real ones sit between 0.2 and 0.6, and the classifier finds it with an AUC of 0.975. Erlang A, calibrated on the same ninety days, reproduces the shape of the real cloud closely. The data-driven generators reproduce the cloud as well, but each smears it in its own way: the copula and the mixture scatter points into regions where no real half hour falls, SMOTE fills in between real points, and CART draws real values into combinations that never occurred together.

The bottom-right panel is the more instructive one. With ninety days of training data, Erlang A's rows are the hardest to tell from real (AUC 0.614), ahead of CART (0.627), SMOTE (0.699), the mixture (0.763) and the copula (0.767). Erlang A's figure does not move as the training data grow: 0.594 at thirty days and 0.595 at 810. That plateau is the model error. Its pooled service level after ninety days of training is 0.838 against 0.806 in the held-out days, and its abandonment share is 4.0% against 5.0%, and no amount of extra data moves those numbers, because they come from what the model leaves out. The flexible data-driven generators keep improving instead. CART reaches 0.554 at 270 days and 0.548 at 810, essentially the real-against-real floor, and SMOTE reaches 0.582 at 810. Somewhere between 90 and 270 days of history, CART overtakes the mechanistic model on this measure.

The two smoothest data-driven generators do not get there. The copula is at 0.788 with thirty days and still at 0.758 with 810; a Gaussian dependence structure cannot bend the way a queue bends, and more data only estimate the wrong correlation matrix more precisely. That is a model error in exactly the sense that Erlang A's plateau is, and it is a useful reminder that "data-driven" does not mean "assumption-free". The mixture improves more slowly, from 0.770 to 0.709; BIC chose the largest number of components on offer, 24, from 270 days onwards, so part of its plateau is the cap I put on it.

One caution on reading these numbers. The discriminator's power depends on how many rows it sees. A bias of three percentage points in the service level, which is what Erlang A carries, is hard to see in 960 rows and easier to see in more. Fidelity scores are statements about a model and an evaluation sample together.

## Outside the range of the data

The second question is the one mechanistic models are usually built for. Suppose fresh demand runs some fraction above normal for a stretch of days, without the planners being told, as it would after an outage elsewhere or an unexpected price change. What happens to the service level?

The question has to be put to each generator in a form it can understand, and that is already a trade-off. A mechanistic generator takes it literally: multiply the demand model by m, keep the staffing rule, simulate. A data-driven generator has no column for fresh demand. The best it can be given is the calendar and a number of offered calls equal to m times the training mean for that calendar cell, from which it draws staffing and outcomes. In the world of the simulation those are different questions, because callers who give up call again and their second attempts are counted as offered calls. At m = 1.5 the synthesizers are handed 5,448 offered calls a day, while the real centre counts 6,472. A generator that cannot represent fresh demand cannot be asked about it without someone first translating the question, and the translation needs a model.

![Demand surge](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/mechanistic-vs-data-driven-generators/figures/fig2_surge.png)

*Figure 2. Left: pooled service level when fresh demand is m times normal, for reality (black) and for each generator, averaged over five replicate histories and sixty days per point. The shaded band holds 90% of the training days' demand relative to the calendar mean. Right: Erlang A with redials added at probability p, with the demand model deflated so that offered calls still match the training data.*

In the training data, day-level demand relative to the calendar mean ranged from 0.74 to 1.31, and 90% of days fell between 0.83 and 1.17. Inside that band every generator except Erlang C is within a few points of reality: at m = 1.0 reality's service level is 0.806 and the others give 0.807 (copula), 0.817 (mixture), 0.829 (CART) and 0.832 (Erlang A). Outside it the picture separates.

The data-driven generators flatten. At m = 1.5, where the real service level is 0.186, the copula says 0.683 and the mixture 0.613; CART, with 0.486, does better than the two smooth models because its leaves at the high end of offered calls return real half hours from the busiest training days, some of which went badly; it still cannot go further than the worst of them. None of them can say anything about a load it has not seen, and how the copula fails is written into its code: an offered-calls value beyond the largest one observed is mapped to the normal score of the largest one observed, so every surge beyond the edge looks like the edge. Their abandonment shares tell the same story, between 8.7% and 14.3% at m = 1.5 against a real 30.8%.

Erlang C fails in the opposite direction. Without abandonment it predicts a service level of 0.047 at m = 1.3 and 0.010 at m = 1.5, when the real centre still answers 47% and 19% of calls in time. A queue in which nobody leaves is a queue that never sheds load.

Erlang A has the right shape and the wrong level. At m = 1.3 it predicts 0.604 against a real 0.470; at m = 1.5, 0.370 against 0.186. The direction of its error is the direction you would expect from what it leaves out: a model without redials underestimates load exactly when abandonment rises, and a model without the day-level shock averages over good and bad days in the wrong order, because the service level is a concave function of load near saturation and the average of a concave function is below the function of the average.

## A mechanism the past cannot see

The right-hand panel of Figure 2 adds redials to Erlang A with a probability p that the analyst does not know, and deflates the fitted demand so that the offered calls still match the table. Read the left edge first. In the observed regime the four versions are almost indistinguishable: their in-sample service levels are 0.828, 0.826, 0.816 and 0.801 for p = 0, 0.3, 0.6 and 0.9, their discriminator AUCs against held-out data are 0.586, 0.576, 0.575 and 0.578, and the classifier cannot rank them. Then read the right edge. At m = 1.5 the same four models predict service levels of 0.367, 0.299, 0.186 and 0.052. The version with the true probability, 0.6, lands on reality to three decimals there.

This is the identifiability side of extrapolation, and it is the part that the phrase "mechanistic models extrapolate" tends to hide. With abandonment at 5%, redials add about 3% to the offered calls, and that is absorbed by the demand model without leaving a trace in the table. The data cannot tell you whether redials exist. Under a surge, abandonment rises to 30% and redials become the main thing that is happening. A mechanistic model extrapolates only as well as the parts of it that the observed regime could not test, and those parts are either supplied from outside the data (call-level records that match caller identities, the operations literature, the platform's own logs), or they are guessed. Aguir and co-authors studied how retrials change call-centre performance in 2004; the table alone would never have told me to look.

To see which of the analyst's assumptions carries the error, I switched the true components into Erlang A one at a time and in combination, and ran each version under the m = 1.3 surge. The plain Erlang A overstates the service level by 0.131. Replacing exponential handle times with the true log-normal ones changes that to 0.135, and replacing exponential patience with the true mixture changes it to 0.134: neither matters. Adding the day-level and interval-level demand shocks brings the error to 0.101. Adding redials alone brings it to 0.036, and adding redials and the demand shocks together brings it to −0.005. In the observed regime the same exercise moves the error between +0.026 and −0.017, and no single switch dominates.

The patience result deserves a second look, because exponential patience is the assumption of Erlang A that has been most visibly refuted by data. Brown and co-authors' 2005 statistical analysis of a bank's call centre found that patience was clearly not exponential, and also that Erlang A's predictions were, in their words, surprisingly accurate. The same happens here. A wrong assumption that the calibration can compensate for is cheap; a missing mechanism that the calibration cannot see is expensive. Which assumptions are which is not something the model can tell you about itself.

## Asking what-if

The third question is an intervention. Management is considering adding k agents to every half hour on top of the current staffing rule, or cutting them. What happens to the service level?

The true effect is easy to get in a simulation and impossible to get in the real world: replay the same callers, with the same arrival times, handle times and patience, through the staffing rule with k more agents, and take the difference. Mechanistic generators answer the same way, by replaying each evaluation day's offered calls with the day's staffing plus k. A joint data-driven generator can only be asked to condition, and there are two natural ways to ask. The careful way fixes the calendar, the offered calls and the agents plus k, and draws the outcomes. The naive way fixes the calendar and the agents plus k and lets the synthesizer fill in everything else, including the offered calls, as it would for any row.

![Staffing intervention](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/mechanistic-vs-data-driven-generators/figures/fig3_intervention.png)

*Figure 3. Change in pooled service level when k agents are added to every half hour, relative to the same method's answer at k = 0. Reality (black) replays the same callers. Left: mechanistic generators and synthesizers conditioned on offered calls and agents. Right: synthesizers conditioned on agents only. Five replicate histories, forty evaluation days each.*

The naive answers from the copula and the mixture are close to zero. Cutting six agents from every half hour lowers the real service level by 0.399; the naive copula says 0.012 and the naive mixture 0.023. This is Pearl's distinction between seeing and doing, produced by nothing more exotic than the company's own staffing rule. In the data, half hours with more agents are half hours with more calls, because the planners staff to their forecast and add agents during the day when volume runs high. Conditioning on more agents therefore means conditioning on more calls, and the two cancel.

The naive CART answer is not close to zero. It says −0.241 for six agents fewer and +0.147 for six more, close to its careful answer. The reason is the column order. synthpop's default visit sequence follows the order of the columns in the data, and in this table offered calls come before agents. When agents are fixed, the tree that draws offered calls never looks at them, so the offered calls are drawn as if staffing had been set from outside, which is what an intervention does. The synthesizer behaved causally because a column happened to sit to the left of another. Had agents come first, fixing them would have changed the offered calls drawn after them, as it does in the copula. A sequential synthesizer's visit order is a causal ordering that nobody chose as one.

The careful answers are better. The copula, conditioned on offered calls and agents, gives −0.308 for six agents fewer and +0.137 for six more; CART −0.261 and +0.137; the mixture −0.264 and +0.135. Erlang A gives −0.318 and +0.142. Reality is −0.399 and +0.158. Every method underestimates the effect, and they underestimate it most for cuts, which push the centre into staffing levels the data rarely show and which the data-driven generators flatten. Erlang A has no support problem, and yet it underestimates by about as much as the copula, for the reason the surge already exposed: cut agents, more callers give up, more of them call back, and the offered calls rise. Holding the offered calls at their observed values, as every method here does, blocks that path. In this world the offered calls are partly a consequence of staffing, so conditioning on them is conditioning on a mediator, and the data-driven generators have no way to know it. The mechanistic generator would know it if the analyst had put redials in.

There is a second-order version of this problem that the experiment does not measure but the design makes plain. The intervention here keeps the staffing rule and adds agents on top. If management changed the rule itself, say by stopping intraday revisions, the joint distribution of agents and offered calls in future data would change, and a synthesizer trained on data generated under the old rule would keep reproducing the old rule's correlations. This is Lucas's critique of econometric policy evaluation from 1976, and its older form is Haavelmo's 1944 notion of autonomy: only relations that stay put when other parts of the system change can be used to predict the effect of changing them. In the mechanistic generator the staffing rule is a separate, replaceable component. In the copula it is dissolved into a correlation matrix.

## Two kinds of error, two kinds of budget

The fourth comparison is about where error comes from and what reduces it. The quantity is the long-run service level of the centre in its usual regime, whose true value, from 4,000 simulated days, is 0.8015.

![Error budget](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/mechanistic-vs-data-driven-generators/figures/fig4_error_budget.png)

*Figure 4. Root-mean-square error of the long-run service level over sixteen replicate histories. Left: ninety real days, growing synthetic sample. Right: growing real data, with a large synthetic sample (300 simulated days for Erlang A, 500 days' worth of rows for the synthesizers); black is the plain average of the real days.*

The left panel holds the real data at ninety days and increases the synthetic sample. Every generator starts noisy: with one synthetic day the errors are between 0.037 and 0.075. Erlang A stops improving after about 25 simulated days, at an RMSE of 0.027 that is almost all bias (+0.026). That is the picture from the opening paragraph, measured: simulation shrinks Monte Carlo error and leaves model error alone. The data-driven generators keep improving to between 0.009 and 0.012 at 500 synthetic days, where they too stop, because their remaining error is the estimation error of ninety real days.

The right panel holds the synthetic sample large and increases the real data. The plain average of the real days, with no generator at all, has an RMSE of 0.027 with ten days, 0.011 with ninety and 0.003 with 810. The data-driven generators track it closely and do not beat it by any margin that matters: at 810 days their errors are between 0.002 and 0.005. Erlang A goes the other way. Its error is 0.039 with ten days and 0.030 with 810, and its bias grows from +0.009 to +0.030: more data pin the calibration down, and what they pin it to is the model's own best fit, not reality.

Two conclusions follow, and they cut in different directions. For a quantity that lives inside the observed regime, a data-driven synthesizer adds no information: the best it can do is reproduce the error of the data it was fitted on, plus a Monte Carlo error that the analyst must pay to remove. If the question is the average of something the data already cover, the data answer it directly. And a mechanistic generator's error has a floor that real data do not lower. In this world Erlang A never beats the plain average, not even with ten days of data, because its bias is larger than the sampling error of ten days. In a world where data are scarcer, noisier or more expensive, the comparison could go the other way; the point is that the two errors respond to different budgets, and only one of them responds to buying more computer time.

## A parameter that has no true value

The fifth comparison concerns calibration itself. Erlang A has one parameter the data have to supply, the patience rate θ, and reality's patience is not exponential, so θ has no true value. Under misspecification an estimator still converges. White showed in 1982 that maximum likelihood converges to the parameter of the model that comes closest to the truth in the Kullback–Leibler sense, usually called the pseudo-true value. Other estimators measure closeness differently, so they converge to different places.

![Calibration](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/mechanistic-vs-data-driven-generators/figures/fig5_calibration.png)

*Figure 5. Mean patience implied by three estimators of Erlang A's patience rate, as 1/θ, by days of calibration data: mean and standard deviation over twelve replicate histories. Hollow markers: each estimator on 1,000 days. Black line: reality's mean patience.*

The three estimators are the ratio of abandonments to total waiting time, a simulated-method-of-moments estimator that picks the θ whose simulated abandonment share matches the observed one, and a second simulated-method-of-moments estimator that matches the mean waiting time per call instead. Both moment estimators replay the observed days, with their offered calls and staffing, three times per evaluation. All three are consistent for θ if patience is exponential. Here, on 1,000 days, they settle at mean patiences of 189 seconds (ratio), 178 seconds (abandonment share) and 193 seconds (waiting time), against reality's mean patience of 224 seconds. Their precision differs as much as their targets. With 270 days of data the ratio estimator's mean patience varies by about 1.3 seconds across histories, the waiting-time estimator's by about 2 seconds and the abandonment-share estimator's by about 5; with ten days the abandonment-share estimator varies by almost 40 seconds. The ratio uses the waiting times directly, and under exponential patience it is the maximum-likelihood estimator, which is why it is so much tighter.

The spread shrinks and the estimates keep missing 224. That is what Kennedy and O'Hagan's 2001 framework for calibrating computer models was built to handle: it writes the observations as the simulator's output at the best parameter plus a discrepancy function plus noise, and treats the discrepancy as something to be estimated rather than ignored. Brynjarsdóttir and O'Hagan showed in 2014 that the price is identifiability: the calibration parameter and the discrepancy are confounded, and without prior information about the discrepancy the data cannot separate them. Ignoring the discrepancy instead gives estimates that are biased and confidently so. The simulation-based inference literature has reached the same place in its own vocabulary. Frazier, Robert and Rousseau showed in 2020 how approximate Bayesian computation behaves when the model cannot reproduce the observed summaries, and Cannon, Ward and Schmon found in 2022 that neural posterior estimators become more overconfident the more misspecified the simulator is.

There is a fourth θ worth computing. Matching the abandonment share on 120 days of a demand surge at m = 1.3 gives a mean patience of 156 seconds. The parameter that makes the model right in the regime you care about is not the one the normal regime gives you, and it is not reality's mean patience either. And it does not rescue the forecast. Run as a full generator under that surge, Erlang A predicts service levels of 0.601, 0.615 and 0.602 with the three long-run calibrations, 0.585 with reality's mean patience, and 0.629 with the surge-calibrated value, against a real 0.422 on those 120 days. The surge-calibrated θ reproduces the surge's abandonment share only when the model is handed the surge's real offered calls and staffing. Run as a generator that has to produce its own offered calls, it predicts 10.7% abandonment against a real 17.8%, because the mechanism that inflates the offered calls is missing and no value of θ can supply it. A calibrated parameter is a property of the model, the data and the criterion together, and it is honest to report it that way.

## What the rows give away

The sixth trade-off is disclosure. Synthetic data are often generated so that a table can be shared without sharing its rows, and Stadler, Oprisanu and Troncoso's 2022 analysis, titled "Synthetic Data – Anonymisation Groundhog Day", is a reminder that generators trained on records can leak them. A simple check, close to the holdout-based assessment of Platzer and Reutterer, compares how close synthetic rows come to the training rows with how close real held-out rows come to them. Real rows that were never in training set the yardstick: by construction, 5% of them are nearer to a training row than their own fifth percentile.

SMOTE's rows are the closest by far: 73.8% of them sit nearer to a training row than that fifth percentile, and their median distance to the nearest training row is 30% of the held-out median. That is what interpolating between two neighbours does when the neighbours are close. CART comes next, with 11.7% of rows inside the fifth percentile and 0.3% exact copies of a training row, because each of its cells is a donor and a row whose cells all come from the same donor is that donor. The copula and the mixture sit at 2.0% and 2.4%, further from the training rows than real data are, which is the other face of their poorer fidelity. Erlang A is at 4.9%, indistinguishable from the 5% of fresh real data, and no row is a copy.

The mechanistic generator's advantage here is structural rather than measured. It never sees a row. What it takes from the data is a handful of aggregates: a demand profile, a mean handle time, an abandonment ratio. That is not a formal privacy guarantee, and with a small population even aggregates can disclose something, but it is a different kind of object from a generator that stores donors or interpolates between neighbours. In the interval table of a contact centre nothing is personal; in a patient register or a payroll, the same numbers would decide whether the synthetic file can leave the building.

## What each one costs to build, run and keep

Cost has three parts, and they point in different directions.

Building is where the mechanistic generator is expensive. The queue in this experiment, with redials, shifts and an intraday staffing rule, is a few hundred lines of carefully checked code, and writing it required knowing how contact centres work. Each data-driven generator is fitted in one call: under a tenth of a second for the copula, CART and SMOTE, and about two and a half seconds for the mixture with its search over component counts.

Running is where the data-driven generators are cheap. On the machine that produced the results, a thousand rows took between 0.006 and 0.026 seconds from the data-driven generators and about 0.19 seconds from the two Erlang generators, which simulate every call. That is an order of magnitude, and in this example it is irrelevant. It stops being irrelevant when a simulator takes minutes per run, as agent-based markets, epidemic models with spatial structure and physics engines often do; most of the simulation-based inference literature exists because simulations are expensive enough that the number of runs has to be rationed.

Keeping is less obvious. When the data drift, a data-driven generator is refitted and nothing else needs to change. A mechanistic generator has to be re-examined: if callers start using a call-back option, or the planners change their rule, the model must be told, because no refit of its parameters will discover a mechanism it does not contain. On the other side of the ledger, validating a mechanistic model has an established practice, set out for example in Sargent's 2013 survey of verification and validation of simulation models, and the components of a mechanistic model can be checked one at a time against domain knowledge. A data-driven generator can only be validated as a whole, against held-out data from the same regime, which is the one place it was never in doubt.

## Deciding under a model you know is wrong

The last comparison is a decision. A product launch is expected to raise fresh demand by a factor somewhere between 1.1 and 1.3 for a run of days; the planners' forecast does not include it. Management will add k agents to every half hour on launch days and wants the day's service level to reach 70% on at least 80% of those days. Each method picks the smallest k that meets the requirement in its own synthetic launch days, and the choice is then scored in reality over 300 launch days.

Four methods are compared. The point model is the calibrated Erlang A with the surge set to the midpoint, 1.2. The domain-randomised model, in the spirit of Tobin and co-authors' 2017 work on transferring neural networks trained in simulation to real robots, draws a fresh value of everything the analyst is unsure about for every synthetic day: the surge between 1.1 and 1.3, the patience rate between half and twice the calibrated value, a service-time coefficient of variation between 0.5 and 1.5, a redial probability between 0 and 0.9 with a delay between five and twenty minutes, and a day-level demand shock with a coefficient of variation up to 20%. The ranges are wide and they contain the truth. The robust model uses the same ranges differently: each draw is a whole scenario simulated over a hundred days, and the requirement has to hold in 90% of twenty scenarios rather than on the pooled days. The copula, finally, is given offered calls equal to the surge times the training mean, draws staffing given those calls, adds k, and draws outcomes.

![Staffing decision](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/mechanistic-vs-data-driven-generators/figures/fig6_decision.png)

*Figure 6. Left: share of launch days meeting the 70% target, by agents added, as each method expects it and as it happens in reality (black). Right: the k each method chooses, placed on reality's curve.*

In reality the smallest k that works is 4, which meets the target on 84% of launch days at a cost of 48 extra agent-hours a day. The point model chooses k = 1 and meets the target on 55% of days in reality. The copula chooses k = 0, because its synthetic launch days already pass at 80%, and in reality only 41% of them do; its days are assembled from rows drawn independently, so the day-to-day variation that sinks real days is averaged away, and its outcomes are flattened beyond the edge of the data as before. The domain-randomised model chooses k = 3 and meets the target on 76% of days: close, and still short. The robust model chooses k = 6, meets the target on 90% of days, and pays 72 extra agent-hours a day for it, half again as much as necessary.

The randomised model's near miss is worth understanding, because domain randomisation is the standard answer to the reality gap in robotics. It did what it promises. Its ranges covered reality, and its curve lies much closer to reality's than the point model's does. But a requirement checked on days pooled across all those parameter draws is a requirement on the average world in the randomisation distribution, not on the particular world you are in. When the requirement is a probability that has to be met in the actual world, averaging over worlds is not enough, and the robust version, which asks the requirement to hold across most scenarios, buys the guarantee with spare capacity. Between them they describe a trade-off rather than a solution: wider uncertainty bought from outside the data protects against the reality gap only at a price, and the price is set by how wide the uncertainty had to be.

## Hybrids, and what they trade

The two families are ends of a range, and much current practice sits between them. Each hybrid buys something from both sides, and each keeps some of both sides' costs.

Plasmode and semi-synthetic simulation keep the real covariates and simulate the outcome. Franklin and co-authors used the idea in 2014 to evaluate pharmacoepidemiologic methods on real healthcare databases, and the causal inference competition analysed by Dorie and co-authors built its datasets on real covariates with simulated treatment assignment and outcomes. The covariates keep all their real awkwardness, and the outcome mechanism is known, so methods can be scored. The cost is that the scores depend on the simulated mechanism, which the benchmark designer chose. RealCause, from Neal, Huang and Raghupathi, pushes the other way: it fits flexible generative models for treatment and outcome to real data and samples interventional distributions from them, so the ground truth is realistic, but it is the ground truth of a fitted model, with the support and conditioning properties of any fitted model.

Kennedy and O'Hagan's discrepancy term is a hybrid in the statistical sense: a mechanistic simulator plus a flexible, data-driven correction. It recovers fidelity in the regime where the correction is trained and, by the result of Brynjarsdóttir and O'Hagan, gives up the interpretation of the physical parameters unless the analyst has prior information about the discrepancy. Universal differential equations, in the formulation of Rackauckas and co-authors, build the same idea into the model: a known differential equation with a neural network standing in for the part of the dynamics nobody can write down. The learned part extrapolates like a data-driven model, and the known part like a mechanistic one.

Tabular foundation models are a hybrid in the opposite direction. TabPFN, in the version Hollmann and co-authors published in Nature in 2025, is a data-driven model pretrained on millions of synthetic datasets drawn from a prior built on random structural causal models: a mechanistic generator of tables, used to teach a neural network what tables tend to look like. Its strengths and its blind spots are those of that prior.

## Reading the trade-offs as questions

Put together, the measurements suggest that the useful question is not which family is better but what the generated data will be used for, and that different uses expose different trade-offs.

If the synthetic data stand in for real data inside the regime the data cover, to share a table, to test a pipeline or to train a model that will be used under the same conditions, a flexible data-driven generator has the advantage that its error keeps falling as data accumulate, down to the limit its own assumptions set, which for CART and SMOTE was close to the real-against-real floor. With enough history, CART's rows here were as hard to tell from real ones as real ones are from each other. The costs are disclosure, which depends heavily on the method (SMOTE's rows sat on top of the training rows), and the fact that the synthetic data carry no information the real data did not already contain.

If the question is about a regime the data never covered, a data-driven generator has nothing to say and says it confidently, by returning the edge of the data. A mechanistic generator can answer, and its answer is only as good as the parts of it that the observed regime could not test. In this world, the assumption most often criticised, exponential patience, did not matter, and the one nobody could see in the table, redials, decided everything.

If the question is about an intervention, conditioning in a joint synthesizer gives the right answer only by accident of column order or by a careful choice of what to condition on, and the careful choice can still be wrong when a variable that looks like a confounder is partly a consequence of the intervention. A mechanistic generator represents the intervention directly and keeps the decision rule as a component that can be replaced.

If the generator exists to score methods against a known truth, which is how the whole of this article used one, the mechanistic family is the only one that can do it, because a data-driven generator's "truth" is its own estimate. The conclusions are then conclusions about the simulated world, and the distance from that world to the real one is not something the simulation can measure.

And if the generator feeds a decision, the model's error has to be carried into the decision, either by randomising what the analyst is unsure about or by asking the decision to hold across scenarios, and the second costs more than the first because it promises more.

## What this experiment does not show

The referee is a simulator I wrote. It is richer than every generator fitted to it, and none of its parameters were available to the analyst, but the conclusions are about this world. In particular, the fact that Erlang A's rows were the hardest to tell from real ones at ninety days reflects a domain in which a simple queueing model happens to be close to right; in a domain without a good mechanistic model, the comparison would start from a much worse mechanistic baseline. The data-driven generators are the classical ones. Neural synthesizers might fit the observed regime better; that they share the properties tested here, support and conditioning, is a statement about how they are built, not a measurement. The experiment uses one interval table with nine columns; wider tables with many categorical columns are where data-driven synthesizers are usually deployed and where their fidelity is hardest to achieve. And the privacy check is a distance heuristic, not an attack.

Two conjectures are worth stating as conjectures. The first is that the pattern in Figure 1, a mechanistic generator ahead at small samples and a flexible data-driven one ahead at large samples, with the crossover set by how close the mechanism is to right, is general; the experiment shows it in one world. The second is that, for questions outside the observed regime, the error of a mechanistic generator is dominated by mechanisms that are dormant in the observed regime, rather than by misspecified forms of mechanisms that are active in it. The redials result is one instance. Whether it holds more widely is an empirical question about fields, not a theorem.

## Reproducing the results

The code is in [github.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/tree/main/mechanistic-vs-data-driven-generators](https://github.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/tree/main/mechanistic-vs-data-driven-generators). The queue simulator is compiled with numba and checked in the tests against hand-worked cases and the Erlang C waiting-time formula. `experiments/run_all.py` runs the seven experiments and writes `results/summary.txt`, which contains every number quoted here; it takes about forty minutes on two cores, and `--quick` runs everything at small sizes in a couple of minutes. All randomness flows from one master seed.
