# Do Callers Who Hang Up Call Back? Testing an Interval-Level Redial Estimator Against the Caller IDs of a Real Call Centre

*The 1999 "Anonymous Bank" call-centre records, an estimator that worked in simulation, and the confounder the simulation did not contain.*

In the two previous articles of this series I built a simulated contact centre, gave it callers who hang up and call again, and then tried to recover that behaviour from the kind of table a contact-centre platform exports: offered calls, abandoned calls and agents for every half hour. The first article concluded that a queueing model fitted to that table could not tell whether redials existed. The second corrected that conclusion. A fit could not see the redials, but a statistic designed for them could: if callers who hang up call again a few minutes later, a half hour with many abandonments should be followed by a half hour with more offered calls than the calendar predicts. The within-day slope of surplus calls on the previous half hour's abandonments, matched against the same slope computed on simulated days, recovered the true redial probability of 0.6 to within a few hundredths, and it fell to zero in a simulated world where nobody redialled.

That was a test of the estimator in the world it was designed for. I wrote the simulator, I chose which mechanisms it had, and I designed the statistic knowing what the simulator contained. The placebo world without redials differed from the main world in exactly one respect, the one the statistic was built to detect. A real call centre is under no such obligation.

This article takes the estimator to real data. The data are the call-by-call records of the call centre of an Israeli bank for the whole of 1999, collected by Ilan Guedj and Avi Mandelbaum and published by the Technion's SEE Center as the "Anonymous Bank" database. They are well known in queueing research: Brown and co-authors used them in 2005 for what became a standard statistical portrait of a call centre. Two features make them suited to this test. They can be aggregated into exactly the half-hour table the estimator expects, so the estimator can be run as an analyst with only that table would run it. And many records carry a customer ID, so for those callers the thing the estimator is trying to infer can be measured directly: when someone hangs up, does the same ID call again, and how soon?

The short version of what follows. The estimator from the previous article, run on the real table, says that about two thirds of the callers who hang up call again. The caller IDs say the extra calling caused by hanging up is somewhere between 0.14 and 0.20. Most of the slope the estimator reads as redials is something else, and the something else can be seen without any IDs, by running the same slope backwards in time. Demand in this centre arrives in busy spells that last longer than a half hour, and busy spells produce both abandonments and surplus calls in neighbouring intervals without any caller calling twice. Two repairs that account for those spells bring the estimate down to between 0.15 and 0.28, consistent with the IDs, though with intervals wide enough that the table alone cannot rule out the absence of redials.

The code, the half-hourly table built from the records, the results and the figures are in the folder [anonymous-bank-redials](https://github.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/tree/main/anonymous-bank-redials) of my Medium-Blog repository. Every number below is printed by one script into `results/summary.txt`. The raw records are not stored there; a script downloads them from the SEE Center and checks the archive's checksum.

## The records

The database holds twelve tab-separated monthly files with one row per call that asked to speak to an agent. Brown and co-authors report that about 1.2 million calls reached the centre in 1999, of which about 750,000 ended in the automated voice-response unit and about 450,000 asked for an agent; the files contain 444,448 of the latter. Twelve rows have a clock time where a number belongs and are dropped, which leaves 444,436 calls. Each row gives the time the call entered and left the voice-response unit, the time it joined and left the queue, the time service started and ended, the agent who served it, the call type, the customer's priority, the outcome, and a customer ID. The outcome is AGENT for 353,143 calls (served), HANG for 87,694 (the caller hung up) and PHANTOM for 3,599, which the documentation describes as virtual calls to be ignored, adding that the collectors themselves are not sure what they are.

The Israeli working week runs from Sunday to Thursday, with short Fridays and quiet Saturdays, and the centre's volume follows it: between 77,836 and 86,911 calls on each of the five working weekdays across the year, 28,548 on Fridays and 10,734 on Saturdays. The analysis uses Sunday to Thursday from 07:00 to midnight, 34 half hours a day. Of the 258 working days with calls, eleven have fewer than half the median day's volume (holidays and partial days) and are dropped, leaving 247 days. A call counts as offered to the agents if it waited in the queue or reached an agent, and it is counted in the half hour in which it left the voice-response unit. Over the 247 days that gives 384,155 offered calls, 1,555 per day and 45.7 per half hour on average, of which 60,045 were abandoned: 15.6%, three times the abandonment of the simulated centre in the earlier articles.

The day has the shape familiar from call centres everywhere. Volume rises from 17 offered calls in the first half hour to a peak of 72 between 10:00 and 10:30, stays between 54 and 68 from then until 17:00, and falls through the evening to 17 in the last half hour. Abandonments follow with sharper peaks, 14 to 15 calls per half hour between 09:30 and 10:30 and again at 15:00, against one or two at the edges of the day. Only 44% of offered calls were answered within twenty seconds, and an offered call waited 65 seconds on average.

Two quantities the earlier simulations took as given have to be built from the records here. The first is the number of agents. The records name the agent who served each call but not who was logged in, so the table counts the distinct agents whose service overlapped each half hour. That is a lower bound on the agents on shift: an agent who took no call in a half hour does not appear. It averages 7.9 per half hour and rises from about four at the edges of the day to about ten at midday. The second is the handle time. The recorded service time averages 192 seconds, but an agent who finishes a call is not necessarily free for the next one; after-call work, breaks and time logged in but not taking calls are all invisible in the records. The simulator described below needs an effective handle time, and I set it by asking how long it must be for the simulator, with the real days' agent counts replayed, to abandon as many calls as the data. The answer is 1.54 times the recorded mean, 294 seconds. This is a calibration, not a measurement, and it absorbs whatever the agent count misses as well as the after-call work.

## What the table sees and what the IDs see

The half-hourly table built from these records has, for each of the 247 days and 34 intervals, the offered calls, the abandoned calls, the answered calls and those answered within twenty seconds, the total waiting time, the total service time and the agent count. That is the analyst's data in this article, and the estimators below use nothing else. The same table is committed to the repository, so everything except the ID analysis can be reproduced without the raw records.

The customer IDs are the check. A customer ID is present on 47% of all calls, which sounds like too little to work with until one reads the documentation. The ID is zero for callers the system did not identify, prospective customers among them, and, because of what the documentation calls a system bug, it was recorded only for calls that waited in the queue. Among offered calls that queued, 77.5% carry an ID; among offered calls answered at once, 3.6% do. Since an abandoned call has by definition waited, 58% of abandoned calls carry an ID.

The queueing condition matters more than the coverage, because it decides which callbacks can be seen. A caller who hangs up and calls again is visible as a redial only if the second call also waits in the queue. Overall, 62.6% of offered calls queued, but the share varies enormously with the load: across half hours, its quartiles are 0.28, 0.55 and 0.81. A callback placed during a quiet spell is likely to be answered at once and vanish from the ID record. This turns out to bias the direct measurement less than one might fear, for reasons given in the next section, but it is the first thing to know about these IDs.

The second thing is that an ID is not a person. The 12,903 distinct IDs place a median of five calls each in the year, and 90% of them place 28 or fewer, but 146 IDs place more than 200 calls, and between them they account for a third of all calls that carry an ID. These IDs behave like shared lines, a business or an office calling on behalf of many people: their calls overlap, with the next call entering the system before the current one has ended. For the IDs with more than 200 calls, 81% of queued calls that were served are overlapped by the next call in this way. Treating such an ID as one caller would count a colleague's unrelated call as a redial. The clean comparisons below use IDs with at most 50 calls in the year, and IDs with at most 10 as a stricter check.

## Counting callbacks from the IDs

The direct measurement compares two groups of queued calls from the same kind of ID: calls that were abandoned and calls that were served. For each call, the question is whether the same ID entered the system again within a window after the call ended, where the end of an abandoned call is the moment the caller hung up and the end of a served call is the end of service. The served group is the baseline. People call their bank again for many reasons, a follow-up question, a second account, an instruction they forgot, and those reasons have nothing to do with hanging up. What hanging up adds is the difference between the groups.

![Calling again after hanging up, measured from customer IDs](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/anonymous-bank-redials/figures/fig1_caller_ids.png)

*Figure 1. Left: the share of queued calls followed by another call from the same customer ID within a window after the call ended, for abandoned calls (blue) and served calls (orange), IDs with at most 50 calls in the year. Middle: the difference between the two, for IDs with at most 10 and at most 50 calls; dotted lines weight each visible callback by one over the share of offered calls that queued in its half hour, to correct for callbacks the IDs cannot see. Right: the time from hanging up to the next call, for abandoned calls followed by another call within three hours.*

For IDs with at most 50 calls, the records hold 22,458 abandoned and 71,708 served queued calls. Within ten minutes of hanging up, 29.2% of abandoned calls are followed by another call from the same ID; within ten minutes of the end of service, 17.7% of served calls are. Within an hour the shares are 66.5% and 50.0%, and within three hours 83.1% and 69.5%. The excess of the abandoned group is 0.115 at ten minutes, rises to a maximum of 0.167 at 45 minutes, stays at 0.166 at an hour, and falls slowly after that, to 0.136 at three hours. The fall is not callers changing their minds. As the window lengthens, the served group catches up through calls that would have happened anyway, and the excess is a difference between two curves that both keep rising.

The baseline is only a baseline if the two groups are alike in how often they call for reasons of their own. One check is the window before the call rather than after it. Hanging up cannot affect calls that came before, so if the groups are comparable, the share with a previous call from the same ID in the hour before should be the same in both. It is: 58.6% for abandoned calls and 59.0% for served calls. At ten minutes the shares are 22.4% and 21.6%, and at three hours 78.1% and 79.3%. The groups call equally often before; they differ only after.

The stricter group, IDs with at most ten calls in the year, gives the same picture with smaller levels: 7,271 abandoned and 22,709 served calls, an excess of 0.093 at ten minutes, 0.144 at thirty, 0.171 at an hour and 0.158 at three hours, with the prior-window shares again equal (29.8% and 30.6% at an hour). The heavy IDs give larger numbers, 0.284 at an hour for IDs with 51 to 200 calls and 0.351 for those above 200, but these are the overlapping lines, and their baseline is depressed by the overlap itself: when the next call from a shared line usually starts before the current service ends, the served group's "next call after the end" is often not counted. Pooled over all IDs the excess reaches 0.28 at an hour, and that number says more about shared lines than about callers.

Then there is the visibility problem. A callback appears in the record only if it queued, and the chance of queueing is higher when the centre is busy. Abandonments happen when the centre is busy, and busy spells last, so a callback twenty minutes after hanging up meets a busier centre than a callback twenty minutes after a service. Taking each call's own half hour and looking twenty minutes past its end, the share of offered calls that queued is 0.81 for abandoned calls and 0.69 for served calls. Callbacks after hanging up are easier to see.

The correction weights each visible callback by one over the share of offered calls that queued in its half hour, which recovers the callbacks one would have seen had every call carried its ID, under the assumption that within a half hour identified customers queue as often as everyone else. The weighting counts only the first visible callback, so it overstates the share once callers place several calls inside the window, and I use it for windows of an hour or less. It raises both groups, the abandoned group by a factor of about 1/0.78 and the served group by about 1/0.75 at the visible callbacks' own half hours, and the net effect on the excess is upward, not downward. For IDs with at most 50 calls the corrected excess is 0.142 at ten minutes, 0.192 at thirty and 0.190 at an hour; for IDs with at most ten calls, 0.116, 0.174 and 0.200. The differential visibility, which on its own would inflate the raw excess, is outweighed by the plain fact that both groups' callbacks are undercounted, and an undercounted difference is a smaller difference. The weighted prior-window shares remain close (0.859 and 0.903 at an hour for the larger group), a little less balanced than the raw ones.

Taking windows of thirty and sixty minutes, which contain 62% and 80% of the callbacks, and both the raw and the corrected measures for both groups of IDs, the excess calling caused by hanging up lies between 0.14 and 0.20. That is the number the interval estimator should find, with two caveats on what it means. It is the effect of hanging up relative to being served, not relative to never having called; a served caller with an unresolved problem calls back too, and that kind of repeat call, which Ding, Koole and van der Mei call a reconnect, sits in the baseline. And it describes the callers who carry IDs, who are existing customers rather than prospects; the 42% of abandoned calls without an ID may behave differently.

The right panel of Figure 1 shows how long the callbacks take. Of the abandoned calls followed by another call within three hours, 18,657 for IDs with at most 50 calls, 35% come back within ten minutes, 62% within thirty and 80% within an hour. The median delay is 19 minutes and the mean 34.5. The deciles run from two minutes at the first to 94 minutes at the ninth. These include the baseline calling, which is spread more evenly in time, so the delay distribution of true redials is probably somewhat shorter than this. Either way it is far from the ten-minute exponential delay the earlier articles assumed: a fifth of these callbacks come after more than an hour, which is two intervals or more in the table's terms.

## The slope on the real table

The estimator from the previous article works in three steps. First, for each day and half hour, the surplus: offered calls minus the mean offered calls of that weekday and half hour across the data, with the expected calls rescaled so that each day's expected total equals its observed total. The rescaling removes anything that affects a whole day, such as a busy Monday, so that a day with more abandonments and more calls for reasons unrelated to redials does not produce a slope by itself. Second, the slope of the surplus in each half hour on the abandoned calls of the half hour before, within days, with the abandonments demeaned within each day. Third, the same slope computed on long runs of simulated days at a grid of redial probabilities, with the same random numbers at every grid point so that the simulated slope moves smoothly with the probability. A quadratic through the simulated slopes, forced to be increasing, is the binding function in Gouriéroux, Monfort and Renault's language, and the probability at which it equals the real slope is the estimate. Its sampling error comes from a bootstrap over days, each bootstrap slope inverted through the same binding function.

Inferring retrial behaviour from data that do not identify callers is an old problem. Hoffman and Harris estimated a caller retrial rate for a telephone information system in 1986. Aguir and co-authors used a fluid approximation of a queue with abandonment and retrials to recover true arrival rates from call-centre demand data in which retrials cannot be told apart from first calls. Ding, Koole and van der Mei found that from such data the fresh demand, the redial probability and the reconnect probability cannot all be estimated at once: one of them has to be supplied for the other two to be estimated accurately. The slope estimator sidesteps that problem by using the timing of events within the day rather than their totals, which is also what exposes it to the problem this article is about.

The simulator is built from the table. Each simulated day takes the agent counts of one of the real days, replayed half hour by half hour. Fresh calls arrive as Poisson counts per half hour with a rate equal to the real mean offered calls for that weekday and half hour, scaled by a day factor drawn from the real days' totals and deflated for the redials that the real means already contain. The queue is the event-by-event Erlang A simulator of the earlier articles: first come, first served, exponential handle times with the effective mean of 294 seconds, and exponential patience whose rate is the number of abandoned calls divided by the total time callers spent waiting. That ratio is the maximum-likelihood estimate of the patience rate when patience is exponential, and here it puts the mean patience at 413 seconds. A caller who hangs up calls again with probability p after a delay drawn from an assumed distribution, and a redial can itself be abandoned and redialled. Mandelbaum and Zeltyn's account of the Palm/Erlang-A queue is the standard reference for this model.

On the real table the slope is 0.367, with a bootstrap standard deviation of 0.088. In the simulator without redials and with independent demand across half hours, with a ten-minute exponential delay, the same slope is −0.012; with p = 0.3 it is 0.103, and with p = 0.8, the edge of the grid, 0.483. The real slope therefore maps to an estimate of 0.67 with a ten-minute exponential delay, with a 90% interval from 0.50 to the edge of the grid. With exponential delays of five and twenty minutes the estimate is 0.70 and 0.66; with delays drawn from the callbacks measured in Figure 1 it is 0.74. Against an excess of 0.14 to 0.20 measured directly, every version of the estimator puts the redial probability at three to five times what the IDs show.

The estimator has a placebo test built in, and running it is the first thing an analyst with only the table can do.

## Looking backwards in time

Redials travel forward in time. A caller who hangs up at 10:20 can call back at 10:35, but not at 9:50. So if the slope of surplus calls on the previous half hour's abandonments measures redials, the slope of surplus calls on the following half hour's abandonments should not. Whatever produces a slope in that direction is something other than redials, and the size of the backward slope is a direct estimate of how much of the forward slope that something might be.

![Lag and lead slopes of surplus calls on abandonments](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/anonymous-bank-redials/figures/fig2_lag_lead_slopes.png)

*Figure 2. The within-day slope of surplus offered calls on abandoned calls one to three half hours earlier (right) and one to three half hours later (left), for all offered calls (blue), for offered calls minus those identified from customer IDs as redials (orange), and for the identified redials alone (green). Bars are 90% intervals from a bootstrap over days.*

Figure 2 shows the result. The slope on the following half hour's abandonments, the lead slope, is 0.354, against a lag slope of 0.367. Two half hours apart the slopes are 0.208 backwards and 0.231 forwards, three apart 0.101 and 0.163. The relation between abandonments and surplus calls is almost symmetric in time, and most of it cannot be redials.

What produces a symmetric relation is demand that comes in spells. Suppose that on some mornings, for reasons nobody in the centre controls, more people than usual want to talk to the bank between ten and eleven: a market movement, a problem with a card system, a statement that went out with a mistake on it. That hour's half hours all have more offered calls than the calendar predicts, and because the agents were scheduled for an ordinary morning they all have more abandonments. Any two adjacent half hours inside the spell show surplus calls and extra abandonments together, and it does not matter which of the two comes first. The day-level rescaling does not remove this, because the spell is shorter than a day, and the slope reads the co-movement as the response of calls to abandonment.

The table carries direct evidence of such spells. Within days, the lag-one autocorrelation of the surplus is 0.43: a half hour with more calls than expected tends to be followed by another. The variance of the surplus is 3.2 times what Poisson counts around the calendar would produce. Neither property can come from redials of the size the IDs measure: in the simulator described below, redials with a probability of 0.3 and no clustering give an autocorrelation of 0.08 and a dispersion of 1.17. Avramidis, Deslauriers and L'Ecuyer listed this pattern, variance above the mean and positive correlation between the counts of different periods of the same day, among the essential features of call-centre arrivals in 2004, and Jongbloed and Koole had modelled the extra variance as a Poisson mixture in 2001. The simulated world of the earlier articles had a day-level shock, which the rescaling removes, and an independent shock for each half hour, which produces neither autocorrelation nor a slope. Its placebo world without redials gave a slope of −0.039, and I wrote that this showed how well the design of the statistic worked. It showed that the design worked against the confounders the simulation contained.

The IDs allow a split that the table alone does not. Removing from each half hour the offered calls whose previous call from the same ID was abandoned less than an hour earlier, the identified redials, which make up 6.0% of offered calls, leaves a lag slope of 0.308 and a lead slope of 0.313, still symmetric. The identified redials alone have a lag slope of 0.050 and a lead slope of 0.033. Their slope is asymmetric, as redials should be, and their lag-minus-lead difference of 0.017 (standard deviation 0.005) is about the whole of the asymmetry in the total, which is 0.013 with a standard deviation of 0.027. The lead slope of the identified redials is not zero, which is what clustering would add to any series that tracks the load, and redials do: more abandonment in a busy spell means more redials in the same spell.

## Three ways to separate redials from busy spells

If clustering is the problem, there are three obvious ways to deal with it, each with its own cost.

The first uses the placebo directly. The lag slope contains redials plus clustering; the lead slope, it would seem, contains clustering only. Their difference should contain redials only, and its binding function can be computed and inverted exactly as before. It is the simplest repair and requires no new modelling. Its weakness is that demand may be symmetric in time but the queue is not. A half hour with more calls than expected leaves a backlog, and the backlog raises abandonments in the following half hour, so congestion carried forward by the queue adds to the lead slope and not to the lag slope. The difference is therefore negative when nobody redials, and by how much depends on how much congestion carries over, which depends in turn on how demand clusters. There is also some loss of signal: when abandonments are persistent, the redials from half hour t − 1, which land in half hour t, are correlated with the abandonments in t + 1 as well, so part of the redials' effect appears in the lead slope and the difference subtracts it.

The second keeps the lag slope but adds a control. Busy spells act on the surplus in half hour t through the surplus in half hour t − 1, which is a noisy measure of how busy the spell is; redials act through the abandonments in t − 1. A regression of the surplus in t on both, within days, should leave on the abandonments only what clustering cannot explain. I call its coefficient on abandonments the controlled slope. The cost is the noise: the previous surplus measures the spell with Poisson error, so the control is incomplete, and abandonments, which are a second noisy measure of the same spell, keep part of the clustering's effect. That is the textbook errors-in-variables problem, and it biases the controlled slope towards the plain one.

The third models the clustering. The fresh-call rate in the simulator is multiplied by a log-normal factor that follows a first-order autoregression within the day, with persistence ρ and standard deviation σ. With ρ = 0 and σ = 0 it is the simulator above; with ρ near one, busy and quiet spells last for hours. The redial probability and the two clustering parameters are then estimated together by matching five statistics: the lag slope, the lead slope, the controlled slope, the lag-one autocorrelation of the surplus and its dispersion. The last two pin down the clustering, the asymmetry between the first two and the controlled slope pin down the redials, and none of them is asked to do both jobs alone. Each statistic is simulated on a grid of 150 parameter combinations, six redial probabilities from 0 to 0.6, five persistences from 0 to 0.85 and five standard deviations from 0.03 to 0.3, with 494 simulated days at each point and the same random numbers throughout. A quadratic response surface in the three parameters fits each statistic with an R² between 0.976 and 0.991. The estimate minimises the squared distance between the surfaces and the observed statistics, each weighted by one over its bootstrap variance, and the bootstrap over days is repeated 300 times, re-solving each time, for the intervals. This is the costliest repair, in computation and in assumptions: the log-normal autoregression is one shape of clustering among many, and the method is only as good as that shape.

## A check where the answer is known

Before trusting any of the three on the real table, they need to be tested where the answer is known, which brings back the simulation. This time the simulated worlds are built from the real centre: its calendar, its days' agent counts, its effective handle time and patience. There are two kinds of world. In one, demand is independent across half hours, as in the earlier articles. In the other, it clusters as much as the joint estimator finds in the real data, with ρ = 0.75 and σ = 0.21. In each, the true redial probability is 0, 0.15 or 0.3, the delay is exponential with a ten-minute mean, and the analyst knows the delay. Each of the six worlds produces 16 independent histories of 247 days, the length of the real data, and each history is given to all four estimators. The three single-statistic estimators use binding functions simulated without clustering, which is what an analyst who had not thought about clustering would do. The joint estimator uses response surfaces simulated over the grid, and the inverse-variance weights from the real data.

![Estimators on simulated histories with a known redial probability](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/anonymous-bank-redials/figures/fig3_simulation_check.png)

*Figure 3. Estimated redial probability on 16 simulated histories of 247 days (small dots) and their mean (large markers), for each true probability (dashed lines) and each estimator. Left: demand independent across half hours. Right: demand clustered within the day as much as the joint estimator finds in the real data.*

With independent demand, the left panel of Figure 3, all four estimators work. The plain lag slope averages 0.004, 0.147 and 0.298 for true probabilities of 0, 0.15 and 0.3, with standard deviations between 0.008 and 0.031. The controlled slope averages 0.014, 0.128 and 0.276, slightly attenuated, and the joint estimator 0.037, 0.153 and 0.291, slightly biased upwards at zero because a probability cannot be negative and its errors there are one-sided. The lag-minus-lead estimator is the noisiest (standard deviations up to 0.057) and averages 0.038, 0.117 and 0.261. This is the kind of world the earlier article tested in, and its conclusion holds here.

With clustered demand, the right panel, the plain lag slope stops measuring redials. It averages 0.598 when nobody redials, 0.644 at a true 0.15 and 0.706 at a true 0.3. Its root mean squared error is 0.60 in the world without redials, and its response to a change of 0.3 in the truth is about 0.1. In the language of the simulator, the clustering alone produces a lag slope of about 0.31, close to the 0.308 left in the real data after removing the identified redials. The estimator from the previous article, given a centre like this one, would report a large redial probability whether or not anyone redialled, and nothing in its own output would say so.

The lag-minus-lead estimator fails in the opposite direction. It averages 0.002, 0.009 and 0.076, close to zero whatever the truth. The statistic itself still moves with the redial probability: with clustered demand it averages −0.074, −0.055 and −0.038 for the three true values, against −0.046, −0.030 and −0.003 with independent demand. But clustering shifts it down by about 0.03, because busy spells build backlogs that carry congestion into the following half hour, and the binding function computed without clustering reads that shift as an absence of redials.

The controlled slope survives, imperfectly. It averages 0.084 without redials, 0.146 at 0.15 and 0.193 at 0.3, with standard deviations around 0.03. It is biased upwards at zero by the incomplete control and responds to the truth with a little over a third of the right slope, so its root mean squared error is 0.089 at zero, 0.025 at 0.15 and 0.111 at 0.3. Near 0.15 it happens to be right; elsewhere it is pulled towards a value around 0.15.

The joint estimator is the only one that tracks the truth in both worlds. With clustered demand it averages 0.065, 0.186 and 0.275, with standard deviations around 0.05 and root mean squared errors between 0.055 and 0.084. It also recovers the clustering, with an upward bias: its persistence estimate averages between 0.80 and 0.83 against a true 0.75, and between 0.05 and 0.08 in the worlds without clustering. The price of modelling the confounder is precision. With 247 days and a standard deviation of 0.05 per history, the joint estimator cannot reliably separate a redial probability of 0.15 from 0.25.

## Estimates from the real table

Figure 4 shows what each estimator says about the real centre, under four assumptions about the delay between hanging up and calling again: exponential with a mean of five, ten or twenty minutes, and the empirical delays of Figure 1.

![Redial probability estimated from the real half-hour table](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/anonymous-bank-redials/figures/fig4_real_estimates.png)

*Figure 4. The redial probability estimated from the real half-hour table by each estimator, under four delay assumptions, with 90% intervals from a bootstrap over days. The dotted line marks the edge of the grid of simulated probabilities, where the intervals of the plain lag slope are cut off. The grey band is the excess calling after hanging up measured from customer IDs.*

The plain lag slope gives 0.70, 0.67, 0.66 and 0.74, with lower ends of the 90% intervals between 0.47 and 0.54 and upper ends at the edge of the simulated grid. The lag-minus-lead estimator gives 0.52, 0.38, 0.28 and 0.33, with lower ends between 0.12 and 0.22 and upper ends between 0.50 and the edge of the grid. The controlled slope gives 0.18, 0.16, 0.15 and 0.18, with lower ends between 0.02 and 0.04 and upper ends between 0.25 and 0.30. The joint estimator gives 0.28, 0.22, 0.17 and 0.19, with every interval starting at zero and ending between 0.31 and 0.43. Its estimates of the clustering are stable across the delay assumptions, a persistence between 0.73 and 0.78 and a standard deviation between 0.20 and 0.22.

Read against the simulation check, the four rows say different things. The plain lag slope's answer is what the simulation predicts for a clustered centre with any redial probability at all, and it carries no information about redials. The controlled slope lands inside the band measured from the IDs, but the simulation check says it would have landed near there for a wide range of true values; its agreement with the IDs is partly a property of the estimator. In the clustered simulation the controlled slope itself averaged 0.029, 0.056 and 0.076 for true probabilities of 0, 0.15 and 0.3; its real value of 0.059 sits just above the middle one, which points to a redial probability of about 0.17. That is reassuring, but it leans on the same simulator. The joint estimator is the one whose answer the simulation supports, and with the empirical delays it says 0.19, inside the ID band, with a 90% interval from 0 to 0.37.

The lag-minus-lead estimator needs more explanation. In the clustered simulation it was biased towards zero, yet on the real table it reports between 0.28 and 0.52. The reason is visible in the statistics themselves. In the simulator without redials, the lead slope exceeds the lag slope by 0.046 with independent demand and by 0.074 with the data's clustering, because the queue carries congestion forward. In the real data the lag slope exceeds the lead slope by 0.013, and the series without identified redials has lag and lead slopes within 0.005 of each other. The comparison is sharpest in the clustered simulation without redials, which is the closest simulated analogue of the real series with the identified redials removed. Its lag slope is 0.307, against a real 0.308; its autocorrelation of the surplus is 0.429, against a real 0.430. Its lead slope is 0.382, against a real 0.313. The simulator reproduces the clustering of this centre almost exactly and overstates how much congestion its queue carries forward, and the lag-minus-lead estimator reads the missing carry-over as redials.

The joint estimator sees the same discrepancy and cannot resolve it. At its estimate with the ten-minute delay, the fitted lag slope is 0.339 against an observed 0.367 and the fitted lead slope 0.388 against 0.354, while the controlled slope, the autocorrelation and the dispersion fit to within 0.006 of the observed values. In the simulated worlds with the data's clustering, the lead slope stays above the lag slope at every redial probability tried, because the simulated queue carries congestion forward more than the real one appears to, and the fit settles for a compromise. The intervals do not include this misfit: they describe the sampling error of the statistics, given a simulator that is right. That is the uncomfortable part of indirect inference on real data. The estimate is only as good as the simulator's account of every statistic used, including the ones that were included only to identify a nuisance.

Why the real centre carries less congestion forward than the simulated one, the data do not say, and I can only list candidates. The simulator replays each day's agent counts as fixed in advance, but a supervisor who sees a queue building can pull agents from other work onto the phones within minutes, which would clear backlogs faster than replayed staffing allows. The agent counts in the table are themselves built from the calls agents served, so a half hour in which more agents were pulled onto the phones shows more agents, but the timing within the half hour is lost. The effective handle time of 294 seconds, chosen to reproduce the abandonment level, may make the simulated queue more persistent than the real one if the true constraint was something other than handle time. And patience in real tele-queues adapts to the waits callers expect, as Zohar, Mandelbaum and Shimkin argued and supported with call-centre data, while the simulator's patience is fixed. Any of these, or all of them, could produce the mismatch. Each is a conjecture.

## How much of the slope is redials

The question this article set out to answer was how much of the slope the estimator reads as redials is in fact redials. The pieces can now be put together, with the caveat that they come from different instruments.

From the IDs: the identified redials, a generous definition that includes callers who would have called within the hour anyway, have a lag slope of 0.050, 14% of the total of 0.367. They cover only callers with an ID and only callbacks that queued. Scaling them up as if the 42% of abandoned calls without an ID behaved the same way, and as if a fifth of callbacks went unseen, gives about 0.050 / (0.58 × 0.8) ≈ 0.11, still under a third of the slope. That arithmetic is rough, and it errs on the generous side.

From the simulation: with the real data's clustering and no redials, the simulated lag slope averages 0.307; with a redial probability of 0.15 it averages 0.343, and with 0.3, 0.394. The real 0.367 falls between the last two. By this account clustering alone produces about 0.31 of the slope, and redials of the size the IDs measure the remaining 0.03 to 0.06.

The two accounts agree that redials explain somewhere between a tenth and a third of the slope and busy spells the rest. They are not independent, because the identified redials are themselves concentrated in busy spells, and they are not precise. But the order of magnitude is not in doubt. An estimator built on the slope alone, without a check on the lead, will find redials in any centre where demand clusters, and most real centres' demand does.

## What this changes in the earlier articles

The previous article reported that the structural slot, the redial probability estimated from the slope and added to Erlang A, was the only correction that moved a surge forecast towards the truth, that it switched itself off in a world without redials, and that its parameter could be checked against call-level logs. All three statements were true of the simulated world. The third has now been carried out, and the check fails for the plain statistic: the call-level logs say about 0.17, and the slope says 0.67.

The second statement needs the most qualification. The slot switched itself off in a world without redials and without within-day clustering. In a world with the clustering of this real centre, it would have switched itself on with a probability around 0.6 whether or not anyone redialled. A placebo that differs from the main world only in the mechanism of interest tests the estimator's sensitivity, not its specificity, and I presented it as a test of both.

The practical consequences are large. A redial probability of 0.67 means that two of every three abandoned calls return as offered calls, and in a surge, when abandonment rises, the slot would multiply the extra load accordingly. With 0.17, the multiplication is a quarter as strong. The forecast of the previous article was right about its own world because the slot's statistic was right about its own world. Applied to this centre, the same pipeline would have put about four times too much redial feedback into a surge forecast, and the staffing it recommended for a surge would have erred on the expensive side.

The first statement, that a structural slot moved the forecast towards the truth where output corrections could not, is not touched by this test, because it was about where to put a correction rather than how to size it. What is touched is the claim that the slot is cheap in data and expensive in thought. It turns out to be expensive in thought twice over: once to name the mechanism, and again to name everything else that could produce the statistic used to size it.

The first article's conclusion, which the second one softened, also comes back in a different form. That article said the data could not tell whether redials exist. The second said a designed statistic could. This one finds that, for a real centre with 247 days of half-hourly data, the best-supported estimator gives an interval from zero to about 0.4. The table does contain information about redials, but not enough, after the clustering is accounted for, to exclude their absence at the 90% level.

## Working with only the table

Most analysts who want to know whether their callers redial will not have customer IDs, or will have them in a system they cannot query. The test above suggests a sequence for working with only the interval table, and it costs little beyond what the original estimator already required.

Compute the lead slope next to the lag slope. It is the same calculation with the time index reversed, and if it is close to the lag slope, the lag slope is not measuring redials. Look at the lags beyond one as well; a slope that decays slowly in both directions is the signature of spells rather than of a response.

Measure the clustering directly. The lag-one autocorrelation of the within-day surplus and its dispersion relative to Poisson take a few lines of code. If the autocorrelation is near zero and the dispersion near one, the earlier estimator's assumptions hold, and its answer can be taken roughly at face value. If not, the simulator needs a clustering component before any of its binding functions mean anything.

Estimate redials and clustering together, and report intervals. A single-statistic estimator gives a single number with an air of precision. The joint estimator here gave intervals from zero to about 0.4 on a year of data, and that width is the honest answer to the question.

Check the simulator against every statistic the estimator uses, not only the target. The misfit between the fitted and observed lead slopes was the most informative thing the joint estimator produced: it said that the simulated queue's carry-over of congestion was wrong, which says something about the simulator that an estimate alone hides.

Vary the delay assumption. On this table the delay mattered less for the plain slope than for the other estimators: five-minute and twenty-minute delays moved the lag-minus-lead estimate from 0.52 to 0.28 and the joint estimate from 0.28 to 0.17. The callbacks measured from IDs took a median of 19 minutes, with a long tail beyond an hour, which is not what an exponential with a ten-minute mean looks like.

And if any call-level data with identifiers exist, even for a sample of days or a subset of callers, use them. Some twenty thousand abandoned calls with IDs were enough here to pin the redial effect to a band of six hundredths, which a year of interval data did not achieve. When using them, read the documentation for how the identifier was recorded: in this database, it was recorded only for calls that queued, and the measurement had to be corrected for that.

## Limits of this test

This is one centre, one year and one country's working week, and 1999 is a long time ago in contact-centre terms. Callers then reached a bank by phone for things they now do in an app, and their reasons for calling, and for calling again, were different. The test shows that within-day clustering can dominate the slope; it does not show that it will in any particular centre today.

The direct measurement relies on the served group being a fair baseline for the abandoned group. The prior-window check supports this, but the two groups could differ in ways the prior window cannot show: a caller who hung up because the call was not urgent might also be less likely to call back for other reasons, and a caller who was served might be more likely to call back with a follow-up that the service itself caused. Either would make the measured excess smaller than the redial probability the simulator means, and both are plausible. The band of 0.14 to 0.20 should be read as a measurement under a stated comparison, not as a law of these callers' behaviour.

The visibility correction assumes that within a half hour identified customers queue as often as everyone else. Priority customers, whom the system credits with a minute and a half of waiting when their call starts so that they move up the queue, wait less than others in the same half hour; if their callbacks are less often queued and they also call back at a different rate from other callers, the correction is off in their direction.

The simulator makes choices that the real centre does not: exponential handle times, where Brown and co-authors found them close to log-normal in these data; exponential patience fixed in time; a log-normal autoregression for the clustering; agent counts replayed as fixed and built as a lower bound from the calls served; an effective handle time chosen to match the abandonment level. The simulation check shows how the estimators behave if the simulator is right. It cannot show how they behave if it is wrong, and the lead-slope misfit says it is wrong in at least one respect that matters.

The joint estimator's response surfaces are quadratic, and its bootstrap re-solves the fit 300 times. Both are approximations, and both are reported. The intervals throughout describe sampling error given the simulator, not the error in the simulator itself, and should be read as lower bounds on the uncertainty.

Finally, the comparison between the interval estimators and the IDs is between two different quantities. The simulator's redial probability is the probability that an abandonment produces a callback at any delay, with the callback itself subject to abandonment and further redials. The ID excess is a difference in the share calling again within a window, so it misses redials that come after the window, and it counts each abandoned call once, however many calls follow it. With 80% of the callbacks inside an hour, the first effect could put the redial probability the simulator means a few hundredths above the band, and the comparisons above should be read with that in mind.

## Two conjectures

Two conjectures, stated as conjectures.

The first is that any estimator that reads a behavioural response from the timing of aggregate events, in data where the events themselves cluster, will confuse the clustering with the response unless it is checked against the reverse direction of time. Call centres with redials are one case. Others are epidemics, where a rise in cases followed by a rise in testing could be either a response to the rise or a common cause driving both, and online platforms, where a burst of failures followed by a burst of retries looks the same as a burst of traffic. The lead slope is a cheap placebo for all of them, and it is rarely reported.

The second is that the mismatch in carry-over between the real centre and the simulator, the real queue clearing backlogs faster than a queue with replayed staffing, is a general feature of managed service systems rather than a peculiarity of this one: supervisors react to queues within minutes, and interval-level staffing data record the reaction only as a count. If that is right, simulators that take staffing as given will overstate how long congestion lasts, and any statistic whose binding function depends on that persistence will be biased in a way that no amount of data from the same simulator can reveal.

## Reproducing the results

The folder in the repository holds a script that downloads the archive from the SEE Center and checks its SHA-256, the package that reads the records and builds the half-hourly table, the ID analysis, the statistics, the simulator and the estimators, five experiment scripts, a script that collects every number into `results/summary.txt` and one that draws the figures. A full run takes about six minutes on two cores. All randomness flows from one master seed, and a full run reproduces the committed results exactly. The half-hourly table is committed, so the slope analysis, the simulation check and the estimates run without the raw records; the ID analysis needs them. A continuous-integration workflow runs the tests and every experiment at small sizes on each change.

The data were collected by Ilan Guedj and Avi Mandelbaum and are published by the Technion SEE Center, whose documentation states that they are free to use and asks users to notify Avi Mandelbaum and to acknowledge the source. This article and its code acknowledge it here, and in the repository's citation file.
