# Correcting Regressions for Measurement Error With Replicates, Validation Samples and Instruments

## What a noisy covariate does to its own coefficient and to its neighbours, what each kind of auxiliary data can repair, and why a predictive model often should not be corrected

A regression is fitted to columns, and a column is a measurement. Blood pressure is one cuff reading taken by one examiner on one afternoon. Income is what a respondent recalls in an interview. Ability is a test score, customer satisfaction is the average of five survey items, and weight is whatever the person typed into the form. The gap between the column and the quantity the analysis is about is measurement error, and it is one of the oldest data challenges in statistics: Spearman (1904) proposed a correction for it before most of modern regression existed. It is also one of the most ignored in data science, partly because its main symptom is invisible. A noisy covariate does not make a model fail. It makes the model quietly understate the effect of that covariate and hand part of it to the other variables in the regression.

Three facts organise everything that follows. The first is that classical measurement error in a covariate shrinks its coefficient toward zero by a factor called the reliability ratio, and moves the coefficients of every covariate correlated with it. The second is that none of this can be corrected from the data at hand: every correction imports information from outside the regression, whether replicate measurements, a validation subsample measured properly, an instrument, or a reliability coefficient published by someone else, and every one of those sources carries an assumption that can fail. The third is that whether to correct depends on the question. A coefficient that is meant to describe the world needs the correction. A model that will predict from the same noisy measurement it was trained on usually does not, and correcting it makes its predictions worse.

This guide works through those three facts on real data. Four public data sets each supply one kind of outside information: three blood-pressure readings per person in NHANES, measured and self-reported weight and height in a small validation study, two different test scores as measures of ability in a wage survey, and the items of a personality questionnaire. A simulation built on NHANES covariates then tests nine estimators against six kinds of error, including the kinds that break the usual corrections, and a final experiment shows when the corrected model is the wrong one to deploy. The estimators (corrected least squares, regression calibration, SIMEX, instrumental variables and multiple imputation) were written for the article and checked against their known large-sample behaviour; the code is linked at the end.

## Attenuation, leakage and three variations

The simplest model of measurement error says that the recorded value W is the true value X plus noise: W = X + U, where U has mean zero and is independent of X, of the other covariates Z and of the outcome. Statisticians call this classical error. Fit the regression of the outcome on W and Z instead of X and Z, and the least-squares coefficients converge, as the sample grows, to

```
b_W  →  λ β,          λ = var(X | Z) / (var(X | Z) + var(U))
b_Z  →  β_Z + (1 − λ) β γ,   γ = coefficient of Z in the regression of X on Z
```

The coefficient of the noisy covariate is multiplied by λ, the reliability of W after the other covariates have been accounted for, which is always below one. The coefficient of every other covariate absorbs part of the lost effect, in proportion to how strongly that covariate predicts X. The second line is the one practitioners forget. If blood pressure is measured with error and age predicts blood pressure, the age coefficient in a model with both will be too large, because age is a cleaner proxy for the part of blood pressure that the noisy reading misses. In a causal analysis this is residual confounding: adjusting for a mismeasured confounder removes only part of the confounding, and the treatment effect keeps the rest (Fuller, 1987; Carroll, Ruppert, Stefanski and Crainiceanu, 2006).

The reliability that matters is the conditional one, and it can be much lower than the unconditional reliability a test manual or a replicate study reports. If a test score has reliability 0.9 in the population but half of its variance is predicted by the other covariates in the model, the noise is the same and the signal it competes with has halved, so the conditional reliability is about 0.82. In the NHANES-based simulation below, age, sex and BMI explain a fifth of the variance of systolic pressure, and an unconditional reliability of 0.7 becomes a conditional one of 0.65.

Three variations on the classical model change the picture. Error in the outcome rather than in a covariate does not bias the coefficients of a linear regression, as long as it is unrelated to the covariates (Hausman, 2001): it adds noise to the residual, inflates the standard errors and lowers the R², and that is all. When the outcome error is related to a covariate, it biases that covariate's coefficient directly, and the NHANES readings below show a real case. Berkson error, named after Berkson (1950), reverses the roles: the true value scatters around the recorded one, X = W + U, as when every worker in a job category is assigned the category's average exposure or every patient in a dose group is recorded at the nominal dose. In linear regression Berkson error costs precision and causes no bias. And differential error, which depends on the outcome itself, as when cases recall their past exposures differently from controls, can bias a coefficient in either direction and defeats most corrections. In nonlinear models such as logistic regression, classical error attenuates roughly as in the linear case, but the corrections become approximations (Rosner, Spiegelman and Willett, 1990; Carroll et al., 2006).

## Nothing is corrected for free

In the data that a regression normally sees (the noisy covariate, the other covariates and the outcome) the true coefficient and the size of the measurement error cannot be told apart. When the variables are roughly normal, a large effect measured with a lot of noise and a smaller effect measured well produce the same joint distribution of what was recorded, and no amount of data distinguishes them. Every correction therefore brings in information from outside, and the article is organised by the four places that information can come from.

Replicates are two or more measurements of the same quantity on the same person, with errors that are independent of each other. Their disagreement measures the error variance, and from it the reliability. A validation subsample is a subset of the data in which the true value, or a much better measurement of it, was also recorded; it gives the relationship between the measured and the true value directly, including any structure that is not classical. An instrument is a second variable that is correlated with the true value but independent of the measurement error and of the outcome's own noise; a second measurement taken independently is the usual example. And a reliability coefficient can come from elsewhere: a test manual, the internal consistency of a multi-item scale, a published replicate study.

The estimators are ways of using that information. Corrected least squares, the method of moments of Fuller (1987), subtracts the error variance from the covariance matrix of the covariates before solving the normal equations. Regression calibration (Carroll and Stefanski, 1990; Carroll et al., 2006) replaces the noisy value by the best prediction of the true value given the measurements and the other covariates, then fits the outcome model as usual; in linear regression with replicates the two are the same estimator, and the code checks that they agree to eight decimals. SIMEX, simulation-extrapolation (Cook and Stefanski, 1994), adds more noise of known size, watches how the coefficient degrades, and extrapolates the trend back to the point of no noise. Instrumental variables use the second measurement to isolate the part of the first that reflects the true value, an idea that goes back to Reiersøl (1945) and Durbin (1954). Multiple imputation for measurement error (Cole, Chu and Greenland, 2006) treats the true value as missing outside the validation subsample and imputes it from the measurements, the covariates and the outcome. Each source of information carries an assumption, and the four real cases below show each assumption failing.

## Replicates: three blood-pressure readings in NHANES

The National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey measures blood pressure with a protocol designed for exactly this problem. After five minutes of seated rest, a certified physician examiner in the mobile examination centre takes three consecutive readings, with a fourth attempt if one is interrupted, and records them in steps of 2 mmHg (National Center for Health Statistics, BPX_G documentation); in 2009–2010 the standard device was a mercury sphygmomanometer (Ostchega et al., 2012). The published average excludes the first reading whenever there is more than one, so with three readings it is the mean of the second and third. In the 2009–2012 cycles, as distributed in the NHANES R package (Pruim, 2026), 9,390 of the 10,990 adults aged 20 to 79 have three systolic readings, an age and a body-mass index.

The three readings agree closely. Their pairwise correlations are 0.949 (first and second), 0.935 (first and third) and 0.953 (second and third). The second and third readings give a within-person standard deviation of 3.84 mmHg against a between-person standard deviation of 17.24 for their average, so a single reading has a reliability of 0.952, and the average of two has 0.975. Used as an exposure in a regression, a single reading would attenuate its coefficient by about 5 per cent and the NHANES average of two readings by about 2.5 per cent. That is a small correction, and the honest conclusion for a protocol measurement taken by trained staff is that within-session noise barely matters.

The readings are not replicates, though, and the way they fail to be replicates matters more than their noise. The first reading is higher than the average of the second and third by 1.39 mmHg on average, and the difference grows with age: it is 0.64 mmHg for people in their twenties and 2.93 mmHg for people in their seventies, an increase of 0.48 mmHg per decade (standard error 0.035). The second reading is in turn higher than the third by 0.74 mmHg, again more so in older people (0.24 mmHg per decade). Pressure falls as the session goes on, and it falls further in older participants. The pattern is consistent with the alerting reaction to being measured, the effect that at a larger scale is called white-coat hypertension, and it means that "the" blood pressure of a person depends on when the measuring stopped.

*Figure 1. Left: mean difference between the first reading and the average of the second and third, and between the second and third readings, by age band, with 95% confidence intervals; 9,390 NHANES 2009–2012 adults with three systolic readings. Right: the age slope of systolic pressure from regressions on age, sex and BMI, with each reading or average as the outcome. The intervals for readings 1 and 2 overlap, but the slopes come from the same people: the difference between the first-reading slope and the slope for the average of readings 2 and 3 is 0.048 mmHg per year with a standard error of 0.0035.*

The right panel of Figure 1 shows the consequence when blood pressure is the outcome. A regression of systolic pressure on age, sex and BMI gives an age slope of 0.463 mmHg per year with the first reading as the outcome, 0.428 with the second, 0.403 with the third, and 0.416 with the NHANES average of the second and third. Classical error in an outcome cannot do this. The first reading's error is related to age, so it adds 0.048 mmHg per year, 11.5 per cent, to the age gradient of blood pressure. It also raises the R² from 0.200 to 0.216, because part of the "noise" is explained by age: a noisier outcome that fits better is a sign that the noise is not noise. An analyst who used the first reading because it was the only one available in some other data set would overstate how fast blood pressure rises with age, and no correction based on the variance of the error would fix it, because the problem is the error's correlation with a covariate, not its size.

The second lesson of these replicates is about what they cannot see. All three readings were taken within one session, so their disagreement measures error over minutes. What epidemiologists usually want is a person's long-run usual blood pressure, which varies from week to week and season to season, and that variation is invisible to readings taken minutes apart: their errors share everything the session has in common. Studies that remeasure the same people years later find far more dilution, the regression dilution bias of epidemiology (Hutcheon, Chiolero and Hanley, 2010; Frost and Thompson, 2000, compare the corrections). MacMahon and colleagues (1990), correcting for it in prospective studies, found associations of blood pressure with stroke and coronary heart disease about 60 per cent greater than uncorrected analyses had reported, and Clarke and colleagues (1999) estimated that associations with a single baseline measurement of blood pressure or cholesterol understate the real ones by about a third in the first decade of follow-up, a half in the second and two-thirds in the third. A regression-calibration correction based on the within-session readings would apply the 5 per cent correction and report the result as corrected; relative to usual blood pressure it would still be heavily attenuated. Replicates correct exactly the error that varies between them, and nothing else.

The diastolic readings add a final warning about what a "measurement" can contain. Of the 9,468 adults with three diastolic readings, 104 have at least one reading of 0 mmHg, 164 zero readings in all. A diastolic pressure of zero is not a physiological value, and a model that treated it as one would be fitting an artefact of the measurement procedure.

## A validation sample whose gold standard contains the worst error

The Davis data in R's carData package come from a study of body image and weight preoccupation among people who exercise regularly (Davis, 1990). Each of 200 participants was weighed and measured; 183 reported their own weight and 183 their own height, so the data set is itself a validation study: for 181 people it contains both a self-report and the measured value for each quantity.

Self-reports here are good. Among the 180 people with complete data other than one problem row discussed below, reported and measured weight correlate at 0.956 for women and 0.979 for men, and reported and measured height at 0.940 and 0.952. The errors are not classical, though. Women under-report their weight by 0.52 kg on average and the under-reporting grows with weight: the slope of reported on measured weight is 0.938, so a woman who weighs 10 kg more reports about 9.4 kg more. Men's reports are nearly unbiased (slope 0.990, mean error +0.59 kg). Reported heights are on average 2.38 cm (women) and 1.76 cm (men) below the measured ones. Error that depends on the true value, as women's weight reports do, is common in survey data (Bound, Brown and Mathiowetz, 2001), and it is exactly what replicate-based corrections assume away and what a validation sample can measure.

The analysis question is the allometric exponent of weight on height: in a regression of log weight on log height and sex, a coefficient of 2 would mean that weight scales with the square of height, the premise behind the body-mass index. On the measured values, after removing one row, the exponent is 2.087 (standard error 0.253). On the self-reports it is 1.864, an attenuation of 11 per cent, and replacing only the reported height by the measured one restores all of it and slightly overshoots (2.144), so the attenuation comes from the error in reported height, which sits on the right-hand side of the regression.

The row that had to be removed is row 12. Its measured weight is 166 kg and its measured height is 57 cm; the person reported 56 kg and 163 cm. The two measured values were evidently entered in each other's columns, and the worked examples that accompany Fox's regression textbook swap them back before analysing the data (UCLA Statistical Methods and Data Analytics). With row 12 included, the measured-data exponent is −0.495, a negative relationship between height and weight, and the self-reports, at 1.864, are far closer to the truth than the "gold standard". The correlation between measured and reported weight among women falls from 0.956 to 0.501 when that single row is included.

![Figure 2](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/measurement-error/figures/fig2_davis_validation.png)

*Figure 2. Left: self-report error (reported minus measured) for weight and height in carData::Davis, by sex; row 12 (not plotted) lies far off both scales. Right: estimates of the height exponent in log weight = a + b log height + sex when only a random 25 per cent of participants keep their measured values; median and 5–95 per cent range over 2,000 random validation subsets with row 12 removed, and, with row 12 kept, over the 1,523 subsets that did not include it and the 477 that did. The dashed line is the estimate on all measured values with row 12 removed.*

To see how a validation design behaves on these data, the analysis was rerun 2,000 times with only a random quarter of the participants keeping their measured values, as if the study had measured only a subsample. Three estimators use the validation subset. Fitting the regression on the validated quarter alone is unbiased but imprecise: its median is 2.081 with a 5–95 per cent range from 1.40 to 2.89. Regression calibration predicts each person's measured height from their reported height and sex, using the validated rows, and fits the outcome model on the calibrated heights; its median is 2.084 with a range from 1.87 to 2.36, and its root mean squared error around the full measured-data estimate is 0.149, against 0.222 for the uncorrected self-reports and 0.453 for the validated rows alone. Multiple imputation of both measured values from both reports and sex is nearly as accurate in the median (2.062) but noisier (root mean squared error 0.238), because with 45 validated people the imputation model is itself uncertain.

The right panel of Figure 2 shows what row 12 does to those designs. In 477 of the 2,000 subsets the random quarter included it. In those subsets the validated-rows estimate has a median of −0.87, regression calibration −0.56 and multiple imputation −0.88: every method that trusts the validation data is wrecked, while the uncorrected self-reports, which never see the bad measured values, stay at 1.864. When the row is in the data but not in the validation subset, the corrections work as well as on the clean data, because the calibration model is fitted only on validated rows.

Two lessons follow. A validation sample measures the error structure, and that is its great advantage over replicates, which assume a structure. But it is not error-free by definition: the gold standard is another recording, made by people and typed into the same spreadsheet. A validation subsample deserves the same outlier screening as any other data, ideally with a robust fit and an outlier map, and a correction should never be applied before its calibration model has been looked at. The second lesson is about scale. With correlations around 0.95 between reports and measurements (a reliability of about 0.9 for reported height) and a sample of 180, the self-report bias is about 0.22 on an estimate with a standard error of 0.26. A good correction pays for itself, but only modestly; with less reliable measurements or a larger sample, where bias dominates the error, the case for correcting becomes overwhelming.

## Two noisy measures of ability and the return to schooling

The classic problem of measurement error in economics is the return to schooling. People with more schooling earn more, but they also tend to have more of whatever is called ability, so a regression of log earnings on years of schooling attributes some of the effect of ability to schooling (Griliches, 1977). Adding a test score as a control helps only as far as the test measures the relevant ability, and the residual confounding that remains is a measurement-error problem in exactly the sense of the second line of the formula above.

The wage2 data distributed with Wooldridge's econometrics textbook (Wooldridge, 2025) come from Blackburn and Neumark (1992): 935 men from the National Longitudinal Survey with monthly earnings, years of schooling, experience, tenure, marital status, region, urban residence and race, and two test scores, IQ and the Knowledge of the World of Work test (KWW). The two scores correlate at 0.414. If both measure the same ability with independent errors, either can serve as an instrument for the other.

A regression of log earnings on schooling and the controls gives a return of 0.0654 per year (standard error 0.0064), 6.5 per cent. Adding IQ lowers it to 0.0544, adding KWW to 0.0576, adding both to 0.0498. Each test absorbs some of the confounding. Instrumenting IQ with KWW, so that only the part of IQ shared with KWW counts as ability, gives a return to schooling of 0.0250 (standard error 0.0188) and an IQ coefficient of 0.0130, more than three times the 0.0036 of the uninstrumented regression. Instrumenting KWW with IQ gives 0.0175 (standard error 0.0161). The first stages are strong, with t-statistics of 6.4 and 6.8, so the instruments are not weak.

![Figure 3](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/measurement-error/figures/fig3_wage_reliability.png)

*Figure 3. Return to a year of schooling in wooldridge::wage2 (935 men; controls: experience, tenure, marital status, region, urban residence, race), with IQ as the ability control corrected for an assumed reliability of IQ (corrected least squares), against the two instrumental-variable estimates and the regression without any ability measure. The grey band (0.37–0.39) marks reliabilities just above the R² of IQ on the other regressors, where the correction diverges and is not drawn; at or below 0.37 it is undefined.*

Figure 3 puts those numbers on one axis. The blue curve is the return to schooling after correcting the IQ coefficient for an assumed reliability of IQ, from 1 (no correction, 0.0544) downward. At a reliability of 0.9 the correction is small (0.0523); at 0.8 the return is 0.0493, at 0.7 0.0445, at 0.6 0.0355, and at 0.5 it has fallen to 0.0129. The instrumental-variable estimate of 0.0250 corresponds to a reliability of 0.54, and at that reliability the corrected IQ coefficient (0.0130) also matches the IV estimate of the IQ coefficient: the two methods tell one story. The curve cannot be extended below 0.37, the R² of IQ on schooling and the controls, because there the assumed error variance would exceed the variation in IQ that the other regressors leave unexplained. The data set a lower bound on how unreliable IQ can be assumed to be.

The decisive question is which reliability is believable, and here the measurement-error framework forces a distinction that is easy to blur. Test–retest correlations of full-scale IQ over a few weeks are around 0.9 (0.89 over a mean interval of 32 days for the WISC-IV; Williams, Weiss and Rolfhus, 2003), and the five-year stability of general ability in young adults is about 0.8 (Breit, Scherrer, Tucker-Drob and Preckel, 2024). A reliability of 0.54 is far below what any psychometrician would accept for IQ as a measure of IQ. But the error that matters for the wage regression is not the test–retest error of the test; it is the gap between what the test measures and the confounder, the wage-relevant ability that also drives schooling. That gap is not measurement error in the psychometric sense, and no test manual reports it. The instrumental-variable estimate claims it is large. It does so under the assumption that KWW affects earnings only through the shared ability, which is doubtful for a test of knowledge about jobs that is itself plausibly a product of schooling and work. The two IV estimates differ by 0.0075, with a bootstrap standard error of 0.0191, so the data cannot detect a violation, but they cannot rule one out either.

The practical result is that the return to schooling in these data is anywhere between about 2 and 5.4 per cent, depending on an assumption about measurement that the data cannot settle, and Figure 3 is the honest way to report it: a sensitivity curve with the assumption on the horizontal axis, rather than a single corrected number. The same logic applies to any adjustment for a proxy confounder in observational data science, whether a credit score standing in for financial behaviour or a segment label standing in for customer intent. Ashenfelter and Krueger (1994) took the replicate route instead, asking identical twins to report each other's schooling as well as their own, so that two independent reports of the same quantity were available. Comparing twins removes the shared family background and genes, but it also removes most of the true variation in schooling while keeping the reporting error, so the within-twin differences are far less reliable than the levels. Their correction raised the estimated return to 16 per cent a year: error in the variable of interest and error in a confounder push the estimate in opposite directions.

## Survey scales: reliability from the items themselves

Survey scales are the everyday case of measurement error in data science. Satisfaction, engagement, trust and personality are measured by averaging several items, and the items supply their own estimate of reliability: their internal consistency. Cronbach's (1951) alpha estimates the reliability of the average if the items are parallel; McDonald's omega, from a one-factor model, relaxes that to items that load differently on the same factor (McDonald, 1999).

The bfi data in Revelle's psych package (Revelle, 2026) contain 25 personality items, five for each of the Big Five traits, answered on a six-point scale by 2,800 participants of the SAPA web project, along with age, gender and education. For the 2,236 respondents complete on the 25 items, education, age and gender, the five scale scores have alphas of 0.705 (agreeableness), 0.732 (conscientiousness), 0.769 (extraversion), 0.816 (neuroticism) and 0.612 (openness); the one-factor omegas are within 0.01 of each. Openness, the least reliable scale, loses almost 40 per cent of its variance to noise.

The analysis is the kind of "driver" regression that data scientists run on survey data every week: education level, from 1 (high school, not finished) to 5 (a graduate degree), on the five scale scores, age and gender, with each coefficient expressed per standard deviation of its scale. Correcting for unreliability means the multivariate version of corrected least squares, with each scale's error variance set to one minus its alpha times its variance and the errors of different scales assumed independent.

![Figure 4](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/measurement-error/figures/fig4_bfi_drivers.png)

*Figure 4. Coefficients of education level (1–5) on the five Big Five scale scores, age and gender in psych::bfi (2,236 complete respondents), per standard deviation of each observed scale, as measured and corrected for unreliability with Cronbach's alpha; 95 per cent bootstrap intervals from 1,000 resamples, with alpha re-estimated in each resample.*

Figure 4 shows the coefficients before and after the correction. Openness is the only scale clearly associated with education, at 0.118 education levels per standard deviation (95 per cent interval 0.074 to 0.164). Corrected for its reliability of 0.61 it becomes 0.213 (0.134 to 0.299), 1.81 times larger, more than the factor of 1/0.612 = 1.63 that the univariate formula would suggest, because the correction also undoes the leakage between correlated scales. The other four coefficients are small and their intervals include zero before and after the correction, but the correction multiplies them by factors between 1.67 and 2.81, and the ranking among them changes: agreeableness moves from fifth to third. Corrected for unreliability, the correlation between agreeableness and extraversion is 0.645 rather than the observed 0.475, which is why their coefficients move so much when the noise is removed from both.

Two features of this case generalise. The ranking of drivers by their naive coefficients partly ranks them by how reliably they are measured: a well-measured weak driver can outrank a poorly measured strong one, and in a driver analysis that informs where to invest, that is a decision error built into the measurement. And the correction widens the intervals roughly in proportion, by factors of 1.3 to 1.9. The corrected coefficient for extraversion, −0.084, is 2.5 times its naive value, and its interval, −0.187 to +0.010, is nearly twice as wide and still includes zero; the correction did not create evidence, it rescaled it. Alpha is a lower bound on reliability when items are not parallel, so the alpha correction, if anything, overcorrects, and the near-identical omega results suggest that this matters little here. What matters more is the assumption that errors are independent across scales: response styles such as acquiescence create errors shared across items of different traits, and a correction that ignores them is incomplete.

## A stress test: nine corrections against six kinds of error

The real cases each show one method under one kind of error, with an unknown truth. A simulation can put every method against every kind of error with the truth known. To keep the covariates realistic, it is built on NHANES. The population is the 10,075 adults with a blood-pressure average, age, sex and BMI; the true exposure is NHANES's systolic average in units of 10 mmHg, and the outcome is generated from a known model, linear in one version and logistic in another:

```
linear:    y = 2 + 0.5 x + 0.02 age − 0.3 female + 0.05 BMI + N(0, 1)
logistic:  logit P(y = 1) = −7.45 + 0.3 x + 0.03 age − 0.2 female + 0.03 BMI     (prevalence about 20%)
```

Each of 300 replications per scenario draws 1,000 people and gives every one two error-prone readings of x; a random 15 per cent also have x itself recorded, an internal validation subsample of about 150 people. The error variance is set by the reliability of one reading in the population, 0.9, 0.7 or 0.5. Because age, sex and BMI explain 19.7 per cent of the variance of blood pressure, the conditional reliabilities that govern the bias are lower: 0.878, 0.652 and 0.445.

Six error structures are compared. Classical error is independent noise. Correlated replicate errors share half their variance between the two readings, as readings taken minutes apart do. Heteroscedastic error has a standard deviation proportional to the true value. Berkson error reverses the relationship: the readings are an assigned value and the truth scatters around it. Systematic error flattens the readings toward the mean, w = m + 0.8 (x − m) + u, the pattern of the women's weight reports in the Davis data, exaggerated. In differential error, half of the error variance is shared by the two readings and correlated (0.3) with the outcome's own noise. Nine estimators are fitted to every sample in the linear scenarios (eight in the logistic ones, without two-stage least squares): least squares on one reading and on the mean of two; regression calibration from the replicates; SIMEX with the quadratic and with the rational extrapolant, using the error variance estimated from the replicates; two-stage least squares with the second reading as the instrument; regression calibration and multiple imputation (20 imputations) from the validation subsample; and least squares on the validated rows alone. Standard errors come from 100 bootstrap resamples for regression calibration, 50 for SIMEX (in the linear scenarios only), Rubin's rules for multiple imputation and the usual formulas otherwise. The Monte Carlo standard error of a coverage near 95 per cent is 1.3 percentage points.

### Classical error: the corrections work, and one extrapolant does not

The theory of the first section predicts the naive results almost exactly. With one reading, the exposure coefficient is biased by −12.2, −34.5 and −55.8 per cent at the three reliabilities, against −12.2, −34.8 and −55.5 predicted from the conditional reliabilities. The age coefficient, which has no measurement error of its own, is biased by +12.5, +36.7 and +59.4 per cent, against +12.9, +36.9 and +58.7 predicted from the leakage formula. Figure 5 shows the two side by side: with this simulation's coefficients, the bias that measurement error moves into a correlated covariate is as large, in relative terms, as the attenuation it causes. Averaging the two readings halves the error variance, which cuts the bias by about half at a reliability of 0.9 but only by about a third at 0.5 (−6.4, −20.8 and −38.7 per cent). The one-reading confidence intervals contain the true coefficient in 11.7 per cent of samples at a reliability of 0.9 and in none at 0.7 or 0.5.

![Figure 5](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/measurement-error/figures/fig5_leakage.png)

*Figure 5. Classical error, linear outcome: relative bias of the exposure coefficient (left) and of the age coefficient (right), which is measured without error, by the reliability of one reading, for the naive estimates on one reading and on the mean of two, regression calibration from the replicates and multiple imputation from the validation subsample; 300 samples of 1,000 per point.*

Every correction that matches the error structure removes the bias. Regression calibration from the replicates is biased by +0.2, +0.5 and +0.1 per cent, with coverage of 95.0, 94.3 and 95.3 per cent; two-stage least squares by +0.4, +0.3 and 0.0 per cent; regression calibration and multiple imputation from the validation subsample by at most 1.1 per cent in every cell; and they also remove the leakage into the age coefficient, to within about 2 per cent. The correction has a cost in variance, but at these reliabilities it is small next to the bias it removes: at a reliability of 0.7, the root mean squared error of the exposure coefficient is 0.174 for one reading, 0.026 for regression calibration from the replicates, 0.030 for two-stage least squares, 0.029 for rational SIMEX, 0.031 and 0.033 for the two validation-based corrections, and 0.055 for the validated rows alone. Using the validated rows alone is unbiased, but it throws away 85 per cent of the data, and the corrections that combine the two kinds of information have root mean squared errors about 1.7 to 1.8 times smaller.

![Figure 6](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/measurement-error/figures/fig6_simulation_bias.png)

*Figure 6. Relative bias of the exposure coefficient (mean over 300 samples, with ±1.96 standard deviations of the estimates across samples) for nine estimators under six error structures, linear outcome, reliability 0.7. The numbers at the right of each panel are the coverage of nominal 95 per cent intervals; values more than four points from 95 are in orange.*

The exception is SIMEX with the quadratic extrapolant, the default in R's simex package (Lederer and Küchenhoff, 2006). It is unbiased at a reliability of 0.9, biased by −3.0 per cent at 0.7 (coverage 87.3 per cent) and by −14.6 per cent at 0.5 (coverage 22.7 per cent). Figure 7 shows why, on one sample with a reliability of 0.5 in which SIMEX is applied to a single reading with a known error variance, a harder case than the simulation's mean of two readings. The SIMEX path, the naive coefficient as more and more noise is added, is a convex curve that flattens as the noise grows; a quadratic fitted to it cannot bend upward fast enough on the way back to the point of no error, and extrapolates to 0.329 where the truth is 0.5. The rational extrapolant, which has the exact form of the path in linear regression with classical error (Cook and Stefanski, 1994), extrapolates to 0.531 in this sample and is unbiased on average (+0.3 per cent). The rational fit is numerically fragile, as the logistic results below show, which is a good reason for a cautious default; but in linear models with substantial error, the quadratic under-corrects by a predictable amount.

![Figure 7](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/measurement-error/figures/fig7_simex.png)

*Figure 7. SIMEX on one simulated sample (n = 1,000, reliability 0.5, one reading): the naive slope after adding extra measurement error with variance λ times the known error variance, averaged over 200 draws per λ, with the quadratic and rational extrapolants and their values at λ = −1.*

Heteroscedastic error, with a standard deviation proportional to the true value, behaves like classical error for every estimator: the corrections use only the average error variance, and in linear regression that is enough. Berkson error behaves as theory says it should. The naive estimates are unbiased (within 0.5 per cent), and the replicate-based corrections leave them alone, because two readings of an assigned value agree perfectly and the estimated error variance is zero. The danger with Berkson error is the reverse case: an error variance taken from somewhere else and plugged into a correction would overcorrect an estimate that needed nothing.

### When the assumption behind the correction fails

The middle panel of the top row and the last two panels of the bottom row of Figure 6 are where the corrections break, and each failure has the shape of one of the real cases.

When the two readings share half of their error, as within-session readings do, the replicates see only the half that differs. At a reliability of 0.7, regression calibration from the replicates is biased by −21.2 per cent, rational SIMEX by −21.1 and two-stage least squares by −21.4, against −34.9 per cent for one reading: they remove about 40 per cent of the bias and report the result with confidence intervals that almost never cover the truth (in at most 1 of 300 samples). The validation-based corrections are unbiased (+0.3 and −0.5 per cent), because the validation subsample compares readings with the truth rather than with each other. This is the NHANES situation, made explicit.

When the readings are flattened toward the mean, the replicate-based corrections replace one bias with another. The flattening multiplies the naive coefficient by 1/0.8 = 1.25 while the noise attenuates it; at a reliability of 0.9 the two nearly cancel, and the single-reading estimate is biased by only +2.5 per cent. The replicate-based corrections remove the attenuation and keep the inflation: regression calibration, rational SIMEX and two-stage least squares are biased by +25 to +28 per cent at every reliability, with coverage between 0.3 and 1.3 per cent at a reliability of 0.9. (The quadratic SIMEX agrees at 0.9 and looks better at lower reliabilities only because its own under-correction offsets part of the inflation.) At a reliability of 0.9 that is far worse than doing nothing; at 0.7 and 0.5 the inflation is smaller than the attenuation it replaces, so the corrections beat a single reading, though at 0.7 they still lose to the plain mean of two readings (−11.8 per cent). Two readings that share the same systematic distortion agree with each other perfectly about it. Only the validation subsample, which compares the readings with the truth, sees the flattening, and regression calibration and multiple imputation from it are unbiased (within 1 per cent).

When the error is tied to the outcome, the two validation methods part company. Regression calibration predicts the true value from the readings and the covariates but not from the outcome, so its calibrated values carry the differential error into the outcome model, and it overcorrects: +8.7, +15.5 and +23.4 per cent at the three reliabilities, with coverage falling to 49 per cent. Multiple imputation includes the outcome in the imputation model, as it must (Moons, Donders, Stijnen and Harrell, 2006), and is unbiased (within 1.1 per cent) with coverage between 92 and 94 per cent. The replicate-based corrections remove part of the bias and keep the rest (−5.5 per cent at a reliability of 0.7).

### Logistic regression: no correction is exact

With a binary outcome and classical error, the naive coefficient is attenuated by 9.5, 28.6 and 49.1 per cent at the three reliabilities, slightly less than in the linear case. Regression calibration, which is exact for linear models, is an approximation here (Rosner, Spiegelman and Willett, 1990), and it overcorrects as the error grows: +1.6, +7.3 and +10.5 per cent from the replicates, +1.2, +6.7 and +8.4 per cent from the validation subsample. The quadratic SIMEX is close at reliabilities of 0.9 and 0.7 (+0.4 and +0.6 per cent) and under-corrects at 0.5 (−8.4 per cent). The rational extrapolant shows its fragility: it diverged in 2 of 300 samples at a reliability of 0.9 and in 6 at 0.5, producing coefficients as large as 92 where the truth is 0.3, though its median bias is at most 5 per cent. Multiple imputation is biased upward by 9.8 and 13.8 per cent at the two lower reliabilities, part of which is the small-sample bias of logistic regression fitted to the 150 validated people, the same bias that makes the validated rows alone overestimate the coefficient by 3 to 10 per cent. In a logistic model, a corrected coefficient is far less biased than the naive one, and more accurate once the error is substantial, but its last few per cent of bias depend on which approximation was used.

## Prediction: when the uncorrected model is the right one

Everything so far has been about coefficients, and a data scientist building a predictive model might reasonably ask whether any of it applies. Usually it does not, and the reason is worth stating precisely. A regression of the outcome on the noisy reading estimates the conditional expectation of the outcome given the reading, E[y | w, z]. A corrected regression estimates E[y | x, z], the expectation given the true value. A model that will predict from readings made the same way as the training readings needs the first, not the second.

The simulation tested this directly. For every sample in the linear scenarios, the naive model (fitted on one reading) and the corrected model (regression calibration from the replicates) were used to predict the outcome for 1,000 people drawn afresh from the same population, with new errors and new outcome noise, first from one new reading per person, as in training, and then from the exact values, as if the model had been deployed with a much better instrument. Figure 8 shows the calibration slope, the slope of the outcome on the prediction (1 means the predictions are neither too spread out nor too compressed), and the mean squared prediction error.

![Figure 8](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/measurement-error/figures/fig8_prediction.png)

*Figure 8. Classical error, linear outcome: calibration slope (left) and mean squared error (right) of predictions on 1,000 new people, for the naive and the corrected model, deployed with one reading per person or with the exact values; mean over 300 samples per point.*

Deployed on one reading, the naive model is perfectly calibrated at every reliability (slopes of 1.000, 0.997 and 0.997) and the corrected model is not: its calibration slopes are 0.940, 0.806 and 0.643, meaning its predictions are too spread out, and its mean squared error is higher than the naive model's at every reliability (1.340 against 1.222 at a reliability of 0.7, 1.789 against 1.355 at 0.5). The correction multiplies the coefficient by about 1/λ, and applying that coefficient to a reading that still contains the noise amplifies the noise. Deployed on exact values, the roles reverse. The naive model's predictions are too compressed (calibration slopes of 1.052, 1.130 and 1.165) and the corrected model is calibrated (0.998, 0.995 and 0.996) and the most accurate of all four combinations, with a mean squared error within about 1 per cent of the irreducible noise.

The rule is to correct the coefficients for the measurement the model will be deployed on. When training and deployment measure the same way, that means no correction. When they do not (a model trained on survey self-reports and deployed on administrative records, or on readings from one generation of sensor and deployed on the next, or on clinic measurements and deployed on home monitors), the naive model is miscalibrated in a predictable direction, and the fix is to recalibrate to the deployment measurement, which is a measurement-error correction with the deployment reliability in place of 1. A change in measurement quality is a distribution shift that standard drift monitoring may not see, because the mean and variance of a feature can stay almost the same while its reliability changes.

The same logic explains why measurement error matters for model interpretation even when it does not matter for prediction. Coefficients, partial dependence and feature importances all describe E[y | w, z], so a noisily measured feature looks less important than a cleanly measured one with the same real effect, and its importance leaks to whatever correlated features are measured better. The bfi driver analysis is the regression version of this, and the same distortion afflicts permutation importances and SHAP values computed on noisy inputs.

## Doing it in R and Python

R has most of the specialised tools. The simex package (Lederer and Küchenhoff, 2006) wraps any fitted model whose noisy covariate has a known error standard deviation:

```r
library(simex)
naive <- lm(y ~ w + age + female + bmi, data = d, x = TRUE)
fit <- simex(naive, SIMEXvariable = "w", measurement.error = sd_u,
             fitting.method = "nonlinear", B = 200)    # the default is "quadratic"
summary(fit)
```

The current version, 1.8, uses the quadratic extrapolant unless told otherwise, with λ = 0.5, 1, 1.5 and 2 and 100 simulations per λ; the simulation above shows why `"nonlinear"` is worth requesting in linear models with substantial error, and worth checking carefully in nonlinear ones. Its standard errors can use the jackknife variance estimator of Stefanski and Cook (1995), and its `mcsimex` does the same for misclassified categorical variables. The mecor package (Nab, van Smeden, Keogh and Groenwold, 2021) implements regression calibration and related corrections for linear models with replicate, internal-validation and external-validation designs. For instruments, `ivreg` in the AER or ivreg packages and `feols` in fixest fit two-stage least squares; for multi-item scales, lavaan fits the latent-variable regression directly instead of correcting sum scores; and mice can impute a mismeasured covariate from a validation subsample as if the true value were missing, which is the multiple-imputation approach used here. The STRATOS guidance papers (Keogh et al., 2020; Shaw et al., 2020) are the best current overview of which method to use with which design.

Python has the building blocks but no maintained measurement-error library: a search of the Python Package Index finds no SIMEX or regression-calibration package, and the one named simex is an unrelated text-matching tool. Two-stage least squares is in linearmodels, with a formula syntax that marks the endogenous variable and its instrument:

```python
from linearmodels.iv import IV2SLS
iv = IV2SLS.from_formula(
    "lwage ~ 1 + educ + exper + tenure + married + south + urban + black + [IQ ~ KWW]",
    data=wage2).fit(cov_type="robust")
```

Regression calibration with replicates is short enough to write by hand, and in linear regression it is the whole correction:

```python
import numpy as np
W = df[["w1", "w2"]].to_numpy()                 # two replicate readings
k = W.shape[1]
s2_u = W.var(axis=1, ddof=1).mean()             # within-person error variance
V = np.column_stack([W.mean(axis=1), df[["age", "female", "bmi"]]])
S = np.cov(V, rowvar=False)
c = S[0].copy(); c[0] -= s2_u / k               # cov(x, [wbar, Z])
x_hat = V.mean(0)[0] + (V - V.mean(0)) @ np.linalg.solve(S, c)
# then fit the outcome model on x_hat and Z, and bootstrap the two steps together for standard errors
```

The standard errors are the part that is easy to get wrong. A naive standard error computed after plugging in the calibrated values ignores the uncertainty in the error variance and in the calibration model; bootstrapping the whole procedure, as the simulation and the bfi analysis here do, includes both. The from-scratch estimators used in this article (corrected least squares, regression calibration with replicates or validation data, SIMEX with quadratic and rational extrapolants, two-stage least squares and multiple imputation) are in `me.py` in the repository, with checks of their large-sample behaviour.

## A working order for regressions on measured data

The cases and the simulation suggest an order of work for any regression whose covariates are measurements rather than facts.

Name the quantity each important column is supposed to measure, and how it was measured. "Systolic blood pressure" is not a quantity; "the first of three readings in one session" and "the average blood pressure over a year" are, and they have different relationships to an outcome. The same goes for ability and an IQ score, for satisfaction and a five-item average, and for income and a survey answer.

Look for outside information before fitting anything: replicate measurements, a subsample measured properly, a second measurement that could serve as an instrument, a reliability coefficient for the instrument used. If none exists, plan a sensitivity analysis over plausible reliabilities, as in Figure 3, rather than reporting the naive estimate as if the question did not arise.

Use the auxiliary data to check the error structure before using it to correct. Do the replicate errors look independent, or do they share a session, a device or a rater? Is the error related to the true value, as women's weight reports were, or to another covariate, as the first blood-pressure reading was, or to the outcome? Each answer rules some corrections in and others out.

Screen the auxiliary data like any other data. A validation subsample with a typing error in it, like row 12, does more damage than no validation subsample at all.

Choose the estimator by the information available. With independent replicates, regression calibration, or SIMEX with the rational extrapolant when the error is large; the second replicate as an instrument when the error variance is uncertain. With a validation subsample, regression calibration with the outcome kept out of the calibration model, or multiple imputation with the outcome kept in, which also covers differential error. With only a published reliability, corrected least squares or SIMEX, reported as one point on a sensitivity curve.

Correct the confounders as well as the exposure. The bias that measurement error leaks into correlated covariates can be as large as the attenuation of the noisy covariate itself, as it was in the simulation, and in a causal analysis it is residual confounding.

Report the uncorrected and the corrected estimates side by side, with standard errors from a bootstrap of the whole procedure, error-variance estimation included.

For prediction, do not correct the coefficients unless the measurement will change between training and deployment. If it will, calibrate the model to the measurement it will be deployed on, which may mean collecting a small sample measured the new way.

## What correction can and cannot do

Measurement error is a data challenge that no amount of data solves on its own. A larger sample makes an attenuated coefficient more precisely wrong; a more flexible model fits the noisy relationship more faithfully. What fixes it is information about the measurement itself, and the four kinds of that information each fixed a different problem in the cases above. Replicates measured within-session noise in NHANES and showed that it barely matters, while also revealing a systematic first-reading effect that biases an age gradient by 11.5 per cent and that no error-variance correction could touch. A validation sample measured the self-report error in the Davis data, non-classical for women's weight, and brought the median estimate of the height exponent from 1.86 to within 0.003 of the measured-data value, except when the validation subsample itself contained the worst recording error in the data set. Two test scores served as instruments for each other in the wage data and cut the return to schooling from 5.4 to 2.5 per cent, but only under an assumption about what the tests measure that the data could not check. And internal consistency corrected a survey driver analysis, nearly doubling the one clear effect and reordering the noise.

The simulation turned those cases into rules. Every correction works when its assumption holds: regression calibration, rational SIMEX, two-stage least squares and the validation-based methods all removed a 35 per cent attenuation and the matching 37 per cent leakage into a correlated covariate, with honest confidence intervals. Each fails in a specific way when its assumption is wrong. Replicates that share error under-correct; replicates that share a systematic distortion replace the attenuation with an inflation of a quarter, which at high reliability is worse than doing nothing; regression calibration without the outcome in the calibration model overcorrects differential error; and the quadratic SIMEX default under-corrects whenever the error is large. Logistic models add a layer of approximation to every method. And for prediction from the same measurement, the uncorrected model is the right one.

Three conjectures follow, offered as conjectures. The first is that in observational analyses in industry, such as marketing attribution, churn drivers or the evaluation of a programme from administrative data, residual confounding from mismeasured confounders is a larger source of bias than the choice among the estimators those analyses usually debate (regression, matching, weighting or machine-learning adjustment), because every one of those estimators adjusts for the proxy as recorded, and the leakage formula applies to all of them. The second is that driver analyses and feature-importance rankings built on survey scales systematically under-rank the constructs measured with the fewest or least consistent items, so that investment follows measurement quality as much as real effects. The third is that many corrections reported as complete are partial in the way the NHANES readings are: replicates taken close together in time, from the same device or by the same rater correct the error that varies between them and leave the shared error in place, and in sensor and wearable data, where repeated readings within a day are cheap and readings across months are not, the long-run attenuation of effects is larger than the corrections suggest. Each could be tested with data that contain both kinds of replicate.
