\section{Conclusions}\label{sec:conclusions}

\paragraph{Association.} In the \eu\ from 2009 to 2026, the negative association between
quarterly GDP growth and the change in the unemployment rate is a widespread descriptive
regularity outside the pandemic quarters. How many individual country sums are
established once the number of countries tested is taken into account depends on the
error rate controlled: from \val{hi_negative_holm} to \val{nr_bh_reg}. Its size depends on
the model. Beyond the common
European cycle, the average country sum lies between \val{by_mu_lo} and \val{by_mu_hi}
across accepted hierarchical specifications. The mean long-run sum, a different quantity,
is \val{dyn_mu} in the specification that reproduces the persistence of unemployment
changes.

\paragraph{Heterogeneity.} Some country differences are credible in every accepted
specification and persist under other measures of labour input; Spain against the
Netherlands and against Luxembourg are the clearest cases. These are pairwise statements;
no ranking of countries is established.

\paragraph{Prediction.} The association adds little to forecasts of unemployment. Outside
the pandemic, pooling, GDP lags and the common factor gain a few per cent. Nothing
reliably beats assuming that the unemployment rate will not change.

\paragraph{Causation.} The identification strategies available with public data are
uninformative; they neither establish nor rule out a causal effect of any size.

\paragraph{The earlier claims.} Spain's large association is supported. The other two are
qualified. The Dutch delayed response meets its registered rule in the primary fit but not
in every accepted specification. Luxembourg's association is weak and sign-unstable in the
classical estimates. The registered Bayesian rule for a weak association is met in none of
the registered fits. It is met on the long-run sum of the dynamic amendment only through
a lagged-change coefficient that survey noise produces, an artefact. The commuter
explanation for Luxembourg is not supported: domestic and resident employment barely move
with GDP. The large financial sector does not explain why output per hour moves almost
one for one with output there, since the link stays weak outside finance. Transitory noise
in quarterly output is not established: one of its fingerprints points towards it before
adjustment, the other away. Luxembourg's weak link remains unexplained.

\paragraph{The general lesson.} An Okun coefficient estimated from aggregate data is a
summary of how output and unemployment moved together over a particular period. It is not
a structural parameter of a country's labour market.
