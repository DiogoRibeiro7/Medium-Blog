\section{Classical estimates}\label{sec:classical}

\subsection{Method}

For each country, equation~\eqref{eq:okun} is estimated by ordinary least squares. Lag
orders are $K\in\{0,1,2,4\}$, with and without the change in the participation rate as a
control. The headline specification is $K=2$ without participation.

\paragraph{Inference.} Standard errors use the Newey--West covariance with a Bartlett
kernel and four lags in calendar quarters \citep{newey1987hac}. The variance of
$\hat\theta_i$ uses the full covariance matrix of the lag coefficients. The classical
intervals use normal critical values, an approximation with \val{cl_n_quarters} quarters
per country. A registered study (below) replaces that reference with null-imposed sieve and
moving-block bootstraps of the same HAC-studentised statistic, which follow its
small-sample behaviour, including the fixed-$b$ effect of the bandwidth.

\paragraph{Samples.} The full period, 2009--2026; the same period without any observation
whose window touches 2020--2021; 2009--2019; 2022 onwards; and a flag-screened sample. The
research plan listed a pandemic-excluded comparison before any estimate. Making it the
headline sample was decided after the full-sample estimates had been seen.

\paragraph{Review.} A separate review recomputed every quantity of the first pass by an
independent numerical route and re-estimated \val{cl_n_core_published} core
specifications per country. It classified each country as robustly negative, negative
with specification-dependent significance, or sign-unstable.

\paragraph{The participation control.} Half of those core specifications control for the
change in participation. That variable is itself a response to output, and it is
mechanically linked to the outcome, because the labour force is the denominator of the
unemployment rate. Those specifications therefore estimate a different, partly mediated
quantity. A registered re-classification (\texttt{CORE\_GRID\_PROTOCOL.md}) applies the
same rule to the \val{cl_n_core} specifications without the control. Its verdicts are the
ones used here; only \val{cl_changed} changes.

\subsection{Results}

\paragraph{The association.} With 2020--2021 excluded, the two-lag sum is negative in
\val{hi_point_negative} of \val{n_countries} countries. With normal critical values, its
95\% interval lies below zero in \val{cl_n_neg_excluded} (table~\ref{tab:countries}), and
in \val{cl_n_neg_holm} after a Holm adjustment over the \val{n_countries} countries. In
\val{cl_n_robust} countries every core specification without the participation control
agrees in sign and excludes zero (\val{cl_n_robust_published} with the control included),
again with normal intervals.
These are associations conditional on contemporaneous and lagged GDP growth, and
contemporaneous growth is not exogenous.

\paragraph{Small-sample inference.} The registered study reaches a more cautious
conclusion:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the normal reference over-rejects: under each country's fitted null it rejects
    \val{hi_size_normal} of the time at a nominal 5\%, and too often in
    \val{hi_over_rejecting} of \val{n_countries} countries, while the sieve bootstrap
    rejects \val{hi_size_boot};
  \item under both bootstraps, \val{hi_negative} sums are negative at the 5\% level;
    \val{hi_lost} no longer are;
  \item after a Holm adjustment over the countries, \val{hi_negative_holm} remains under
    both schemes, against \val{cl_n_neg_holm} with the normal reference.
\end{itemize}
The registered null-imposed bootstrap understates the evidence when a sum is large. A
diagnostic added after the run shows why: imposing $\theta_i=0$ moves the omitted,
persistent GDP effect into the residuals and raises their first-order autocorrelation
(median increase \val{hi_rho_gap} before 2020). The size study draws its null from the
same residuals, so its rejection rates describe that more persistent null. The
bootstrap-t intervals, which use the unrestricted residuals, lie below zero in
\val{hi_boot_below} countries.

\paragraph{Two post-hoc checks.} An external review prompted two checks after the
registered run (\texttt{docs/NULL\_RESIDUALS.md}). Neither is registered.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{Errors from the unrestricted residuals.} The null is still imposed on the
    mean, but the error process is taken from the unrestricted residuals.
    \begin{itemize}
      \item On this less persistent null, the normal reference rejects
        \val{nr_size_normal_unres} of the time instead of \val{hi_size_normal}, still too
        often.
      \item The registered bootstrap holds its size there too
        (\val{nr_size_boot_reg_unres}).
      \item A bootstrap that draws its errors from the unrestricted residuals finds
        \val{nr_holm_unres} sums after Holm. It over-rejects
        (\val{nr_size_boot_unres}), however, so it does not replace the registered count.
    \end{itemize}
  \item \emph{The error rate controlled.} Requiring both schemes is restrictive: with
    either scheme alone, Holm leaves \val{nr_holm_sieve_reg}. Holm also controls the
    chance of any false rejection, whereas ``how many countries'' is a question about the
    false discovery rate.
    \begin{itemize}
      \item Benjamini--Hochberg on the registered $p$-values leaves \val{nr_bh_reg} sums
        under both schemes. Its guarantee assumes independent, or positively dependent
        one-sided, tests.
      \item The Benjamini--Yekutieli adjustment, valid under any dependence, leaves
        \val{nr_by_reg}.
    \end{itemize}
\end{itemize}
Taken together, the sign of the association is widespread across countries. How many
individual country sums are established depends on the error rate controlled and on the
dependence assumed: from \val{hi_negative_holm} to \val{nr_bh_reg}.

\paragraph{Definition breaks.} A registered sensitivity
(\texttt{MEASUREMENT\_PROTOCOL.md}) repeats the headline inference under the study's
definition-break rule (section~\ref{sec:data}). The rule drops every post-2021 change for
Spain and France, and a few break quarters elsewhere.
\begin{itemize}
  \item \val{ms_rule_bootstrap} sums are negative under both bootstraps, against
    \val{hi_negative}.
  \item After Holm, \val{ms_rule_bootstrap_holm} are, against \val{hi_negative_holm}.
  \item Under Benjamini--Hochberg, \val{ms_rule_bootstrap_bh} are, against
    \val{nr_bh_reg}.
  \item \val{ms_rule_lost} lose significance under one bootstrap scheme, and the Holm
    count changes for \val{ms_rule_holm_changed}.
\end{itemize}
Spain's sum moves from \val{ms_ES_head} to \val{ms_ES_rule}, and France's from
\val{ms_FR_head} to \val{ms_FR_rule}. Their post-2021 changes, measured under the earlier
definition, weaken both.

\paragraph{The pandemic.} Including 2020--2021 moves the sum towards zero in
\val{cl_n_attenuated} countries (figure~\ref{fig:pandemic}). In \val{cl_n_full_outside}
countries the full-sample estimate lies outside the 95\% interval of the estimate without
the pandemic. The pandemic quarters hold the largest output movements of the sample, so
the full-sample coefficient describes those quarters, not the 2009--2019 relationship.
Job-retention schemes are the usual explanation for why unemployment did not follow
output. The registered test below does not establish it.

\paragraph{Hours.} A registered study (\texttt{HOURS\_PROTOCOL.md}) estimated Okun's law in
hours worked for the \val{hr_n_hours} countries with hours data, with the case study's
estimator (section~\ref{sec:cases}), with and without the pandemic
(figure~\ref{fig:hours}). If job retention explains the attenuation, hours should keep
following output in 2020--2021 while unemployment does not, and the adjustment should move
to hours per worker. The point estimates have that pattern, with one exception:
\begin{itemize}
  \item including the pandemic raises the mean hours elasticity from \val{hr_mean_h_excl}
    to \val{hr_mean_h_full}, a ratio of \val{hr_ret_h}. The mean unemployment elasticity
    moves from \val{hr_mean_du_excl} to \val{hr_mean_du_full}, a ratio of \val{hr_ret_du};
  \item in the \val{hr_n_persons} countries with employment in persons, hours per worker
    take up more of the adjustment (\val{hr_d_hpw}). This comes at the expense of output
    per hour (\val{hr_d_prod}), not of the number of workers (\val{hr_d_e_dom}), which the
    explanation predicted would respond less;
  \item across countries, unemployment is attenuated more where hours per worker rise
    more, but only weakly (Spearman \val{hr_p3}).
\end{itemize}
None of the four registered tests is decided. For the first three, a flaw in the
registered design made this unavoidable. A bootstrap resample without a pandemic quarter
(\val{hr_no_pandemic} of them) gives a difference of exactly zero, so the $p$-value could
not fall below \val{hr_p_floor}. An amendment registered after that result holds the
number of pandemic quarters fixed in every resample. Under it, the first two patterns are
supported (Holm $p$ \val{hr_p1_amended_holm} and \val{hr_p2a_amended_holm}), and the
change in the number of workers is not decided. Job retention would produce this pattern,
but so would paid absence, and the 2021 survey redesign falls in the same window.

\paragraph{Expansions and contractions.} A registered study
(\texttt{ASYMMETRY\_PROTOCOL.md}) asked whether unemployment responds more strongly to
falling output than to rising output, as it would if firing is quicker than hiring. It
adds the negative part of GDP growth, with the same lags, to the headline regression. In
the primary model the extra response to negative growth is common to all countries, and
each country keeps its own intercept and Okun slopes. That model uses all
\val{as_n_negative_quarters} negative-growth quarters.
\begin{itemize}
  \item Unemployment responds by \val{as_plus} per point of positive growth and by
    \val{as_minus} per point of negative growth, on average. The difference is
    \val{as_c} \val{as_c_ci} (Holm $p$ \val{as_c_holm}, \val{as_c_verdict}).
  \item The mean of the country-by-country asymmetries, over the \val{as_mg_n} countries
    with enough negative-growth quarters, is \val{as_mg} \val{as_mg_ci}
    (\val{as_mg_verdict}).
  \item In all \val{as_n_settings} sensitivity settings the pooled difference is negative,
    and in none is either test decided.
\end{itemize}
The point estimates lean towards a stronger response to contractions, but the data do
not establish it. Contractions in this sample are concentrated in 2009--2013 and in the
countries hit by the euro-area crisis, so even a decided difference could reflect
different shocks rather than a different labour-market response.

\paragraph{Common shocks.} The classical sums contain the common European cycle. A
registered study (\texttt{CCE\_PROTOCOL.md}) removes it in the standard classical way,
with the common-correlated-effects mean-group estimator of \citet{pesaran2006cce}. Each
country's regression is augmented with the cross-country averages of GDP growth, with the
same lags, and of the unemployment change.
\begin{itemize}
  \item The mean sum is \val{cce_mean} \val{cce_mean_ci} (Holm $p$ \val{cce_mean_holm},
    \val{cce_mean_verdict}): the association survives removing common shocks.
  \item It is less negative than the plain mean group, \val{cce_mg_ols}, by \val{cce_diff}
    \val{cce_diff_ci} (\val{cce_diff_verdict}). That brings it close to the mean of the
    Bayesian factor model (section~\ref{sec:bayes}).
  \item Both results hold in every one of the \val{cce_n_settings} sensitivity settings.
  \item Country by country, the CCE sums are closer to zero than OLS in \val{cce_n_closer}
    countries, and their intervals exclude zero in \val{cce_n_below} (OLS:
    \val{cce_n_below_ols}). Spain stays clearly negative (\val{cce_ES}), while the
    Netherlands moves from \val{cce_NL_ols} to \val{cce_NL}. The ranks agree with the
    Bayesian medians (Spearman \val{cce_rho_bayes}).
\end{itemize}
Like the Bayesian sum, a CCE sum measures the association beyond the common cycle. It is a
different estimand from the plain sum, and neither is causal.

\paragraph{The EU as a whole.} Every average so far weights countries equally, so Malta
counts as much as Germany. A registered study (\texttt{AGGREGATE\_PROTOCOL.md}) weights them
by their labour force. It also estimates the same regression on Eurostat's own EU
aggregate series.
\begin{itemize}
  \item The unweighted mean of the country sums is \val{ag_mg} \val{ag_mg_ci}. Weighted by
    labour force it is \val{ag_lf} \val{ag_lf_ci}; the five largest labour forces hold
    \val{ag_top_share} of the weight.
  \item The EU as a whole has a sum of \val{ag_eu} \val{ag_eu_ci}.
  \item Neither registered difference is decided. Weighting changes the mean by
    \val{ag_t1} \val{ag_t1_ci}, and the aggregate differs from the weighted mean by
    \val{ag_t2} \val{ag_t2_ci} (Holm $p$ \val{ag_t1_holm} and \val{ag_t2_holm}). In the
    sensitivity settings, \val{ag_n_sens_differ} of \val{ag_n_sens} tests are decided.
\end{itemize}
The aggregate need not equal any average of the country sums. The EU growth rate weights
countries by GDP, and averaging across countries removes much of the noise that pulls
country sums towards zero. A larger aggregate sum is also consistent with the CCE result
above: the association with shocks common to all countries is stronger than with
country-specific ones.

\paragraph{Post-2021 stability.}\label{sec:smallsample} Our first classical pass tested
whether the sum changed after 2021, with interacted HAC regressions and Holm's step-down
adjustment \citep{holm1979}: \val{ss_published_holm} changes were significant. With only
about seventeen post-2021 quarters, however, the asymptotic normal reference is
unreliable. A study registered after those rejections were known replaced it with
null-imposed sieve and moving-block bootstraps of the same statistic
\citep{kunsch1989bootstrap}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \val{ss_survivors} of the \val{ss_published_holm} changes survive;
  \item a size study under each test's fitted null shows why: the nominal 5\% normal test
    rejects \val{ss_size_normal} of the time, whereas the bootstrap test rejects
    \val{ss_size_bootstrap}.
\end{itemize}
The size study and the bootstraps of this test use the same restricted residuals as the
headline study. With the error process taken from the unrestricted residuals (post hoc,
\texttt{docs/NULL\_RESIDUALS.md}):
\begin{itemize}
  \item the normal reference rejects \val{nr_ss_size_normal_unres} instead of
    \val{ss_size_normal}, still far too often;
  \item no change survives Holm under either error process
    (\val{nr_ss_holm_unres} with unrestricted errors);
  \item Benjamini--Hochberg finds \val{nr_ss_bh_reg} with the registered $p$-values, and
    \val{nr_ss_bh_unres} with the unrestricted variant, which over-rejects
    (\val{nr_ss_size_boot_unres}).
\end{itemize}
A weaker association after 2021 is therefore a descriptive pattern, not a tested result.
The Bayesian regime models (section~\ref{sec:bayes}) find no credible shift either, but
they pool the country shifts strongly, so they have little power to detect one.
