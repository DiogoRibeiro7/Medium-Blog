\section{Classical estimates}\label{sec:classical}

\subsection{Method}

For each country, equation~\eqref{eq:okun} is estimated by ordinary least squares. Lag
orders are $K\in\{0,1,2,4\}$, with and without the change in the participation rate as a
control. The headline specification is $K=2$ without participation.

\paragraph{Inference.} Standard errors use the Newey--West covariance with a Bartlett
kernel and four lags in calendar quarters \citep{newey1987hac}. The variance of
$\hat\theta_i$ uses the full covariance matrix of the lag coefficients. The published
intervals use normal critical values, an approximation with \val{cl_n_quarters} quarters
per country. A registered study (below) replaces that reference with null-imposed sieve and
moving-block bootstraps of the same HAC-studentised statistic, which follow its
small-sample behaviour, including the fixed-$b$ effect of the bandwidth.

\paragraph{Samples.} The full period, 2009--2026; the same period without any observation
whose window touches 2020--2021; 2009--2019; 2022 onwards; and a flag-screened sample. The
research plan listed a pandemic-excluded comparison before any estimate. Making it the
headline sample was decided after the full-sample estimates had been seen.

\paragraph{Review.} A separate review recomputed every published quantity by an
independent numerical route and re-estimated \val{cl_n_core_published} core
specifications per country. It classified each country as robustly negative, negative
with specification-dependent significance, or sign-unstable.

\paragraph{The participation control.} Half of those core specifications control for the
change in participation. That variable is itself a response to output, and it is
mechanically linked to the outcome, because the labour force is the denominator of the
unemployment rate. Those specifications therefore estimate a different, partly mediated
quantity. A registered re-classification (\texttt{CORE\_GRID\_PROTOCOL.md}) applies the
same rule to the \val{cl_n_core} specifications without the control. Its verdicts are the
ones used here; only \val{cl_changed} changes.

\subsection{Results}

\paragraph{The association.} With 2020--2021 excluded, the two-lag sum is negative in
\val{hi_point_negative} of \val{n_countries} countries. With normal critical values, its
95\% interval lies below zero in \val{cl_n_neg_excluded} (table~\ref{tab:countries}), and
in \val{cl_n_neg_holm} after a Holm adjustment over the \val{n_countries} countries. In
\val{cl_n_robust} countries every core specification without the participation control
agrees in sign and excludes zero (\val{cl_n_robust_published} with the control included),
again with normal intervals.
These are associations conditional on contemporaneous and lagged GDP growth, and
contemporaneous growth is not exogenous.

\paragraph{Small-sample inference.} The registered study reaches a more cautious
conclusion:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the normal reference over-rejects: under each country's fitted null it rejects
    \val{hi_size_normal} of the time at a nominal 5\%, and too often in
    \val{hi_over_rejecting} of \val{n_countries} countries, while the sieve bootstrap
    rejects \val{hi_size_boot};
  \item under both bootstraps, \val{hi_negative} sums are negative at the 5\% level;
    \val{hi_lost} no longer are;
  \item after a Holm adjustment over the countries, \val{hi_negative_holm} remains under
    both schemes, against \val{cl_n_neg_holm} with the normal reference.
\end{itemize}
The registered null-imposed bootstrap understates the evidence when a sum is large. A
diagnostic added after the run shows why: imposing $\theta_i=0$ moves the omitted,
persistent GDP effect into the residuals and raises their first-order autocorrelation
(median increase \val{hi_rho_gap} before 2020). The bootstrap-t intervals, which use the
unrestricted residuals, lie below zero in \val{hi_boot_below} countries. Taken together,
the sign of the association is widespread across countries, but few individual country
sums are established once the number of countries tested is taken into account.

\paragraph{The pandemic.} Including 2020--2021 moves the sum towards zero in
\val{cl_n_attenuated} countries (figure~\ref{fig:pandemic}). In \val{cl_n_full_outside}
countries the full-sample estimate lies outside the 95\% interval of the estimate without
the pandemic. The pandemic quarters hold the largest output movements of the sample, so
the full-sample coefficient describes those quarters, not the 2009--2019 relationship.
Job-retention schemes are the usual explanation for why unemployment did not follow
output. The registered test below does not establish it.

\paragraph{Hours.} A registered study (\texttt{HOURS\_PROTOCOL.md}) estimated Okun's law in
hours worked for the \val{hr_n_hours} countries with hours data, with the case study's
estimator (section~\ref{sec:cases}), with and without the pandemic
(figure~\ref{fig:hours}). If job retention explains the attenuation, hours should keep
following output in 2020--2021 while unemployment does not, and the adjustment should move
to hours per worker. The point estimates have that pattern, with one exception:
\begin{itemize}
  \item including the pandemic raises the mean hours elasticity from \val{hr_mean_h_excl}
    to \val{hr_mean_h_full}, a ratio of \val{hr_ret_h}. The mean unemployment elasticity
    moves from \val{hr_mean_du_excl} to \val{hr_mean_du_full}, a ratio of \val{hr_ret_du};
  \item in the \val{hr_n_persons} countries with employment in persons, hours per worker
    take up more of the adjustment (\val{hr_d_hpw}). This comes at the expense of output
    per hour (\val{hr_d_prod}), not of the number of workers (\val{hr_d_e_dom}), which the
    explanation predicted would respond less;
  \item across countries, unemployment is attenuated more where hours per worker rise
    more, but only weakly (Spearman \val{hr_p3}).
\end{itemize}
None of the four registered tests is decided. For the first three, a flaw in the
registered design made this unavoidable. A bootstrap resample without a pandemic quarter
(\val{hr_no_pandemic} of them) gives a difference of exactly zero, so the $p$-value could
not fall below \val{hr_p_floor}. An amendment registered after that result holds the
number of pandemic quarters fixed in every resample. Under it, the first two patterns are
supported (Holm $p$ \val{hr_p1_amended_holm} and \val{hr_p2a_amended_holm}), and the
change in the number of workers is not decided. Job retention would produce this pattern,
but so would paid absence, and the 2021 survey redesign falls in the same window.

\paragraph{Post-2021 stability.}\label{sec:smallsample} Our first classical pass tested
whether the sum changed after 2021, with interacted HAC regressions and Holm's step-down
adjustment \citep{holm1979}: \val{ss_published_holm} changes were significant. With only
about seventeen post-2021 quarters, however, the asymptotic normal reference is
unreliable. A study registered after those rejections were known replaced it with
null-imposed sieve and moving-block bootstraps of the same statistic
\citep{kunsch1989bootstrap}:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \val{ss_survivors} of the \val{ss_published_holm} changes survive;
  \item a size study under each test's fitted null shows why: the nominal 5\% normal test
    rejects \val{ss_size_normal} of the time, whereas the bootstrap test rejects
    \val{ss_size_bootstrap}.
\end{itemize}
A weaker association after 2021 is therefore a descriptive pattern, not a tested result.
The Bayesian regime models (section~\ref{sec:bayes}) find no credible shift either, but
they pool the country shifts strongly, so they have little power to detect one.
