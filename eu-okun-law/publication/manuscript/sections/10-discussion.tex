\section{Discussion}\label{sec:discussion}

\subsection{Association, prediction and causation}

\paragraph{Association.} The three questions of the introduction have different answers.
The sign of the association is widespread: outside 2020--2021, faster GDP growth goes with
a falling unemployment rate in nearly every member state. Country by country, the evidence
is weaker than normal critical values suggest, and few individual sums survive an
adjustment for testing every country. Its size is not a single number:
\begin{itemize}
  \item it depends on whether the pandemic quarters are included;
  \item it depends on the error model, on whether a common European factor is removed,
    and on how persistence is modelled: the mean country sum runs from \val{by_mu_lo} to
    \val{by_mu_hi} across accepted specifications, and the mean long-run sum, a different
    quantity, is \val{dyn_mu} in the model that
    passes the persistence check;
  \item the hierarchical sum is smaller than OLS on the same quarters in
    \val{by_n_smaller} of \val{n_countries} countries, partly because the two estimate
    different quantities.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{What the size means.} A mean sum of \val{by_mu} means that one extra percentage
point of real GDP growth in a single quarter, which leaves output one per cent higher, goes
with an unemployment rate about \val{by_mu_abs} percentage points lower by the end of the
second following quarter, beyond the common European cycle. In the same first-difference
form, with real GNP rather than GDP, \citet{okun1962} estimated about $-0.3$ for the
United States; on the same quarters,
OLS gives \val{by_ES_ols} for Spain.

\paragraph{Prediction.} Over all target quarters, no registered forecast comparison is
decided. Outside the pandemic, pooling, GDP lags and the common factor improve forecasts
by a few per cent. Nothing reliably beats the forecast that the unemployment rate will not
change.

\paragraph{Causation.} The instruments and restrictions available with public data are
uninformative about a causal Okun coefficient. The coefficients reported here are
conditional associations: summaries of how unemployment and output moved together in a
period with a particular mix of shocks.

\subsection{Positive, negative and unstable results}

\paragraph{Positive.} Results that the registered tests decide and that hold across the
registered checks:
\begin{itemize}
  \item a negative association without 2020--2021, significant at the 5\% level in
    \val{hi_negative} of \val{n_countries} countries under small-sample bootstraps, and
    robust to the participation-free core specifications in \val{cl_n_robust}
    (with normal intervals);
  \item Spain's large association, relative to the European mean;
  \item the Spain--Luxembourg and Spain--Netherlands differences, which survive every
    accepted fit and remain when labour input is measured by employment or hours;
  \item a more GDP-elastic output per hour in the Netherlands than in Spain, supported in
    the primary design and in the \val{cs_p22_settings} sensitivity settings.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Negative.} Results where the registered test found nothing:
\begin{itemize}
  \item no decided forecast gain over all target quarters;
  \item none of the \val{ss_published_holm} post-2021 changes from our first classical
    pass survives small-sample inference;
  \item no informative causal coefficient from external instruments, shift-share or sign
    restrictions;
  \item no support for Luxembourg's commuters or its financial sector as explanations of
    its weak output-labour link, and transitory noise in its quarterly output not
    established;
  \item \val{cs_supported} of \val{cs_total} case-study predictions supported.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Unstable.} Results that depend on choices, samples or runs:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the magnitude of the association, through the pandemic quarters, the error model
    and the treatment of persistence;
  \item how many individual country sums are established: \val{hi_negative_holm} under the
    registered Holm adjustment (against \val{cl_n_neg_holm} with normal critical values),
    \val{nr_bh_reg} controlling the false discovery rate instead (post hoc, assuming
    positive dependence);
  \item the number of countries credibly negative in every accepted fit,
    \val{by_n_neg_all} or \val{by_n_neg_unpooled}, depending on one fit at the
    convergence threshold;
  \item \val{by_pairs_primary} credible country differences in the primary fit against
    \val{by_pairs_surviving} in every accepted fit;
  \item the forecast gains of pooling, GDP lags and the factor, decided only without the
    pandemic targets;
  \item the conditional gain over zero change before the pandemic (\val{pc_e1_zero}),
    which is no longer decided without the quarters with a failed refit;
  \item the Dutch delayed response, which holds in \val{claim_nl_holds} of
    \val{by_n_accepted} accepted fits, and Luxembourg's weak association, which holds in
    the plain regressions but in no registered Bayesian fit. On the long-run sum of the
    ARDL fits it holds only through an artefact of survey noise;
  \item whether hours kept following output in 2020--2021 while unemployment did not, with
    hours per worker taking up the adjustment: the point estimates show it, the registered
    test could not decide it, and an amendment registered after that result supports it;
  \item Portugal's significance across samples;
  \item \val{cs_n_pre2020_only} case-study predictions supported only before 2020;
  \item the employment-protection correlations: all \val{cs_n_epl_negative} have the sign
    opposite to the prediction, but \val{cs_n_epl_decided} are decided, both in the other
    variants, and none in the primary one.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Limitations}

\paragraph{Data.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item All data are revised vintages, so the forecasts are retrospective.
  \item The Labour Force Survey's 2021 redesign introduces flagged and unflagged
    discontinuities. The Bayesian models mask the flagged observations, but the Dutch
    phase-in is not flagged.
  \item Monthly unemployment is a direct estimate in only \val{mo_n_direct} countries;
    elsewhere it is a trend, a moving average or benchmarked to annual averages. Eurostat
    benchmarks the monthly series to the quarterly survey figures, not the reverse
    \citep{eurostat_une_rt_m_esms}, so the quarterly rate used here does not inherit that
    smoothing. A registered check (\texttt{SMOOTHING\_PROTOCOL.md}) could not show this
    directly. Its rule for a quarterly rate that is the average of the monthly rate also
    rejects \val{sm_direct_not_average} of the \val{mo_n_direct} direct-estimate
    countries, and the Netherlands' correlation of quarterly changes, \val{sm_nl_corr},
    ties \val{sm_direct_tie}'s. Its tests compare \val{sm_n_smoothed} smoothed countries
    with \val{mo_n_direct} direct ones:
    \begin{itemize}
      \item the persistence misfit is not concentrated in the smoothed countries: their
        mean lag-1 autocorrelation is \val{sm_p1_smoothed}, against \val{sm_p1_direct}
        (\val{sm_p1_verdict});
      \item the Dutch delayed response is not explained by the trend method (timing test
        \val{sm_p2_verdict}).
    \end{itemize}
    With so few countries, this is no evidence that smoothing drives these results, not
    evidence that it does not.
  \item Hours per worker and employment in persons are unavailable for
    \val{hr_n_no_persons} countries, including Portugal.
  \item The measurement-noise test detects only noise that averages out over a year, and
    it cannot detect error shared by output and hours, for example hours estimated from
    output (section~\ref{sec:cases}).
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Methods.}
\begin{itemize}
  \item The classical intervals use normal critical values, which over-reject
    with \val{cl_n_quarters} quarters per country. The registered null-imposed bootstrap
    that replaces them holds its size, but it understates the evidence when a sum is
    large. Its size study draws the null from the same restricted residuals, which
    exaggerates the over-rejection of the normal reference (\val{hi_size_normal} against
    \val{nr_size_normal_unres} on a less persistent null).
  \item The registered multiplicity adjustment, Holm, controls the chance of any false
    rejection. The false-discovery-rate counts were added after the run, and their
    guarantee needs a dependence assumption these two-sided tests do not strictly meet.
  \item The pandemic-excluded headline was chosen after the full-sample estimates had
    been seen.
  \item The registered test of the hours study could not reach the 5\% level, because
    some bootstrap resamples contain no pandemic quarter. The corrected test was registered
    after that result was known.
  \item The participation-free robustness verdicts were registered after the original
    review's verdicts, which also counted specifications with the participation control,
    had been seen. The two differ only for \val{cl_changed}.
  \item The primary Bayesian model understates the persistence of unemployment changes.
    The amendment that removes this misfit has a common factor correlated with average
    GDP growth (\val{dyn_factor_corr}), so its country sums measure the association beyond
    part of the common GDP cycle.
  \item The post-2021 sample has about seventeen quarters, too short to test a change in
    the association with useful power.
  \item The employment-protection correlations use at most \val{cs_epl_max_n} countries
    and a single year of indicators.
\end{itemize}

\paragraph{Standard analyses not done.} Several analyses a reader might expect are not in
this study:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the gap form of Okun's law, compared directly with \citet{ball2017okun};
  \item asymmetry between expansions and contractions;
  \item a common-correlated-effects mean-group panel;
  \item a labour-force-weighted EU aggregate.
\end{itemize}

\subsection{Open questions}

The study leaves these questions unresolved:
\begin{enumerate}
  \item Do countries differ because their labour markets respond differently to the same
    shock, or because they face different mixes of shocks?
    Equation~\eqref{eq:weighted} shows that both produce different Okun coefficients;
    separating them requires shock-specific identification.
  \item What is the effect on unemployment of a productivity or labour-supply shock in an
    EU country? This needs utilisation-adjusted productivity and quasi-experimental
    variation in labour supply.
  \item Why is Luxembourg's output so loosely linked to its labour input, at the quarterly
    and the year-on-year horizon alike, when neither commuters, transitory noise nor the
    financial sector explains it?
  \item Do temporary contracts carry Spain's employment adjustment? The direction is
    consistent with that view, but the registered test is not decided.
  \item Did the association weaken after 2021? This can only be answered with a longer
    sample.
\end{enumerate}
