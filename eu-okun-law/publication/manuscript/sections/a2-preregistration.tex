\section{Pre-registration record}\label{app:prereg}

\paragraph{What was registered, and when.} Each stage was registered before its own
estimation, sequentially, on the same data. Before any estimate of a stage, its protocol
was committed and pushed. The protocol fixes:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the data and sample;
  \item the estimator and its settings;
  \item the decision rules and multiple-comparison families;
  \item the sensitivity analyses;
  \item for the case study, the hypotheses that cannot be tested reliably.
\end{itemize}
Table~\ref{tab:prereg} lists the commit that first added each protocol. In every case the
commit precedes, in the repository history, the commit that first added that stage's
results.

\paragraph{What this does not mean.} The protocols were all written within five days in
October 2026, on the same frozen data, and each later stage was designed knowing the
results of the earlier ones:
\begin{itemize}
  \item the classical headline sample, without 2020--2021, was chosen after the
    full-sample estimates had been seen. \texttt{METHODS.md} listed the comparison, not
    which sample would lead, and that choice cannot be registered retrospectively;
  \item the Bayesian protocol was written after the classical results;
  \item the small-sample protocol was written after the \val{ss_published_holm} rejections
    of the first classical pass were known, to test whether they survive;
  \item the case-study hypotheses were written after the country estimates were known,
    under a rule that each must make predictions that can fail and be evaluated by the same
    rule in the comparison countries.
\end{itemize}
Registration therefore protects each stage's decision rules against the results of that
stage, not against the results of earlier stages. Table~\ref{tab:prereg} records the
origin of each protocol. Six are items of the first research roadmap, committed before
any estimate. The others are follow-ups: prompted by the results of an earlier stage or
by an external review, as each protocol states in its first section. A follow-up is
confirmatory for its own decision rules, but exploratory in the choice of its question. The timestamps are Git commits in a
public repository, which their authors can in principle rewrite. An external, immutable
archive of the protocols, such as a deposit with a DOI, would strengthen the record.

\paragraph{Changes after registration.} A change made after registration is recorded in
the protocol's change log, with its timing relative to the results and its reason. Three
kinds of change occurred:
\begin{itemize}
  \item \emph{clarifications made before any estimate}: for example, the Bayesian claim
    rules when a specification has no population mean, and the treatment of an indicator
    without variation;
  \item \emph{corrections after the first computation that change no rule}: for example,
    the coverage of employment in persons;
  \item \emph{amendments}: registered extensions with their own decision rules, written
    before their fits. Examples are the dynamic and regime Bayesian models and the monthly
    identification.
\end{itemize}
Analyses added after results were seen are labelled as exploratory in the protocol and in
the report. Examples are the checks of the common factor, and the widened pandemic window
of section~\ref{sec:identification}, added after an external review.

\paragraph{The classical plan.} \texttt{METHODS.md} is the research plan of the classical
stage. It was committed with the data pipeline before any estimate and revised in later
commits without a separate change log; the repository history records each revision.
