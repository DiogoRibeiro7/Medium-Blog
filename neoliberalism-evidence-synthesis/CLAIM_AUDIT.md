# Evidence ledger: what is established and what remains interpretation

The distinction between **measurement**, **causal attribution** and **normative judgment** is central to both essays. The following statements are deliberately calibrated to the available evidence.

| Claim | Status | Evidence that may be cited | What it does not prove |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Portuguese national house prices increased much faster than the average FTE employee salary from 2015 to 2025. | **Measured** | +163.8% HPI, +58.5% nominal FTE salary; derived ratio +66.5%. `results/summary.json`, Figures 1–2. | A home costs exactly 66.5% more years of *median* salary, or national price inflation was caused by weak bargaining. |
| Eurostat's Portuguese house-price-to-income indicator increased sharply from 2015 to 2025. | **Measured** | +60.9% from frozen rounded `tipsho60`, consistent with Eurostat's +60.8% press summary. | A precise ratio of home price to median wage or household mortgage burden. |
| Prices outpaced employee salaries in Spain, Germany and France too. | **Measured, heterogeneous** | Relative price-to-FTE-salary increases of +39.7%, +8.3%, +1.9% respectively; France's official price/disposable-income measure falls 5.6%. | A universal European pattern of identical magnitude or policy cause. |
| The share of Portuguese GDP going to labour unambiguously fell between 1995 and 2024. | **Not established as worded** | Raw employee compensation/GDP rises +0.9 pp; adjusted for self-employment by headcount falls −2.7 pp, by hours −4.2 pp. Figure 3. | The chosen self-employed income allocation is uniquely correct, or a trend test has established a causal decline. |
| U.S. top statutory income-tax rates fell sharply in the Reagan period. | **Measured** | 70% in 1980, 28% in 1988. Figure 4 and IRS/FRED source. | The effective tax rate or top 1% tax burden fell by exactly the same proportion. |
| U.S. household income inequality rose over the decades. | **Descriptive, with a break** | Census money-income Gini series; Figure 4 explicitly marks the 1993 survey redesign. | That the uninterrupted Gini increase is comparable across the redesign, or that tax changes alone caused it. |
| Changes in U.S. aggregate GDP growth and private investment identify tax-cut incentives. | **Not established** | Figure 5 provides historical real growth and private-investment/GDP for context. | The causal response to tax policy; macro outcomes have many simultaneous determinants. |
| Major tax cuts for the rich raise top income shares without evidence of faster average GDP growth. | **Published causal research, design-limited** | Hope & Limberg, *Socio-Economic Review* (2022), 18 OECD economies, 1965–2015, matched reform episodes; see upstream `data/research_estimate_audit.md`. | An exact zero growth effect for every reform or country; causal effect of *all neoliberal policies*; a new estimate by this companion. |
| Lower wages were the sole or universal root cause of the housing crisis. | **Not established** | Our price-to-salary ratio demonstrates one important affordability channel. | The separate causal contributions of land, credit, supply, public housing, investment demand, financial policy and labour institutions. |
| Neoliberalism, treated as one treatment in 1980, caused the entire subsequent housing crisis. | **Not identifiable from this panel** | The essays trace historical mechanisms and cite narrower studies. | A global counterfactual: countries adopted different reforms at different dates alongside technological and monetary changes. |

## One defensible summary for publication

> House prices have moved further away from wages in several European housing markets, most dramatically in Portugal in this sample. That does not, by itself, tell us which policy caused the divergence. But it makes labour income, bargaining institutions and the distribution of wealth indispensable parts of an affordability debate that often concentrates narrowly on the number of buildings.

## A necessary qualification for the two Medium articles

The essays should continue to make their political-economic argument as an interpretation supported by multiple lines of evidence, **not** as the result of one definitive causal model. The empirical companion adds a transparent wage denominator and tests the robustness of the labour-share narrative against measurement definitions. It also makes clear that higher nominal salaries, **holding housing prices fixed**, would mechanically improve the price-to-salary ratio, but this is an accounting counterfactual, not an estimate of how housing prices would respond to a wage policy.
