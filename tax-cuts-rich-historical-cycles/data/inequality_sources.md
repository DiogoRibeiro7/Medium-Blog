# Census household money-income Gini, 1967–2018

The 52 annual observations are transcribed from [FRED GINIALLRH](https://fred.stlouisfed.org/data/GINIALLRH),
which republishes U.S. Census Bureau Current Population Survey household
income inequality figures. Original producer: Census Bureau; FRED series
last updated 2026-09-16; viewed 2026-11-02. The values are Gini **ratios**
from zero to one, not percentage rates.

The measure is household **money income** before income and payroll taxes
and excludes important noncash transfers and realized capital gains.
It is therefore **not** the Gini of after-tax-and-transfer disposable
resources, **not** the Gini of wealth, and **not** a measure of top one-percent
capital-income concentration. Income definitions and household composition
also differ from the concept needed for a full welfare analysis. See the
[Census money-income definition](https://www.census.gov/topics/income-poverty/income/about.html).

The **1992–1993 break requires special attention**. Changes to CPS
income-collection methodology and processing make this jump unsuitable
for direct interpretation as an abrupt economic redistribution.
Contemporary Census methodological discussion explicitly warns about
pre-1993 versus post-1992 comparisons:
[The Changing Shape of the Nation's Income Distribution](https://www.census.gov/library/publications/2000/demo/p60-204.html).
For this reason the graphic separates the two segments rather than drawing
a continuous connecting line across the survey break.

The data begin in 1967, three years after the historical statutory tax
series. No 1964–1966 Gini values are constructed or interpolated.

This descriptive series cannot measure the effects of specific tax acts.
For distributional incidence use the Congressional Budget Office's
consistent pre-tax versus after-tax-and-transfer estimates, with explicit
corporate-tax allocation assumptions. An observed Gini trend alone cannot
attribute a change to any individual tax cut.
