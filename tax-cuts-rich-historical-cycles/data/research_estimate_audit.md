# Research-estimate audit: top-income taxes and TCJA investment

Checked 2026-11-02 against published research and responsible official agencies. Distinguish the reported **estimand** from an observational time series, model sensitivity, or social-welfare conclusion.

## Hope and Limberg (2022)

- Source: [published Socio-Economic Review article](https://academic.oup.com/ser/article/20/2/539/6500315), vol. 20(2), pp. 539–559, [DOI](https://doi.org/10.1093/ser/mwab061).
- Coverage: 18 OECD countries, 1965–2015; major reform episodes selected using a broader measure of taxes on the rich.
- Distributional outcome: top 1% share of **pre-tax national income**, not post-tax household disposable income or wealth.
- Approximate headline: the estimated top 1% pre-tax income share rises 0.6 **percentage points** three years after a major reform.
- Growth and unemployment estimates: no statistically significant average reform response in their study; this neither proves a precisely zero effect nor rules out heterogeneous national episodes.
- Identification: country-level comparison using a matching design; unobserved selection, anticipation, country spillovers and time-varying confounding remain interpretive limitations.

## Chodorow-Reich, Zidar and Zwick (2024)

- Source: [published Journal of Economic Perspectives article](https://www.aeaweb.org/articles?id=10.1257/jep.38.3.61), vol. 38(3), pp. 61–88; [author-hosted version](https://chodorowreich.scholars.harvard.edu/publications/lessons-biggest-business-tax-cut-us-history).
- Scope: 2017 TCJA **business** tax provisions, not an average effect of every tax cut for wealthy households.
- Their roughly **11%** aggregate increase is described as a *loose consensus from the literature* for **tangible corporate investment**. It is not an 11-point change in the GDP investment share and not a directly observed national before/after difference.
- The study discusses corporate revenue losses and finds that different instruments, such as accelerated depreciation, differ in estimated investment per revenue dollar. It does not imply fiscal self-financing or a measured complete social-welfare improvement.

## Congressional Research Service R48485 (2025)

- Source: [Economic Effects of the Tax Cuts and Jobs Act](https://www.everycrsreport.com/reports/R48485.html), 7 April 2025 (CRS-authored report as mirrored; prefer official CRS domain if available).
- The review questions tax-term measurement underlying the 11% business-investment synthesis. It notes an alternative **4.5%** estimate if that issue is adjusted, with possible further dampening from macroeconomic interest-rate and deficit effects.
- Treat 4.5% as a **sensitivity calculation**, not an independent empirical estimate established with a different treatment/control sample.
- The conclusion of the CRS review is broader than one investment series: it assesses output, wages, employment, corporate behavior and the evidence quality, and should not be paraphrased as proving precisely zero effects.

## GAO corporate effective tax rates

- Source: [GAO-23-105384](https://www.gao.gov/products/gao-23-105384), *Corporate Income Tax: Effective Rates Before and After 2017 Law Change*.
- **Publication date: 14 December 2022**, despite the “23” fiscal-year prefix in the report number. The essay bibliography must list **2022**.
- Scope: realized liabilities among the GAO-defined corporate population and income/tax concepts, not the statutory rate faced by every firm and not an investment impact estimate.

## Editorial separation

1. IRS/FRED charts document the annual **top regular-income statutory bracket**; they cannot explain the investment-response magnitude.
2. BEA/FRED nonresidential-investment shares include more than tangible corporate assets. Do **not** compare their percentage-point levels directly with the paper's estimated 11% proportional effect.
3. Census household *money-income* Gini is not Hope–Limberg's top 1% **pre-tax national income share**.
4. CBO before/after-transfer household income bars in 2018 are not a TCJA treatment effect.
5. Neither a positive partial investment response nor an insignificant growth estimate proves the sign of an unmodeled social welfare function.
