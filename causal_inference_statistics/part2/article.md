# Randomization, Confounding and the Value of Experimental Design

*Part 2 of Causal Inference in Statistics: why randomly assigning treatment changes what can be learned from the data, how chance imbalance and precision still matter, and why experimental validity depends on more than a random-number generator. Three thousand simulated datasets, three estimators, and a known causal effect. Python code provided.*

The first article considered a regression coefficient that became more precise as the sample grew but converged to the wrong causal quantity. The omitted variable affected both treatment and outcome; adding observations could not repair that failure. There is a complementary question. What happens if we change the mechanism determining who receives treatment, rather than trying to repair the resulting regression?

I investigate that question with an experiment in which the intervention is beneficial by construction. The true effect on the outcome is −1. In an observational sample, people with worse underlying prognosis are more likely to receive the treatment. Comparing the treated and untreated without accounting for that selection gives the wrong answer: treatment appears to *increase* the outcome by approximately 1.12 units. With random assignment, the same treatment reduces the outcome by approximately 1 unit, as the structural model specifies.

The distinction is not merely between biased and unbiased coefficients. Randomization also changes what justifies the inference, the interpretation of uncertainty, and what a regression is asked to accomplish. In observational data, the analyst needs an argument that treatment assignment is ignorable after accounting for appropriate information. In a randomized experiment, an assignment mechanism known before outcomes are measured supplies a basis for identifying an intention-to-treat contrast. That advantage is real, but not unlimited. A random assignment can produce imbalanced groups in one finite experiment, and an apparently well-randomized study can still fail if its intervention, measurement process or analysis population is poorly defined.

The simulation makes all of this visible without claiming that synthetic data can reproduce every difficulty of real clinical trials or policy programmes. I use 1,000 independent datasets at each of three sample sizes, with observational selection, complete randomization, and an adjusted analysis of the randomized experiment. The numerical results are generated by the accompanying Python script. Their purpose is to compare design-based and model-assisted approaches against a truth that is explicitly defined rather than inferred from an outcome table.

## The question before the design

Suppose a public employment service provides intensive job-search support to workers with a high predicted risk of long-term unemployment. The service wants to determine whether the programme reduces the number of weeks participants remain unemployed. If it looks at the historical data and finds that supported workers remain unemployed longer, should it conclude that the programme is harmful?

That would depend on how people entered the programme. If caseworkers deliberately offered more support to those facing the greatest difficulty, the treated workers might have had longer unemployment spells even without treatment. The allocation process would produce a positive association between treatment and duration that could coexist with a negative causal effect of support. A programme that genuinely helps people could therefore look harmful in a simple comparison of treated and untreated records.

The service could instead assign available support at random among eligible people, subject to ethical and practical constraints. In that case, baseline characteristics determining likely unemployment duration would not systematically determine assignment. Differences in observed outcomes could be attributed to assignment under clearly specified conditions, rather than immediately raising the question of whether the service sent support to the most difficult cases.

This is a stylized example, not an analysis of an actual employment programme. I use an outcome measured in abstract units rather than unemployment duration, so that censoring, labour-market conditions and programme compliance do not distract from the assignment mechanism. A real unemployment study would need to handle those matters separately. The relevant contrast is the same: what an intervention *does* versus who is selected to receive it.

The target quantity in the simulation is the average difference between two potential outcomes, one under treatment and the other under control. Because the treatment effect is constant, that average is also each individual's structural effect. Later articles will relax that convenience. For now it lets us attribute disagreement between estimates to the assignment and estimation procedures rather than to ambiguity about which population or treatment-effect weighting is being examined.

## Potential outcomes and the missing comparison

Let A_i be a binary treatment indicator for individual i. The potential outcomes Y_i(1) and Y_i(0) represent what the outcome would be for that individual under treatment and control. The individual causal effect is Y_i(1)−Y_i(0); the average treatment effect is

\[
\tau=E\{Y(1)-Y(0)\}.
\]

Under consistency and a well-defined intervention, the observed outcome is

\[
Y_i=A_iY_i(1)+(1-A_i)Y_i(0).
\]

We observe one potential outcome for each individual, not both. The missing potential outcome is the central obstacle. Nothing about a larger dataset, by itself, tells us how the treated individuals would have behaved without treatment or how the untreated individuals would have behaved under treatment.

The unadjusted difference in observed means is

\[
D=E[Y\mid A=1]-E[Y\mid A=0].
\]

Writing Y in terms of potential outcomes shows that this is

\[
D=E[Y(1)\mid A=1]-E[Y(0)\mid A=0].
\]

The two means describe different groups of individuals. The causal contrast, however, compares potential outcomes under two interventions in the same target population. If treatment assignment depends on prognosis, the difference between the groups' untreated potential outcomes can contaminate the observed comparison.

An informative decomposition adds and subtracts the untreated potential outcome among treated individuals:

\[
D=E[Y(1)-Y(0)\mid A=1]
 +E[Y(0)\mid A=1]-E[Y(0)\mid A=0].
\]

The first term is the average treatment effect among the treated. The remaining difference is selection bias in the untreated potential outcome. Thus a comparison can be misleading because the groups would have been different even if neither had received treatment. This decomposition does not require a linear regression or normally distributed variables; it follows from the potential-outcomes notation itself.

When effects vary between individuals, there is another complication. The average treatment effect among the treated is not necessarily the average effect in the target population. Even if a study controls the selection difference in untreated outcomes, it must be clear about which population the estimated effect represents. In our constant-effect experiment, that complication disappears by construction, leaving the selection-bias component exposed.

## A deliberately selective treatment policy

Let U represent baseline difficulty, measured before treatment, with larger values indicating worse untreated outcomes. Generate U and an independent disturbance ε from standard normal distributions, and define the potential outcomes

\[
Y(0)=2U+\varepsilon,\qquad
Y(1)=-1+2U+\varepsilon.
\]

For every individual, the treatment lowers the outcome by exactly one unit:

\[
Y(1)-Y(0)=-1.
\]

The treatment effect is not estimated from a regression at this point. It is a structural property of the process generating both potential outcomes. That distinction permits us to evaluate estimators against a known target.

Under observational selection, an individual with baseline characteristic U receives treatment with probability

\[
p(U)=\frac{1}{1+\exp(-1.5U)}.
\]

This logistic mechanism gives people with larger U a higher chance of treatment. Because U also makes outcomes worse, the treated and untreated groups differ in prognosis before any intervention occurs. The probability remains between zero and one for every finite U, so the issue is confounding rather than deterministic lack of treatment variation. In principle, an analyst who measured U correctly could use it to account for the selection process.

The observed outcome is generated as

\[
Y=-A+2U+\varepsilon.
\]

The causal effect remains negative. The observational comparison, however, contains both that effect and the baseline difference between the groups. Conditional on selection, the expected treatment-control outcome difference is

\[
D_{\mathrm{obs}}=-1+
2\{E[U\mid A=1]-E[U\mid A=0]\}.
\]

This expression makes the sign reversal possible. If the selected treatment group has an average U more than half a unit higher than the untreated group, the second term exceeds the magnitude of the true treatment benefit. The unadjusted comparison becomes positive even though treatment benefits every individual.

There is no need for an exotic estimator to manufacture the failure. A regression of Y on A with an intercept is exactly the treated-minus-control mean difference when A is binary. The regression does what it is asked to do. The causal error appears when the observed group difference is interpreted as the effect of assignment without accounting for the allocation mechanism.

## What randomization changes

For the experimental comparison, I keep the same U and ε values but replace observational selection with complete randomization. In each simulated dataset, exactly half the observations are assigned to treatment by randomly selecting their indices. The outcome equation stays the same:

\[
Y_R=-A_R+2U+\varepsilon.
\]

The potential outcomes are unchanged. Only treatment assignment changes. The assignment A_R is generated independently of the individual characteristics and disturbances and has a fixed number of treated and control observations. Therefore, before assignment, the treated and control groups are exchangeable with respect to the potential outcomes.

This is a statement about the assignment process, not a claim that the realized sample groups must contain identical values of U. In any particular randomization, the treated group may happen to contain more individuals with larger U. Randomization protects against systematic selection into treatment across possible assignments; it does not eliminate chance differences in one finite sample.

Because assignment is independent of potential outcomes, the expectation of the difference in means over random assignments equals the finite-sample average treatment effect, conditional on the individuals and their potential outcomes. In the simulation, that effect is −1 for everyone. Repeating the assignment and data generation should therefore produce an average estimate close to −1, without requiring regression adjustment for U.

The same comparison under observational selection lacks that design-based protection. There, treatment status carries information about baseline prognosis. In the randomized design, treatment status is created by an external allocation rule that does not consult prognosis. This is why randomization can turn an otherwise ambiguous comparison into an identified causal contrast.

The phrase *before assignment* matters. Randomization establishes probabilistic properties of how units are placed into groups. Analysts sometimes inspect realized baseline differences and conclude that the randomization failed if one variable differs by chance. A legitimate random procedure can produce visible imbalance, especially in small samples. A questionable nonrandom procedure can produce surprisingly similar measured baseline means. The existence or absence of an ordinary baseline significance test is not the fundamental evidence for a valid assignment mechanism.

## Three analyses

I compare three procedures. First, an observational treatment-control mean difference estimated by ordinary least squares without adjustment. Second, the same unadjusted treatment-control comparison after complete randomization. Third, a regression in the randomized data that includes both treatment and the observed baseline variable U.

The first estimator is deliberately confounded. Its expected result combines a treatment benefit with differences in baseline prognosis. The second identifies the causal effect through the random assignment. The third has the same design advantage as the second but uses information about U to explain outcome variability and improve precision.

The adjusted randomized model is

\[
Y_R=\beta_0+\tau A_R+\gamma U+\varepsilon.
\]

In the simulation, this is also the correct structural outcome model. Consequently, the residual variation after adjusting for U is much smaller than the variation faced by the unadjusted randomized comparison. The third procedure is expected to be more precise, although an actual experiment need not always benefit from a particular adjustment specification.

I use heteroskedasticity-consistent HC2 standard errors for all three regressions. The HC2 estimator uses squared residuals divided by one minus each observation's leverage. This is a model-based large-sample procedure with a leverage correction, not an exact randomization distribution. All methods use the same nominal 95% normal-approximation interval, and the simulation checks its empirical coverage against the known causal effect.

This choice deliberately separates three questions. Does the estimator recover the causal effect on average? How much does the estimate vary between datasets? And how often does the reported uncertainty interval contain the true effect? A model that appears statistically precise can fail the first question. A design that is unbiased can still have poor precision. A valid expectation does not automatically imply correct small-sample interval coverage under every variance estimator.

## The simulation protocol

There are 1,000 independently generated datasets at each of three sizes: 200, 1,000 and 5,000 observations. The generator seed is 20261016. Each dataset receives a fresh vector of U values and outcome disturbances. Treatment is then assigned once by the observational logistic mechanism and once by complete randomization. The same latent characteristics underlie the two scenarios, but their treatment assignments differ.

For the observational case, I draw independent uniforms and compare them with p(U). For complete randomization, I permute the observation indices and assign exactly half to treatment. This creates equal-sized treatment and control groups in the experimental scenario. The observational scenario does not enforce equal group sizes, although symmetry of the logistic mechanism means the expected treated fraction is one half.

After generating outcomes, each estimator returns a treatment coefficient and its HC2 standard error. The analysis records the absolute difference in average U between the treated and control groups. That difference is a deliberately simple diagnostic of baseline imbalance: it measures how differently the two groups are composed with respect to the one prognostic characteristic responsible for selection in this model.

The aggregate metrics are the mean estimated effect, bias relative to −1, root mean squared error, mean estimated standard error, empirical coverage of a nominal 95% interval, and mean absolute imbalance in U. Each serves a separate purpose. Bias concerns recovery of the causal estimand; RMSE combines bias and variability; standard error reports the uncertainty represented by the fitted regression; coverage checks those uncertainty statements across simulated data; imbalance shows the mechanism through which selection produces misleading comparisons.

The experiment fixes treatment assignment rules, structural coefficients and estimator settings before looking at the results. There is no hyperparameter tuning or model selection. Every result reported below is printed by the accompanying script. Re-running it with the same versions and seed makes the numerical comparison reproducible, while the mathematical arguments explain why its qualitative behaviour is not an accident of the seed.

## When selection reverses the answer

The average estimated effects are:

| Observations | Observational selection | Randomized difference | Randomized adjusted |
| ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 200 | 1.1236 | -1.0028 | -1.0036 |
| 1,000 | 1.1289 | -0.9974 | -1.0025 |
| 5,000 | 1.1209 | -1.0023 | -1.0000 |

The true effect is −1. In the observational data, the unadjusted difference is approximately +1.12 at every sample size. A researcher treating that difference as causal would conclude that treatment increases the outcome, whereas the model defines a reduction of one unit for every treated individual.

The sign reversal is a consequence of selective treatment assignment. The mean absolute imbalance in U is approximately 1.06 for the observational groups, compared with 0.114, 0.049 and 0.023 under randomization at sample sizes 200, 1,000 and 5,000. The observational groups remain systematically different as the sample increases. In randomized samples, the remaining imbalance is chance variability and becomes smaller on average with larger samples.

The observational regression is not simply imprecise. Its estimated effect converges toward a stable but causally wrong population difference. The mean estimate changes only modestly between 200 and 5,000 observations. There is no sign that additional observations are pulling it toward the actual intervention effect.

The randomized analyses remain close to −1. At n=200 they differ from the truth by only a few thousandths on average. At n=5,000 the adjusted mean rounds to −1.0000. These Monte Carlo means should not be mistaken for individual study estimates; a particular randomized experiment can produce an estimate appreciably above or below −1. The statement is that the estimation procedure is centred on the causal effect across the assignment and sampling process specified here.

The difference between the observational and randomized analyses cannot be explained by a more elaborate outcome model. The unadjusted regression used in each is the same linear estimator. It is the relationship between assignment and prognosis that changes the interpretation of the coefficient. This is the central contribution of experimental design to identification.

## Uncertainty does not settle the causal question

The simulation also reports uncertainty and error:

| n | Analysis | RMSE | Mean HC2 SE | 95% coverage |
| ---: | --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| 200 | Observational | 2.1417 | 0.2785 | 0.000 |
| 200 | Randomized | 0.3135 | 0.3157 | 0.952 |
| 200 | Randomized adjusted | 0.1416 | 0.1420 | 0.946 |
| 1,000 | Observational | 2.1323 | 0.1247 | 0.000 |
| 1,000 | Randomized | 0.1389 | 0.1416 | 0.963 |
| 1,000 | Randomized adjusted | 0.0629 | 0.0633 | 0.951 |
| 5,000 | Observational | 2.1217 | 0.0557 | 0.000 |
| 5,000 | Randomized | 0.0628 | 0.0632 | 0.951 |
| 5,000 | Randomized adjusted | 0.0291 | 0.0283 | 0.948 |

The observational mean standard error falls from 0.279 to 0.056, but its causal RMSE barely changes. The estimate becomes more precise for the observed group comparison while remaining roughly 2.12 units away from the true effect. None of the observational confidence intervals contains −1 in the 1,000 replications at any sample size.

That zero coverage is coverage of the *causal effect*, not necessarily coverage of the observational regression's own population association. The HC2 procedure describes sampling variation for the fitted coefficient under its working conditions. It does not incorporate uncertainty about omitted baseline prognosis. Interpreting the fitted interval as a causal interval is the failure, not the ordinary calculation of a regression standard error.

For both randomized analyses, RMSE decreases substantially as n increases. Their interval coverage remains close to the nominal 95%. The adjusted analysis also has much smaller standard errors: at n=5,000, approximately 0.028 rather than 0.063. This is an efficiency advantage of including U in a correctly specified regression, conditional on the randomized design and the particular outcome model.

The two randomized estimators are not competing identification strategies. They share the same assignment mechanism and target the same constant effect. They differ in how much outcome variation is explained after treatment is assigned. Under randomization, a prognostic baseline variable can improve precision when appropriately handled, even though omitting it does not introduce the same confounding bias as omitting it in observationally selected data.

The lesson is not that confidence intervals are useless. It is that a confidence interval answers a sampling question conditional on its estimand and assumptions. A very narrow interval around the observational difference can be entirely appropriate as a description of that difference. It becomes misleading when reported as uncertainty about an intervention effect without the identifying conditions needed to equate the two.

## Why the balance difference matters

In this model, the selection mechanism creates a strong difference in U between groups. The observational average absolute imbalance is slightly above one standard deviation because people with high U are preferentially treated. Since the outcome increases by two units per unit of U, the imbalance contributes more than two outcome units to the observed treated-control difference. It is large enough to outweigh the treatment benefit of one unit and reverse the sign.

The randomized assignments have no systematic link with U. Their average absolute imbalance declines as sample size rises, although the magnitude is nonzero in any finite replication. The decline is broadly consistent with the inverse-square-root relationship expected for differences of independent sample means. Under complete randomization, treated and control groups are selected without replacement from the same realized dataset, so the exact finite-population variance accounts for that assignment structure.

A baseline comparison is useful for understanding precision and diagnosing mistakes in the implementation of an experiment. It should not be elevated into a test that determines whether the randomization is valid. A randomized allocation can produce an unusually large imbalance by chance. Re-running randomization until the baseline p-values exceed a threshold would change the assignment mechanism and must be treated as a different design.

Conversely, an observational selection rule could balance a measured variable while remaining highly confounded by an unmeasured one. Looking balanced does not establish exchangeability. Causal identification depends on the assignment rule or on justified assumptions about treatment conditional on covariates, not simply on the visual similarity of two columns in a table.

The balance measure reported here is intentionally limited to one variable because this is the only prognostic common cause in the constructed model. In a real study, baseline variables may be numerous, correlated, noisy or defined after treatment starts. Interpreting balance requires substantive knowledge about their causal roles, their distributions, and the target estimand. A single standardized difference cannot certify the study.

## Randomization as a design rather than a statistical ritual

There are two ways to describe the uncertainty of a randomized comparison. A sampling model regards observations as draws from a larger population and studies how estimates change over repeated samples. A design-based argument can instead hold the finite collection of individuals and their potential outcomes fixed and consider the assignments that the randomization procedure could have produced.

The latter approach is important because it shows precisely what is learned from the assignment mechanism. Under complete randomization, each eligible allocation with the prescribed treated count has a known probability. The difference in means is unbiased over assignments for the finite-sample average treatment effect, assuming outcomes are observed as defined and the assigned treatment is the intervention whose effect is of interest.

Neyman's framework of randomization-based inference formalized such comparisons for agricultural experiments, later becoming central to experimental analysis in other fields. Fisher's randomization test approaches the problem from the distribution of a statistic under a sharp null hypothesis that specifies outcomes under all assignments. These are related but different inferential questions: estimation and uncertainty for an average effect need not be identical to testing a sharp hypothesis of no individual effects.

The Monte Carlo experiment does not compute an exact randomization test. It generates many independent datasets and uses HC2 regression intervals. That is a valid and informative computational choice for the present purpose, but it should not be described as an exact finite-sample design-based analysis. The source of identification is random assignment; the source of reported standard errors is an asymptotic regression procedure.

This separation matters in practice. Researchers often invoke randomization as a reason to interpret a treatment contrast causally, then silently switch to assumptions belonging to a particular regression variance estimator when making confidence claims. The first statement can hold while the second needs additional care. Randomization is a powerful design principle, not a promise that every familiar inferential formula is exact in every trial.

The simplest difference in means has an especially transparent relationship with the randomized allocation. Regression adjustment can be useful, but it introduces modelling choices and sometimes different finite-sample properties. This motivates studying both the treatment assignment procedure and the planned analysis rather than treating the regression software's default output as the definitive analysis of an experiment.

## Adjustment after randomization

The randomized regression including U is much more precise in the simulation. That result is unsurprising given the outcome equation. U explains substantial variation in untreated outcomes, and treatment assignment is unrelated to U except for chance imbalance. By accounting for U, the adjusted analysis reduces residual variation without requiring U for identification.

This differs from the observational setting. There, failing to account for U creates a systematic difference between treated and control outcomes that is not caused by treatment. Adjustment plays an identification role. In the randomized setting, assignment already supplies exchangeability, so adjusting for U chiefly plays an efficiency role in this constant-effect model.

It would be too strong to conclude that adding covariates to a randomized regression always improves precision or preserves simple standard-error properties. When models are misspecified, treatment effects are heterogeneous, sample sizes are small, or covariates are selected after examining outcomes, ordinary regression adjustment can have surprising behaviour. Freedman (2008) examined limitations of regression adjustment under randomization-based inference; Lin (2013) studied more robust regression-adjustment procedures, including treatment-covariate interactions and suitable variance estimators.

One useful distinction is between variables measured before assignment and variables affected by treatment. Baseline prognostic covariates can often be used to increase efficiency when analysis is properly specified. Adjusting for variables that arise after treatment is assigned can change the causal estimand, condition on mediators, or introduce selection bias. The label *covariate adjustment* hides differences that matter for identification.

The simulation uses a single baseline U and a constant effect, so the simple additive regression is exactly aligned with the structural outcome equation. It is a favourable example for adjustment. More realistic investigations should vary effect heterogeneity, functional form, the number of covariates and the ratio of observations to parameters, then compare estimators using both their error and their causal target.

There is also a design choice before the experiment starts: stratification or blocked randomization may deliberately balance important baseline characteristics in the allocation. That is distinct from correcting chance imbalance after an unstratified randomized assignment. Both can improve precision in suitable circumstances, but their uncertainty calculations need to respect the actual assignment mechanism.

## Intention to treat and what was actually received

The synthetic intervention is simple: assigned treatment equals received treatment and directly changes the outcome by −1. Real experiments often do not work like that. A participant may be assigned to a programme but decline it; someone allocated to control may obtain similar support elsewhere; delivery intensity may vary; outcomes may be missing at follow-up.

Under those conditions, the clean randomized comparison generally identifies the effect of *assignment*, not automatically the effect of actually receiving treatment. This is the intention-to-treat estimand. It can be highly relevant to a policymaker because an offered programme may have limited uptake in the conditions under which it would be deployed. It is not necessarily the biological, behavioural or service-delivery effect among those who comply.

Restricting the analysis to those who comply can undermine randomization, because compliance may depend on prognosis or potential outcomes. The resulting comparison of compliant treated and untreated participants may once again involve selected groups. Instrumental-variable approaches can sometimes recover effects among particular compliance types, but only under additional assumptions; that subject deserves its own investigation.

Missing outcomes create a related problem. Assignment may be randomized at baseline, but the set of people with observed outcomes at follow-up may not be. If treatment affects participation in the follow-up, or if loss to follow-up depends on potential outcomes, a complete-case comparison can introduce bias. The trial's allocation record remains randomized; the analysis sample no longer necessarily inherits the desired exchangeability.

The present simulation contains no noncompliance, attrition or interference. It therefore establishes the advantage of randomized assignment under idealized implementation, not that randomized experiments are immune to the same selection problems encountered in observational research. The design has to be protected through data collection and analysis.

## The importance of defining an intervention

A statement that an intervention has a causal effect assumes we know what intervention is being compared with what alternative. In the simulation, A=1 is a single precisely defined operation that subtracts one unit from the outcome. It does not matter who administers the treatment or what form it takes. In real applications those details may determine the effect.

An employment programme might include weekly counselling, access to job listings, training, financial support or some combination. Two participants coded as treated could receive materially different versions. A policy that increases programme availability may not reproduce the effect of compelling everyone to attend. The average effect estimated under one implementation need not be the effect of a different implementation.

Consistency links the observed outcome to the potential outcome under the treatment actually received. It becomes difficult to interpret when treatment labels combine interventions with different consequences. Positivity requires treatment options to be feasible, or observed with positive probability, across the relevant characteristics. Interference arises if one person's treatment affects another person's outcome, such as when job-search assistance redistributes a limited set of vacancies among competing applicants.

All of these issues are absent from the constructed model, but they matter directly to the interpretation of experimental evidence. Random assignment of an ill-defined treatment can yield a well-estimated difference between groups without answering the policy question an analyst intended. The scientific work begins before the random-number generator is called.

These points also limit how far the results from one experiment can be transported to another population. An effect estimated among eligible participants in a pilot programme may not be the effect that follows from scaling the programme nationally. Differences in case mix, intervention delivery, capacity and market equilibrium can all change the target. Internal validity and external validity are separate questions; a good experimental design does not automatically settle both.

## The problem of external validity

The randomized analysis estimates the effect of the assignment in the population represented by the experiment, under its defined implementation. A policy decision may concern a different population, a different time or a different scale of deployment. Even if the experimental estimate is unbiased for its own target, it can be insufficient for that broader decision.

The constant treatment effect makes transport trivial in our simulation: every individual benefits by exactly one unit, regardless of U. If effects differed with U, the average effect in the experimental population would depend on the distribution of U among participants. A programme tested among individuals with moderate baseline difficulty could have a different average effect among individuals facing the greatest barriers.

Selection into an experiment can therefore matter without confounding the randomized comparison *within* the experiment. Volunteers may differ from nonparticipants; eligibility rules may exclude groups that a future policy would reach; implementation may change as services expand. These are not reasons to dismiss randomized trials. They are reasons to state clearly which causal quantity a particular trial identifies.

One should also distinguish the statistical uncertainty of the within-trial estimate from uncertainty about transport to another setting. Standard errors and confidence intervals based on the original experimental sample do not automatically account for differences in the target population or treatment implementation. Transport requires additional information and assumptions about how effects vary and how the target population differs.

The demonstration in this article should therefore be read as a statement about identification under a controlled assignment mechanism. It is not evidence that randomized results from any context can be transferred uncritically to any policy decision.

## What an observational study can still do

Randomization is not always possible. Ethical constraints, implementation costs, political decisions and the irreversibility of some exposures can prevent controlled assignment. Even when a trial is feasible, its duration or scope may be inadequate for the effect of interest. Observational evidence remains essential, but it requires a different argument.

In the simulated observational data, U is measured and treatment has nonzero probability at every finite value of U. A properly specified adjustment for U can therefore recover the effect under the known model. That possibility should not be confused with the unadjusted estimator examined here. The point is not that observational data inherently reverse causal effects, but that the assignment mechanism must be addressed rather than ignored.

Methods such as outcome regression, stratification, matching, propensity-score weighting and doubly robust estimation are different ways of exploiting assumptions about conditional exchangeability and overlap. Their numerical properties vary, and no method can prove that unobserved causes have been controlled. An excellent estimate of a propensity score does not establish that the covariates in that score are sufficient for identification.

Some observational studies can exploit discontinuities, policy timing, instrumental variables or other institutional sources of plausibly exogenous variation. These designs have their own identification arguments, and often identify a different population-specific effect from a randomized trial. The relevant comparison is between credible causal designs and the decisions they support, not a simplistic hierarchy in which all experimental numbers are true and all observational numbers are false.

One particularly useful role of observational evidence is to assess whether experimental findings persist across populations, follow-up periods or implementation settings. It can also reveal rare outcomes or long-term consequences that an initial experiment was not designed to capture. The strength of the resulting conclusions depends on how the pieces of evidence fit together, rather than on the sample size of any single analysis.

## What the experiment does not establish

The benchmark uses a constant treatment effect, a single measured baseline characteristic and normally distributed disturbances. The observational assignment is a known logistic function of U, and the randomized assignment is exact complete randomization with half of each dataset treated. All outcomes are observed, treatment is delivered as assigned and individuals do not affect one another.

The simulated experiment therefore avoids many of the questions that make trial design difficult: recruiting participants, deciding eligibility, choosing an intervention protocol, protecting assignment concealment, defining primary outcomes, handling deviations from the protocol and measuring outcomes that may be censored or missing. None of those complications is tested by this numerical comparison.

The numerical advantage of regression adjustment should also be read narrowly. The analysis adjusts for precisely the variable that determines baseline outcome variation, measured without error. It uses the correct functional form. In another setting, including unnecessary variables, omitting interactions, selecting covariates after examining outcomes or using a high-dimensional model could produce a different efficiency comparison.

The uncertainty intervals use HC2 and a normal critical value. Their approximate 95% coverage in these simulations does not establish that HC2 provides correct confidence intervals under every randomized design or outcome distribution. In particular, clustered assignment, dependence between units or very small sample sizes would require additional treatment. The design of the experiment and the analysis of its uncertainty must be considered together.

There is one more important limitation. The observational and randomized cases share the same structural outcomes but do not attempt to model behavioural responses to being randomly assigned. In a real programme, the fact of assignment, participants' awareness of participation, and the surrounding institutional environment can alter behaviour. The experiment isolates selection bias; it does not model all the consequences of introducing an experimental design into a social system.

Finally, an artificial data-generating mechanism does not demonstrate how large confounding bias is in real employment programmes or how effective support actually is. Its purpose is methodological. It shows that a treatment which benefits every simulated individual can look harmful under selective assignment and that randomization removes that particular source of bias.

## What a study should report

A useful experimental report starts by stating the decision and the causal estimand. Is the target the effect of offering treatment, the effect of receiving it, or the effect under a particular level of compliance? Which population and outcome horizon matter? Without those details, a difference in means may be statistically meaningful but practically ambiguous.

The assignment mechanism should then be documented. Was randomization individual or clustered? Were probabilities equal or unequal? Were blocks, strata or covariate-adaptive rules used? Was the allocation implemented as planned? These questions matter because the mechanism is part of the evidence supporting a causal interpretation.

Baseline characteristics can be summarized to describe the sample and help understand precision. They should not be treated as a pass-fail test of randomization. Any adjustment strategy should be prespecified where practical, use variables measured appropriately relative to assignment, and match the actual design. Standard errors should reflect clustering or other dependence when the design requires it.

The report should separate the treatment contrast, its uncertainty, and the assumptions needed for its interpretation. If compliance is imperfect, it should explain whether the primary result is intention to treat. If outcomes are missing, it should state the extent and plausible mechanisms of missingness, and assess how sensitive the effect is to reasonable departures from the missing-data assumptions.

Finally, the connection to the target decision should be explicit. A policy programme with a well-estimated average effect may still be a poor allocation strategy if benefits differ across people or if the treatment is costly. An experimental estimate can be essential evidence without being the entire decision model. That distinction becomes increasingly important as we move from average effects to treatment-effect heterogeneity and policy evaluation.

## What the experiment established

Across 1,000 simulated datasets at each sample size, the treatment genuinely reduced the outcome by one unit. Under selective observational assignment, the unadjusted treated-control comparison averaged approximately +1.12, reversing the sign of the effect. At n=5,000 its mean standard error was just 0.0557, and none of the nominal 95% intervals across the 1,000 replicates contained the causal effect.

Complete randomization changed the result without changing the estimator: the unadjusted comparison recovered approximately −1. Its RMSE decreased from 0.3135 at n=200 to 0.0628 at n=5,000, with interval coverage close to 95%. Adjustment for a strongly prognostic baseline variable reduced the randomized RMSE further, to 0.0291 at n=5,000, under the favourable conditions of this model.

Randomization is valuable because it changes the assignment mechanism and the justification for a causal comparison, not because it automatically makes a particular regression sophisticated or precise. Chance imbalance remains possible, statistical efficiency still matters, and the intervention, outcome, compliance process and target population must be defined.

The strongest conclusion is therefore about design. A statistically precise observational association can point in the opposite direction from a known intervention effect when treatment is selectively allocated. Changing the assignment mechanism can recover the causal contrast; adding observations to the selected comparison cannot. The following articles examine what can be done when changing assignment is not possible, and which assumptions observational methods need to make that recovery credible.
