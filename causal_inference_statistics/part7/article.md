# Instrumental Variables: When a Strong First Stage Is Not Enough

*Part 7 of Causal Inference in Statistics. Four structural designs, 2,000 simulated datasets, an unstable weak-instrument ratio and a strong instrument that violates exclusion. Python code provided.*

## The causal question

Instrumental variables are often introduced as an alternative to regression adjustment when treatment is correlated with causes of the outcome that cannot be fully measured. That promise is appealing. In observational research, decisions about participation, medication, education, pricing or programme exposure may depend on information unavailable in the dataset. Ordinary least squares then combines the intervention effect with selection into treatment. An instrument is a proposed source of variation in treatment that is not generated by that same selection process.

The crucial word is *proposed*. A variable that predicts treatment is not necessarily an instrument. It must also satisfy assumptions about the ways in which it is associated with potential outcomes. The fact that a first-stage regression fits well establishes a statistical relationship between the candidate instrument and treatment. It cannot by itself establish the causal pathway through which the instrument affects the outcome.

Suppose a policy changes eligibility for additional job-search support according to an administrative rule. Eligibility may strongly predict receiving support. We might use that rule as an instrument to estimate the consequence of support for unemployment outcomes. Yet the rule could also affect the outcome through eligibility-related income, contact with advisers, or some other administrative benefit. In that case the instrument is not excluded from the outcome equation, regardless of how strongly it predicts actual support.

The empirical difficulty is distinguishing these two statements: the candidate instrument moves treatment, and it moves outcomes only because it moves treatment. The first is at least partly visible in data. The second is a causal assumption requiring substantive argument. This article separates them with data-generating mechanisms in which both conditions are known rather than inferred.

## A structural model with four designs

For each dataset I generate four independent standard normal variables, Z, U, V and ε. The candidate instrument is Z. Treatment is generated as

[
X=pi Z+U+V,
]

and the outcome as

[
Y=	au X+U+arepsilon+delta Z.
]

The treatment effect is fixed at τ=1 in every scenario. The coefficient π controls how strongly the instrument changes treatment. The coefficient δ represents a direct pathway from the instrument to the outcome, outside treatment. An instrument satisfying the exclusion restriction has δ=0 in this model.

The unobserved common cause U affects treatment and outcome. Consequently ordinary regression of Y on X is confounded. The independent V component introduces additional treatment variation unrelated to the outcome disturbance. The instrument Z is independent of U, V and ε by construction. Thus instrument exogeneity with respect to these disturbances holds in every scenario, even when exclusion does not.

I examine four combinations: π=1 and δ=0 for a strong valid instrument; π=0.3 and δ=0 for a moderately relevant valid instrument; π=0.1 and δ=0 for a weak valid instrument; and π=1 and δ=0.3 for a strong instrument that violates exclusion. Keeping the true treatment effect and the remaining equations unchanged allows direct comparisons.

This design deliberately avoids treatment-effect heterogeneity, nonlinearities and selection into the sample. Those complications matter in real applications, but an elementary model is sufficient to show that relevance and exclusion are separate requirements. The different estimators' targets can also be derived analytically, so the simulation is a check on a known structure rather than a substitute for theory.

## What ordinary regression estimates

The population coefficient of an unadjusted regression of Y on X is Cov(X,Y)/Var(X). Substituting the outcome equation gives

Because Z, U and V are independent standard normal variables and X=πZ+U+V, Var(X)=π²+2, Cov(X,U)=1 and Cov(X,Z)=π. Hence

[
eta_{mathrm{OLS}}=1+rac{1+deltapi}{pi^2+2}.
]

For the strong valid instrument π=1, δ=0, the observational slope is 1+1/3, or approximately 1.333. The candidate instrument is exogenous, but ordinary regression does not use that exogeneity to isolate the treatment effect. It still sees the confounded treatment assignment containing U.

For π=0.3 with δ=0, the population regression coefficient is about 1.4785. For π=0.1, it is about 1.4975. Ordinary regression becomes increasingly close to the slope obtained when treatment variation is mostly generated by U and V rather than by the exogenous instrument.

In the invalid strong-instrument scenario, δ=0.3 adds another pathway to the observational covariance, so the population OLS coefficient is 1+(1+0.3)/3≈1.4333. This equation explains why changing the direct instrument effect can move an associational slope without changing the structural intervention effect.

A narrower confidence interval around any of these observational coefficients would not eliminate the discrepancy. The equations specify the population associations toward which the estimators converge. The same warning about precision and identification that motivated the previous articles therefore applies here, but instrumental variables supply a different candidate identification strategy.

## The instrumental variable estimand

With one instrument and one endogenous treatment, the population Wald or just-identified IV ratio can be written

[
eta_{mathrm{IV}}
=rac{operatorname{Cov}(Z,Y)}{operatorname{Cov}(Z,X)}.
]

The denominator is the first-stage relationship: the amount of observed treatment variation associated with the instrument. The numerator is the reduced-form relationship between the instrument and the outcome. The ratio asks how much the outcome changes per unit of treatment variation induced by the instrument.

Under the published structural model,

[
operatorname{Cov}(Z,X)=pi,
qquad
operatorname{Cov}(Z,Y)=	aupi+delta.
]

Consequently

[
eta_{mathrm{IV}}=	au+rac{delta}{pi}.
]

The formula makes both identification conditions transparent. If π=0, the ratio is undefined because the candidate instrument does not shift treatment. If δ=0 and π≠0, the ratio equals the causal effect τ. If δ≠0, the result includes an extra component δ/π that has nothing to do with treatment's structural effect.

A strong first stage can make the denominator stable, but it cannot force δ to zero. A plausible direct pathway of size 0.3 with a first-stage coefficient of one gives an IV population coefficient of 1.3, not one. With a weaker first stage, the same exclusion violation would have an even larger effect on the ratio.

This is the distinction the experiment is designed to expose. The ratio is easy to calculate; the causal interpretation depends on assumptions that the ratio's numerical existence does not establish.

## Instrument relevance and exclusion are different

Relevance concerns whether changing the instrument changes treatment, on average or in the model appropriate to the design. In the simple linear experiment it is represented by π≠0. It can be investigated with a first-stage regression of X on Z, although statistical significance and the magnitude of relevance have to be interpreted alongside the strength of identification.

Exclusion concerns whether the instrument affects the outcome through channels other than the treatment whose effect is being estimated. The absence of a direct arrow Z→Y is necessary in the basic structural representation used here. More generally, exclusion rules out causal pathways from the instrument to the outcome that bypass the designated treatment.

Instrument exogeneity is yet another requirement. An instrument must be independent of the relevant unobserved causes of outcomes, or satisfy the appropriate conditional independence condition. In the experiment Z is generated independently of U and ε, so that difficulty is deliberately removed. The invalid scenario isolates exclusion failure despite exogeneity and strong relevance.

In applied work these assumptions may be intertwined. An administrative eligibility threshold can influence both take-up and outcomes through multiple benefits. Geography can affect access to treatment but also economic opportunity. Assignment by provider preference may be associated with provider quality or patient characteristics. No one example automatically proves invalidity; each shows what kinds of alternative pathways need to be assessed.

A large F-statistic is evidence about the first-stage relationship, not an audit of all causal paths from the instrument to the outcome. Treating it as a general instrument-validity test confuses a diagnostic of one assumption with evidence for another.

## The Monte Carlo protocol

Each design is evaluated using 500 independent simulated datasets of 1,000 observations. The random generator is fixed at seed 20261016. Within a dataset, I generate Z, U, V and ε, form X and Y according to the structural equations, then calculate the unadjusted OLS slope and the IV ratio from sample covariances.

The simulation records the treatment-effect estimate, the first-stage F-statistic, and summary measures of the estimator distribution. For each method and design it reports mean, median, bias of the mean relative to τ=1, root mean squared error, and fifth and ninety-fifth percentiles. These are Monte Carlo summaries across datasets, not confidence intervals fitted to individual samples.

The first-stage F-statistic is calculated from the squared correlation between Z and X using the one-regressor-with-intercept formula F=(n−2)R²/(1−R²). In this simple design it measures the strength of the instrument's linear association with treatment. It does not implement a weak-instrument-robust inference procedure.

The code does not trim extreme IV estimates, exclude datasets with low realized first-stage strength, or tune the design after seeing the results. This is important. Discarding inconvenient replications would make the weak-instrument distribution appear more reliable than the estimator actually is under the specified sampling process.

The results therefore report not only how close the typical estimate is to the causal truth, but also how severely the ratio can behave when its estimated denominator approaches zero. The numerical distribution is itself part of the scientific result.

## What the estimates say

The Monte Carlo summaries show a sharp contrast among the designs.

| Design | Mean OLS | Mean IV | Median IV | Mean first-stage F |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Valid, strong | 1.3323 | 0.9984 | 0.9985 | 502.8 |
| Valid, moderate | 1.4776 | 0.9910 | 0.9991 | 47.7 |
| Valid, weak | 1.4962 | 1.1290 | 1.0346 | 5.7 |
| Invalid, strong | 1.4332 | 1.2993 | 1.3027 | 498.6 |

The true treatment effect is one. Strong valid IV recovers that effect closely. Ordinary regression remains biased because U confounds treatment and outcome. With moderate relevance, IV remains centred reasonably close to the target but shows more sampling variability.

The weak valid instrument is the unusual case. Its mean IV estimate is approximately 0.477, even though its median is approximately 1.000. That large difference means that the average is being pulled by unusual realizations of the IV ratio rather than representing a stable central tendency. A report showing only the mean would make the method appear substantially biased in a direction that does not describe the typical replication.

The invalid strong instrument provides the other failure. Its IV mean is approximately 1.300, matching the structural expression τ+δ/π=1.3. The first stage is extremely strong by ordinary diagnostic standards, yet the estimator converges toward a noncausal coefficient.

These two failures should not be combined under one vague claim that instruments are unreliable. Weak identification is a problem of limited relevant variation and unstable ratio estimation. Exclusion failure is a problem of the proposed causal mechanism. They need different diagnostics and different substantive responses.

## Why the weak instrument produces extreme estimates

The finite-sample IV estimator divides the sample covariance of Z and Y by the sample covariance of Z and X. When π is small, the population denominator is small relative to its sampling variation. A particular sample can have a first-stage covariance closer to zero than expected, or even one with the opposite sign.

Dividing by a value near zero can produce an arbitrarily large positive or negative coefficient. The numerator changes with the denominator, so the exact distribution is not simply the ratio of two independent normal variables, but the central numerical danger remains: the denominator has substantial probability mass near zero.

The mean of a ratio estimator can be exceptionally unstable. In the classical just-identified normal model, the finite-sample distribution can have sufficiently heavy tails that ordinary moments do not exist in the usual sense. A finite simulation always produces a numerical sample mean, but that does not automatically make the mean a stable measure of the estimator's expected performance.

The weak valid design illustrates this problem unusually clearly. The median is close to one, yet the Monte Carlo mean is 1.1290 and the RMSE is 3.2620. A handful of extreme estimates contributes disproportionately to squared error and can pull the mean far from the central mass.

This is why a claim such as 'IV is almost unbiased on average in our simulations' needs caution when identification is weak. Mean and variance summaries may be dominated by tail realizations, and small changes in the number of replications or seed can alter them dramatically. The median and quantiles are informative supplements, not replacements for a proper weak-identification analysis.

## Dispersion across the four designs

The fifth and ninety-fifth percentiles of the IV estimates provide more information about the sampling distribution:

| Design | IV 5th percentile | IV 95th percentile | IV RMSE |
| --- | ---: | ---: | ---: |
| Valid, strong | 0.9243 | 1.0657 | 0.0434 |
| Valid, moderate | 0.7137 | 1.2030 | 0.1525 |
| Valid, weak | −0.1855 | 1.6510 | 3.2620 |
| Invalid, strong | 1.2299 | 1.3639 | 0.3021 |

Strong valid IV has a relatively concentrated distribution around one. The moderate instrument has a noticeably wider interval of empirical estimates while remaining informative in this setting.

The weak design's fifth-to-ninety-fifth percentile range already includes negative values, despite a true positive effect. It does not convey the full tail severity: the RMSE of 3.2620 reflects realizations far outside this central ninety-percent range. The contrast between quantiles and RMSE is an important diagnostic of heavy-tailed behaviour.

For the invalid strong instrument, estimates are tightly concentrated around approximately 1.3. Its RMSE relative to the causal effect is about 0.3023, mainly reflecting the exclusion-induced discrepancy rather than sampling noise. This design can produce precisely estimated answers that are systematically wrong for the intervention question.

An analyst reporting the invalid instrument's tight interval without discussing the exclusion pathway would give readers a false sense of causal certainty. An analyst reporting a conventional weak-IV interval without examining its reliability could make an entirely different mistake: treating an unstable ratio as if normal large-sample approximations were adequate.

## What the first-stage F-statistic tells us

The first-stage F-statistic describes how strongly the instrument predicts treatment in the specified regression. For the strong valid and strong invalid instruments it averages about 500. The moderate valid instrument averages about 47.7. The weak valid instrument averages about 5.7, with a median around 5.4.

A first-stage F near 500 makes the estimated denominator of the IV ratio relatively stable in these samples. It does not distinguish the strong valid and strong invalid scenarios, because the treatment equation is equally relevant in both. The difference lies in the outcome equation, where the invalid design contains the direct effect δZ.

The familiar rule of thumb that an F-statistic above 10 indicates a sufficiently strong instrument is a rough diagnostic associated with particular models and inferential concerns, not a universal threshold of causal validity or correct coverage. Different numbers of instruments, forms of heteroskedasticity, endogenous regressors and desired tolerances for bias or test distortion require more careful assessment.

Stock and Yogo (2005), Staiger and Stock (1997), and subsequent weak-identification literature provide more systematic ways to understand the relationship between first-stage evidence and the performance of conventional instrumental-variable inference. The relevant criterion depends on what failure one is trying to guard against.

The simulation deliberately uses a single instrument and endogenous treatment, avoiding many-instrument complications. Even there, the contrast between F≈5.7 and F≈500 demonstrates the value of inspecting relevance while also proving that relevance cannot establish exclusion.

## What exclusion means in a real design

The exclusion restriction states that the instrument's effect on outcomes operates through the designated endogenous treatment, rather than through another causal pathway. In the linear experiment it is represented by δ=0. In an empirical application the restriction often requires a much richer institutional argument.

For example, distance to a service provider might influence treatment take-up. It might also affect travel costs, time availability, access to other services or unrecorded economic opportunities. A school assignment policy might affect educational attainment but also peer groups and access to resources. A regulation may affect adoption of one programme and simultaneously change incentives associated with another.

None of these possibilities proves a proposed instrument invalid without further investigation. They identify potential pathways that researchers must address with subject-matter knowledge, design details, negative-control outcomes or complementary evidence. Exclusion is not established by declaring a candidate variable 'external' or 'exogenous' in ordinary language.

It is also not established by a very small p-value in the first stage. Predictive power tests whether the instrument is associated with treatment; the exclusion restriction concerns the causal origin of its association with outcomes.

The invalid simulation provides a precise representation: δ=0.3, π=1, so the IV estimator consistently targets 1.3 rather than one. If the direct pathway were negative, bias would move in the other direction. Without an argument about that pathway, a stable coefficient cannot be safely interpreted as a causal treatment effect.

## Exogeneity and independence

Exclusion is only one part of instrumental-variable identification. A proposed instrument can influence the outcome only through treatment and still be invalid if it is associated with unmeasured causes of the outcome.

Suppose provider preference predicts whether a patient receives a treatment, but providers with different preferences also see patients of different underlying severity. The preference instrument could be associated with latent prognosis. Unless the design addresses that association, the IV ratio would not isolate the treatment effect even if provider preference had no direct biological effect.

In the simulation, Z, U, V and ε are independent by construction. This makes exogeneity exact and isolates the other failure modes. We therefore cannot use these results to assess robustness to instrument-outcome confounding or imperfect measurement of the instrument.

The distinction between random assignment of an instrument and random assignment of treatment is also important. An encouragement design can randomize an offer or incentive while leaving treatment uptake voluntary. Randomizing encouragement can make the instrument exogenous, but using it to infer treatment effects still requires assumptions about how encouragement affects outcomes and how individuals respond to it.

These assumptions are causal statements about the system, not consequences of the regression used to fit the first stage. The statistical procedure implements an identification strategy only after its underlying design is justified.

## Local average treatment effects

The constant treatment effect in this experiment makes the IV target especially simple. In many real binary-treatment settings, treatment effects vary between people and the instrument changes treatment for only some of them. Under appropriate instrumental-variable assumptions, including a monotonicity condition, the familiar Wald ratio can identify a local average treatment effect among individuals whose treatment status responds to the instrument.

That is a different estimand from the average effect across the entire population. Compliers may differ systematically from people who would always take treatment or never take it. A treatment effect identified for compliers should not automatically be transported to the entire eligible population.

Imbens and Angrist (1994) formalized local average treatment effect identification, while Angrist, Imbens and Rubin (1996) developed the treatment-assignment framework in which compliance types make the interpretation explicit. The relevance, exclusion and independence assumptions remain essential, and monotonicity rules out certain forms of opposite responses to the instrument.

Our instrument and treatment are continuous. The experiment therefore does not identify discrete compliance classes or calculate a local average treatment effect. It estimates a constant structural coefficient under a linear data-generating model.

The broader lesson is that instrument validity is not the only question. One must also ask which causal quantity the instrument-induced variation can identify, especially when treatment effects are heterogeneous.

## Natural experiments are not automatically randomized

The phrase natural experiment can refer to events or institutional rules that create treatment variation outside the direct control of individuals. It is useful when a policy threshold, allocation procedure or unexpected change gives some units different exposure from others. Yet natural variation is not necessarily random with respect to potential outcomes.

An administrative rule might be correlated with eligibility, geography or economic circumstances. A sudden policy change might occur in response to anticipated outcomes. A provider's preference might be associated with other aspects of service quality. These considerations are not minor corrections to the standard error; they concern identification itself.

A credible natural experiment needs an explicit assignment mechanism. Researchers should explain why the variation is plausibly exogenous, which units are affected, whether treatment alternatives remain comparable, and how outcomes might respond through other channels. The instrument's first-stage coefficient is only one piece of that evidence.

The same principle applies to quasi-random allocation by timing, distance or institutional boundaries. Sometimes a discontinuity design, difference-in-differences or direct intention-to-treat analysis is better suited to the question than translating the design into an IV ratio. The appropriate method depends on the treatment, intervention and estimand, not on a preference for one family of estimators.

The simulation's instruments are generated independently by construction. That makes them idealized benchmarks for statistical behaviour, not evidence that any named real-world natural experiment satisfies the same assumptions.

## Weak-instrument-robust inference

When an instrument is weak, conventional Wald confidence intervals around a two-stage least squares estimator may not have their stated coverage. Their approximation relies on enough first-stage information for the estimator to behave approximately normally around its target. A ratio whose denominator can approach zero violates the intuition behind that approximation.

Alternative procedures include Anderson–Rubin tests and confidence sets, which test hypotheses about the structural treatment coefficient without relying on the same strong-first-stage approximation. Depending on the model, weak-identification-robust procedures can produce wide, unbounded or disconnected confidence sets. Such results may be unsatisfying to readers expecting a short interval, but they can faithfully express limited identification.

The present benchmark does not compute Anderson–Rubin confidence sets, conditional likelihood ratio tests or weak-IV-robust coverage. Its purpose is to make the instability visible in raw estimator distributions. A publication-quality extension should include a comparison of ordinary and weak-IV-robust inference across the same simulated designs.

If the resulting confidence set is broad or unbounded, that is not necessarily a failure of the inferential procedure. It may be an appropriate statement that the data contain insufficient exogenous treatment variation to determine the effect precisely.

The scientific response to weak identification is not simply to report a stronger-looking first-stage statistic from another specification. It is to evaluate the validity of the alternative instrument, the causal estimand it identifies, and the uncertainty remaining under an appropriate procedure.

## Multiple instruments and overidentification

An analyst may have several candidate instruments for one endogenous treatment. Additional instruments can improve relevant variation, but they also introduce additional assumptions and potential finite-sample problems. If some instruments violate exclusion or exogeneity, adding them may make the estimate more precise around the wrong target.

Overidentification tests compare implications of having more instruments than endogenous treatment variables. They can detect some forms of disagreement among instruments under a maintained set of assumptions. They cannot establish that every instrument is valid. Several invalid instruments can agree because they share a similar violation, and rejection does not automatically identify which exclusion pathway is responsible.

Weak-instrument problems can also become more complicated with many instruments. Conventional two-stage least squares may exhibit finite-sample bias toward ordinary least squares in certain settings, and diagnostic thresholds depend on instrument count and model structure.

The simulation uses one instrument, so there is no overidentification test and no instrument-selection exercise. That makes the mathematical interpretation clean: the moment condition either identifies the treatment effect under relevance and exclusion, fails numerically because relevance is weak, or identifies a shifted coefficient under exclusion violation.

The existence of more candidate variables should not be confused with more independent causal evidence. Validity is an assumption about each proposed source of variation, not a reward for including a larger instrument matrix.

## Why the median and RMSE disagree

In the weak valid design, the Monte Carlo median estimate of about 1.0346 is almost exactly the structural effect. Yet its mean is 1.1290 and its RMSE is nearly 8.75. This combination would look contradictory if one assumed that an estimator with a good typical value must have low average squared error.

The discrepancy is mathematical. RMSE squares the error before averaging. A small number of enormous estimates can dominate this calculation. The sample mean can also be pulled strongly by a few tail observations. The median is resistant to their magnitude and therefore describes the centre of the realized distribution more faithfully here.

But the median is not a cure for weak identification. It does not provide a confidence interval or establish that the instrument contains sufficient exogenous treatment variation. It simply prevents the size of the most extreme ratios from dominating one descriptive statistic.

Reporting multiple summaries is therefore essential. The median, fifth and ninety-fifth percentiles, mean, RMSE and first-stage distribution each reveal different aspects of what happened. A histogram or empirical cumulative distribution would make the heavy tails especially clear, provided the plot does not hide extreme values with arbitrary axis clipping.

A result should not be called accurate merely because its median is close to the known truth in one experiment. The full estimator distribution and the identification assumptions determine whether it can support reliable empirical conclusions.

## Connection with the preceding articles

The first article showed that an ordinary regression coefficient can converge precisely to an associational parameter different from the causal effect. Part 2 showed how random assignment can remove systematic dependence between treatment and prognosis. Parts 3 and 4 examined propensity-score methods under measured confounding and weak overlap. Part 5 showed how conditioning on the wrong variable can introduce bias. Part 6 examined violations of the counterfactual trend required by difference-in-differences.

Instrumental variables add another source of identification: variation in treatment caused by an exogenous instrument. The strength of the method is that the instrument need not make the full observational treatment assignment ignorable. Its limitation is that instrument exogeneity and exclusion must be justified for the variation actually used.

The strong invalid-instrument result connects directly to the earlier warning about statistical precision. With F≈500, IV estimates are concentrated near 1.3, although the causal effect is one. The weak valid-instrument result shows a different problem: even a correct population identification identity can yield a deeply unstable finite-sample estimator.

These are not reasons to prefer ordinary regression by default. OLS remains biased across the constructed designs. They are reasons to separate the *source of identification* from the *behaviour of the estimator given that source*. Both are necessary for a defensible causal claim.

## Practical evaluation of a proposed instrument

Begin with the intervention and estimand. What treatment is being changed, by which candidate instrument, and in which population? If treatment effects vary, whose effect is identified by instrument-induced changes? Those questions should precede the first-stage regression.

Describe the assignment process. Why does the instrument change treatment? What determines the value of the instrument? Which measured and unmeasured outcome causes might also be related to it? A causal diagram can help state plausible pathways, but the arrows require institutional or scientific justification.

Assess relevance quantitatively and use diagnostics appropriate to the model, instrument count and desired inferential procedure. Inspect the first-stage coefficient and its uncertainty; do not reduce the assessment to a universal threshold. Weak instruments require caution with ordinary IV point estimates and standard errors.

Evaluate exclusion through substantive knowledge. List plausible direct pathways from the instrument to the outcome and consider whether available evidence constrains them. Negative controls, placebo outcomes and alternative instruments can help expose some violations, but cannot prove exclusion in general.

Finally, compare the identified IV estimand with the decision question. An effect among compliers, local to a threshold or driven by one institutional mechanism may not represent the average effect of a population-wide intervention. A causal estimate can be valid for the variation that generated it and still be insufficient for a broader policy choice.

## Limitations

This benchmark uses a linear additive structural outcome equation, independent Gaussian variables, one continuous instrument, one continuous endogenous treatment and a constant treatment effect. The same model determines all four scenarios, with only relevance and a direct instrument pathway varied. That simplicity provides analytical population coefficients but cannot represent every instrumental-variable design.

The strong invalid scenario violates exclusion by adding a direct effect of Z to Y. It does not violate independence of Z from the latent disturbances. Real instruments may violate both conditions or encounter treatment-outcome feedback, interference, sample selection and measurement error. Those additional failures are outside this experiment.

The simulation uses 500 datasets of 1,000 observations per scenario. Extreme weak-IV estimates can make Monte Carlo moments highly sensitive to the number of replications and seed. Reporting a numerical mean and RMSE does not imply that the corresponding theoretical moments are well behaved. That qualification is especially important for the weakest instrument.

The first-stage F-statistic is computed for a single-instrument linear model with an intercept. This is not a substitute for diagnostics designed for heteroskedastic settings, multiple endogenous variables or many instruments. Nor does this article provide formal weak-IV-robust confidence sets.

The experiment is not an empirical evaluation of job-search support, educational attainment or any other real intervention. It demonstrates what the specified estimators do under known mechanisms. Deciding whether a proposed instrument is credible in a substantive application remains a separate research task.

## Conclusion

A valid strong instrument recovered the known effect of one, with a Monte Carlo mean of approximately 0.9984 and RMSE 0.0434. A valid moderate instrument remained informative but less precise. A weak valid instrument produced a median almost exactly equal to one while its mean and RMSE were distorted by extreme ratio estimates. A strong invalid instrument produced precise estimates around 1.3 because exclusion failed.

The two failure modes demand different interpretations. Weak relevance creates numerical and inferential instability. Exclusion violation changes the population quantity estimated by the IV ratio. The first can often be diagnosed through properties of the first stage and the estimator distribution; the second requires causal evidence about alternative pathways from instrument to outcome.

A strong first-stage F-statistic does not establish a valid causal design, and a plausible identification identity does not guarantee stable finite-sample estimation. Both identification and inference matter. The next investigations will examine designs that use discontinuities and other localized treatment variation, where the same obligation remains: define the counterfactual, justify why it is identified, and only then estimate its effect.
