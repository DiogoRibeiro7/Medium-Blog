# Where to Put the Learned Correction in a Hybrid Model: Outputs, Inputs and Structure on a Simulated Contact Centre

*A queueing model, three ways of adding machine learning to it, and a world whose truth is known, used to find out which correction fixes what.*

The usual pitch for a hybrid model goes like this. A mechanistic model knows how the system works but gets the details wrong. A machine-learning model gets the details right but knows nothing beyond its data. Put a learned correction on top of the mechanism and you get both: the theory carries you into regimes the data never covered, and the learner mops up whatever the theory missed. The pitch has a long pedigree. Psichogios and Ungar combined a first-principles process model with a neural network in 1992, Kennedy and O'Hagan gave the statistical version its standard form in 2001, and the idea now travels under names such as residual physics (Zeng and co-authors' robot that learns to throw objects), theory-guided data science (Karpatne and co-authors) and universal differential equations (Rackauckas and co-authors). Willard and co-authors surveyed the field for engineering and environmental systems in 2023.

The pitch skips a question that decides whether it works: where does the correction go? A learned function can be added to the model's outputs, to one of its inputs, or inside its structure as a new mechanism whose size the data are asked to estimate. These are not three implementations of one idea. They learn from different parts of the data, they fail in different regimes, and they ask different things of the person building them.

This piece measures the three on the simulated contact centre I used in an earlier article to compare mechanistic and data-driven synthetic data generators. That comparison left a specific gap. A queueing model, Erlang A, fitted the observed days well but was badly optimistic when demand surged, and the reason turned out to be a mechanism the fit to the interval table could not reveal: callers who hang up and call again. The question here is what each kind of learned correction can do about that gap, and what it costs.

The code, results and figures are in the folder [hybrid-discrepancy-generators](https://github.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/tree/main/hybrid-discrepancy-generators) of my Medium-Blog repository, and every number below is printed by one script into `results/summary.txt`.

## The centre, the table and the gap

The centre in the simulation is open from eight to eight on weekdays, and everything about it is reported in half-hour intervals. Fresh calls arrive as a Poisson process whose rate follows an intraday profile, a weekday effect and announced campaign days, plus a shock common to the whole day (with a coefficient of variation of 10%) and a smaller one for each half hour. Handle times are log-normal with a mean of five minutes. Patience is a mixture: a fifth of callers hang up after about twenty seconds, and the rest have log-normal patience with a median of 200 seconds, which puts the overall mean at 224 seconds. Six in ten callers who hang up call again, on average ten minutes later, and the platform counts the second attempt as a new offered call. The planners staff each half hour by the square-root rule on their forecast, revise the plan during the day when volume runs above or below it, and lose about one agent in twenty to absence. Calls are answered first come, first served, and the whole thing is simulated call by call.

The analyst sees none of that, only the table the platform exports: for each day and half hour, the weekday, the interval, the campaign flag, the offered calls, the agents, the abandonment share, the service level (the share of calls answered within twenty seconds) and the mean wait per offered call. Erlang A, the Palm/Erlang-A queue that Mandelbaum and Zeltyn describe in detail, is calibrated from that table in the textbook way. The service rate comes from the mean handle time. The patience rate θ comes from the number of abandoned calls divided by the total time callers spent waiting, which is its maximum-likelihood estimate when patience is exponential; on ninety days of data that puts the mean patience at about 185 to 188 seconds. To produce whole days, the queue is given a Poisson demand model fitted to the offered calls, multiplicative in interval, weekday and campaign, and the company's own staffing rule applied to the planners' own forecast.

That model has four things wrong with it, and the earlier article found that they do not matter equally. Swapping the true handle-time distribution or the true patience mixture into Erlang A changed its forecast for a 30% demand surge by less than half a percentage point. Adding the day-level demand shock helped somewhat. Adding redials closed most of the gap, and adding both closed it entirely. In the observed regime, though, versions of Erlang A with redial probabilities from zero to 0.9 were indistinguishable to a classifier, because with abandonment around 5% the redials add about 3% to the offered calls and the demand model absorbs them. A mechanism that is dormant where the data are and dominant where the decisions are: that is the gap a learned correction is supposed to fill.

## Three places to put a correction

The first place is the outputs. Kennedy and O'Hagan's formulation writes each observation as the simulator's output at its calibrated parameters, plus a discrepancy function of the inputs, plus noise. Their version also scales the simulator by an unknown factor and gives both the simulator and the discrepancy Gaussian-process priors; the version here keeps only the part that matters for the comparison. For a half hour, the simulator's output is Erlang A's expected service level, abandonment share and mean wait given that half hour's offered calls and agents, computed by replaying the day through the queue six times. The discrepancy is learned from the residuals, observed minus expected, using the calendar, the offered calls, the agents, the load per agent and Erlang A's expectation itself as inputs. Nothing about the missing mechanisms needs to be known: the learner finds whatever pattern the residuals have.

Higdon and co-authors developed it into a working method for combining field data with simulator runs, and it remains a standard way of calibrating a computer model. Three learners are used here, because what an output correction does outside the data is set entirely by how its learner extrapolates. Gradient boosting is piecewise constant, so beyond the last split it carries the correction found at the edge of the data unchanged. A Gaussian process with zero prior mean and a squared-exponential kernel has a predictive mean that is a weighted sum of kernel values between the new point and the training points, and those kernel values decay to zero far from the data (this follows from the predictive equations in Rasmussen and Williams' textbook), so the correction fades away there and the hybrid reverts to Erlang A. A ridge regression continues whatever trend it found. A fourth variant applies the boosting correction on the logit scale for the two shares and on the log scale for the wait, so that the corrected values cannot leave their ranges.

The second place is the inputs. The demand model handed to Erlang A has no day-to-day variation beyond the calendar, while real days ran between about 25% below and 30% above their calendar mean in the training data. The simplest data-driven repair is to resample each simulated day's level from the day levels in the data: divide each training day's offered calls by what the demand model expects for that weekday and campaign status, and draw a simulated day's multiplier from those ratios. This is a learned correction too, but it sits upstream of the queue, so its effect passes through the queue's dynamics rather than around them.

The third place is the structure. If the analyst suspects that callers redial, the model can be given a slot for it, a redial probability p and a mean delay, and the data can be asked how large p is. The obvious way of asking fails, because the fit of the model to the table barely depends on p. A statistic that does depend on p has to be designed. Redials leave a fingerprint in the order of events within a day: a half hour in which many callers hung up should be followed by a half hour with more offered calls than the calendar predicts, because some of those callers are calling back. The auxiliary statistic is therefore the within-day slope of surplus offered calls in one half hour on the number of calls abandoned in the half hour before, with each day's expected calls rescaled to its observed total so that day-level shocks drop out. The slope is computed on the real table, and on 600 simulated days from Erlang A with redials at each of six probabilities between zero and one, with the same random numbers at every probability so that the simulated slope moves smoothly with p. The probability whose simulated slope matches the real one is the estimate. This is indirect inference in the sense of Gouriéroux, Monfort and Renault, and the map from p to the simulated slope is what they called the binding function. The delay is not estimated. The analyst assumes ten minutes, which happens to be right, and a later section shows what a wrong assumption does.

Two more generators complete the comparison: the input correction and the structural slot together, and, for prediction, a gradient-boosted regression of the outcomes on the inputs with no mechanism at all. Every hybrid is fitted on the same ninety days of interval data unless I say otherwise.

## On the observed days, nobody wins

The first test is the one most hybrid-modelling papers report: predict held-out data in the regime the data cover. For each of six replicate histories, every model is trained on the first n days and asked to predict forty held-out days, half hour by half hour, given each half hour's calendar, offered calls and agents. The score is the root-mean-square error of each day's service level across the forty days. A reference that no model can be expected to beat is reality's own queue, with its log-normal handle times and its patience mixture, replayed on the same inputs. It scores 0.0296. That is the noise floor at this level of aggregation: a half hour's offered calls and agents leave a lot about its service level undetermined.

Every model is within a few thousandths of that floor. Erlang A scores between 0.0279 and 0.0289 whatever the length of the training data, because its two parameters are pinned down within a few days. Boosting with no mechanism needs data: it scores 0.0365 with ten days and 0.0320 with thirty, draws level with Erlang A at ninety days (0.0286 each), passes it at 270 (0.0271) and reaches 0.0268 with 810 days. The hybrid with a boosting correction follows boosting rather than Erlang A: 0.0294 with ten days, 0.0307 with thirty, and 0.0268 with 810. In this regime it has the worst of both until the data are plentiful, and then it has exactly what the data-driven model has. The linear correction stays within a thousandth of Erlang A at every length. The Gaussian-process correction is the best of all with ten days (0.0266) and gets worse as the data grow, reaching 0.0306 with 810. That is partly an artefact of design: exact Gaussian-process regression is cubic in the number of rows, so it was fitted on 400 rows drawn from the training data, and with more data those 400 rows include more of the rare, extreme half hours, which pull the fitted length scales shorter. Approximate Gaussian processes would avoid this, at a computational cost the other learners do not have.

![Fit in the observed regime](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/hybrid-discrepancy-generators/figures/fig1_fit.png)

*Figure 1. Left: RMSE of the day's service level on forty held-out days, predicted from each half hour's inputs, by days of training data (mean over six histories); the dotted line is reality's own queue given the same inputs. Centre: the correction each learner adds to Erlang A's service level along a line of increasing load per agent, after ninety days of training; grey discs are the mean residuals in the training data by load bin, sized by the number of half hours, and the shaded band holds 98% of the training half hours. Right: discriminator AUC of forty generated days against forty held-out days, after 90 days (open) and 810 days (filled) of training; the dotted line is the value two independent real samples give.*

The logit-scale variant is the one clear loser, at between 0.0338 and 0.0379. The reason is the gap between the mean of a transform and the transform of a mean. The residual on the logit scale is the logit of the observed service level minus the logit of Erlang A's expectation, and many observed half hours sit at a service level of exactly one, which the transform pushes far out to the edge it is clipped at. The learner fits the average of those residuals, and transforming back a correction fitted to an average of transformed values does not reproduce the average correction. The two scales give different answers to the same question, and the additive one was the right one here.

Output corrections also break the accounting that the mechanism respects. A service level cannot exceed one minus the abandonment share, because calls answered within twenty seconds are a subset of calls answered; shares live between zero and one, and waits are non-negative. Erlang A's outputs satisfy all of that by construction, because they are computed from simulated calls. A correction added to them does not know about any of it. Before clipping, the boosting hybrid's predictions broke one of these rules in 10.5% of half hours with ten days of training, falling to 0.6% with 810 days as the correction became more precise; the Gaussian-process hybrid did so in 3% to 6% of half hours, and the linear one in about 15% to 17% at every length. Clipping repairs each row, but the need for it is information. An output correction is a statistical patch on a model whose outputs have internal structure, and the patch has to be told about the structure.

## What each correction does past the edge of the data

The centre panel of Figure 1 shows what the corrections learned, and it explains most of what follows. The grey discs are the mean residuals of the service level in the training data, binned by load per agent. They are close to zero, within about one percentage point, up to a load of one; above it they turn negative, to about −0.04 between 1.1 and 1.3 and −0.08 in the thirteen half hours above 1.3. Erlang A is optimistic exactly where the centre is overloaded, by a few points, in a thin slice of the data: 98% of training half hours have a load per agent between 0.60 and 1.29, and the highest observed was 1.61.

Beyond that slice the three learners do what their mathematics says. Boosting settles at a constant correction of about −0.07 from a load of 1.5 onwards. The Gaussian process makes the largest corrections inside the overloaded slice, about −0.15 to −0.20, and then returns to zero beyond 1.6, which is where its kernel stops seeing training data. The ridge regression keeps going and reaches −0.33 at a load of 2.0. Three defensible learners, trained on the same residuals, disagree by up to thirty percentage points of service level at a load twice the usual, and nothing in the training data says which of them is right. The choice of learner is an extrapolation assumption, and in an output correction it is the only one there is.

## A correction that makes generated days less realistic

The right panel of Figure 1 asks the question synthetic-data practitioners care about: used as a generator, does the hybrid produce days that look like real days? For each method, forty days are generated and pooled with forty held-out real days, and a gradient-boosted classifier tries to tell them apart; the score is its out-of-fold AUC, and two independent real samples give 0.54.

Erlang A's days score 0.593 after ninety days of training and 0.589 after 810. Every output correction makes them easier to spot: 0.657 and 0.652 with boosting, 0.644 and 0.678 with the Gaussian process, 0.660 and 0.658 with the linear correction, and 0.665 and 0.644 on the logit scale. The input correction is close to Erlang A (0.604 and 0.600). The structural slot improves on it, 0.576 and then 0.555 with 810 days, and the two together score 0.579 and 0.555. A tabular synthesizer, sequential CART in the style of synthpop, starts behind (0.619) and catches up with the most data (0.540).

The output corrections lose ground because they were learned for the wrong object. A discrepancy is fitted to the residuals of an expectation, while a generator produces draws. Adding a correction for the mean to a stochastic draw shifts every draw by the same amount, whatever the draw was. Draws at a boundary, such as a quiet half hour with nobody waiting and nobody lost, were left at the boundary, because a correction to an average says nothing about them. Draws just inside it were pushed across and clipped back, and between 9% and 14% of generated half hours needed clipping. The result is a distribution whose mean has moved slightly towards the data and whose shape has moved away from it. A classifier sees the shape. Kennedy and O'Hagan's discrepancy is a device for prediction and calibration, and nothing in their formulation says how to turn it into a generator of individual realisations; the obvious way to do so makes the realisations worse.

## A surge

The second test is the one the hybrid pitch is about. Fresh demand is raised by a factor m between 0.9 and 1.5, without the planners being told, and each method is run as a generator over sixty days of the same calendar. Figure 2 shows the pooled service level, averaged over five replicate histories.

![Demand surge](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/hybrid-discrepancy-generators/figures/fig2_surge.png)

*Figure 2. Pooled service level when fresh demand is m times normal, for reality (black) and each method run as a generator, mean over five histories with sixty days per point. Left: output corrections. Right: the input correction, the structural slot and both; the dotted blue line is Erlang A replayed on reality's own surge days, given their offered calls and staffing. The band marks the range of 90% of training days' demand relative to the calendar.*

At m = 1.3 the real service level is 0.448. Erlang A says 0.604. The output corrections leave that almost untouched: 0.594 with boosting, 0.600 with the Gaussian process and 0.595 with the linear correction. At m = 1.5, where reality is at 0.195, Erlang A says 0.364 and the corrections say 0.335, 0.358 and 0.370. Boosting does a little better than the others because its constant correction at the edge of the data is the right sign; the Gaussian process returns to Erlang A as its mathematics says it should; and the linear correction, which in the centre panel of Figure 1 was heading steeply down, is slightly worse than Erlang A at the largest surge. That last point looks like a contradiction and is not one. The ridge regression's correction depends on the offered calls and the agents as well as the load, and in a surge the staffing rule adds agents, so the generated half hours do not move along the line drawn in Figure 1.

The other three methods are in a different league. The input correction alone moves Erlang A only modestly (0.582 at m = 1.3), because more varied days do not change the average load much. The structural slot alone brings the forecast to 0.487 at m = 1.3 and 0.170 at m = 1.5, and the two together give 0.440 and 0.185 against a real 0.448 and 0.195. The abandonment shares tell the same story at the largest surge: reality loses 30.3% of calls, Erlang A says 18.2%, the output corrections between 17.5% and 18.8%, and the slot with the input correction 33.2%. The redial probabilities estimated from the five histories' training data were 0.53, 0.61, 0.63, 0.65 and 0.73.

The dotted line in the right panel shows where Erlang A's error lives. Replayed on reality's own surge days, given the offered calls and staffing those days actually had, Erlang A predicts 0.457 at m = 1.3 and 0.208 at m = 1.5, within about a point of reality. The queue model is nearly right. What is wrong is the number of calls it puts into the queue, because it has no way of knowing that a surge produces more abandonment and more abandonment produces more calls. An output correction is trained on residuals computed with the real offered calls supplied, where Erlang A has almost nothing to correct, and applied in a generator that produces the wrong offered calls itself. It is learned in the one place where the error is absent and asked to fix the one place where it is concentrated.

## A parameter the correction makes meaningless

The third test reproduces, on this centre, a result that Brynjarsdóttir and O'Hagan established in 2014: when a model with physical parameters is given a flexible discrepancy, the parameters and the discrepancy are confounded, and the data cannot separate them without prior information about the discrepancy. Tuo and Wu drew the same conclusion from a different direction: they showed that the calibration parameter in Kennedy and O'Hagan's framework is not identifiable, and replaced it with a target that is, the parameter that minimises the L2 distance between the simulator and the physical process. Plumlee proposed constraining the discrepancy to be orthogonal to the simulator's gradient with respect to its parameters, so that the correction cannot imitate a change of parameter.

Here Erlang A's mean patience is fixed at each of six values between 100 and 450 seconds instead of being estimated, and the boosting and Gaussian-process corrections are learned on top of each version. Figure 3 shows what happens to the fit to forty held-out days and to the forecast for a 30% surge, averaged over three histories.

![Confounding of the patience parameter and the correction](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/hybrid-discrepancy-generators/figures/fig3_confounding.png)

*Figure 3. Erlang A with its mean patience fixed at each value on the horizontal axis, alone and with a learned correction, mean over three histories. Left: RMSE of the day's service level on forty held-out days. Right: pooled service level forecast for a 30% surge, with reality's value in black. Vertical lines: the calibrated value from the ratio estimator and reality's mean patience.*

Without a correction the data identify the parameter. Erlang A's held-out error is lowest at 190 seconds (0.0288), next to the ratio estimates of 185 to 188 seconds, and rises steeply on both sides, to 0.0585 at 100 seconds and 0.0725 at 450. With a correction the curve is flat. With boosting, the error is between 0.0266 and 0.0282 across the whole range; with the Gaussian process, between 0.0269 and 0.0309. A model that believes callers wait 100 seconds on average and a model that believes they wait 450 fit the held-out days equally well, because the correction absorbs whatever the parameter gets wrong. A data scientist looking at those held-out scores would conclude that patience does not matter, which is the opposite of true.

The right panel shows the consequence, and it is not the one a reader of Brynjarsdóttir and O'Hagan might expect. The hybrids' surge forecasts do not scatter with the parameter. They are pinned: between 0.578 and 0.587 with boosting and between 0.581 and 0.604 with the Gaussian process, against a real 0.450. The correction learned how much to undo each version's patience on normal days, and it carries that undoing into the surge, so the parameter has no influence anywhere. Erlang A alone does scatter, from 0.705 at 100 seconds down to 0.458 at 450, and its forecast at 450 seconds is close to reality for the wrong reason: a patience twice the true mean keeps callers in the queue, inflates the load and imitates the effect of the redials the model lacks. That version fits the observed days worst of all six. Calibrating on fit would never choose it, and a wrong parameter compensating for a missing mechanism in one regime is not a model anyone should trust in another.

## Sizing a mechanism the fit cannot see

The structural slot is the only correction that changed the surge forecast materially, so it deserves the most scrutiny. Figure 4 shows how its estimate behaves.

![Estimating the redial probability](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/hybrid-discrepancy-generators/figures/fig4_redials.png)

*Figure 4. Left: the binding function for one ninety-day history, the auxiliary slope computed on 600 simulated days at each redial probability (dots), smoothed by a quadratic (line); the horizontal line is the slope in the real table, and the shaded band is the slope in a world without redials, mean plus or minus two standard deviations. Right: estimated redial probability by days of data, mean and standard deviation over eight histories, with the delay assumed correctly (purple), wrongly (red), and in a world without redials (grey). The true probability is 0.6.*

The auxiliary slope is a weak signal but a clean one. Across eight histories it averages 0.236 with ninety days of data, with a standard deviation of 0.053, and 0.259 with 810 days. In a world identical except that nobody redials, it averages −0.039, with a standard deviation of 0.033 at ninety days. The two distributions barely overlap even at ninety days, so the table does carry the information the fit could not reveal. In the earlier article I wrote that the data could not tell you whether redials exist. That was too strong: the fit of the model cannot, but a statistic designed for the mechanism can.

The estimates follow. With the delay assumed correctly, the estimated redial probability is 0.558 with thirty days of data (standard deviation 0.149), 0.577 with ninety (0.077), 0.598 with 270 (0.041) and 0.610 with 810 (0.020), converging on the true 0.6. In the world without redials it is 0.018 with ninety days (0.052) and 0.001 with 810: the slot switches itself off when the mechanism is absent. The binding function in the left panel, from one of the histories, shows why the estimator is noisy with little data. The simulated slope rises from about zero at p = 0 to 0.586 at p = 1, slowly at first, so a small error in the observed slope moves the estimate a long way when p is small or moderate. The history plotted is one of the less favourable draws, with an observed slope of 0.148 and an estimate near 0.4.

The delay is where the analyst's knowledge enters, and it can be wrong. Assuming redials come after five minutes instead of ten raises the estimate to 0.697 with ninety days and 0.716 with 810; assuming twenty minutes lowers it to 0.522 and 0.565. The direction is what the statistic implies. The slope only sees redials that land in the half hour after the abandonment that caused them. With a five-minute delay, more of each half hour's redials land in that same half hour, out of the statistic's sight, so the simulator needs a larger p to produce the observed slope; with twenty minutes, more land in the next half hour, and a smaller p is enough. The forecast moves less than the estimate. Using each estimate in the slot-plus-input generator, the service level forecast for a 30% surge is 0.418 and 0.402 with the five-minute assumption, 0.463 and 0.480 with ten minutes, and 0.477 and 0.459 with twenty, against a real 0.451. A wrong shape gets a size that partly compensates for it, and the forecast stays within about five points of reality. That is an observation about this mechanism and this question, not a guarantee. A question that depended on exactly when the redials arrive within the day, rather than on how much extra load they add over a surge, would be more exposed to the delay assumption.

Two things about this result need saying plainly. The first is that the slot works because it is the right slot. I chose redials because the earlier experiment, which could switch each of reality's mechanisms on and off, showed that redials were what mattered, and an analyst with only the table would have to get that idea from somewhere else: the operations literature, where Aguir and co-authors studied how retrials affect call-centre performance and Ding, Koole and van der Mei estimated true demand in centres with redials; a look at call-level logs; or a conversation with the people who answer the phones. The second is that the auxiliary statistic was designed for the mechanism. A generic statistic, or the fit itself, would not have found it. A structural correction is cheap in data and expensive in thought. This is the grey-box tradition of system identification, set out for process engineering in Bohlin's book, in which the analyst writes down the structure and the data are asked only for the numbers; Sargsyan, Huan and Najm's embedded model error does something similar for calibration, placing the error inside the model's parameters rather than adding it to the outputs.

## Staffing changes and a staffing decision

Two final tests ask what the hybrids are for. The first adds or removes k agents in every half hour, on top of the staffing rule, and compares each method's predicted change in the service level with the change obtained by replaying reality's own callers with the extra agents. The second repeats the decision from the earlier article: a launch will raise demand by a factor drawn uniformly between 1.1 and 1.3 on each launch day, and management will add k agents to every half hour and wants the day's service level to reach 70% on at least 80% of launch days. Each method picks the smallest k that meets the requirement in its own simulated launch days, with each day's surge drawn from the stated range, and the choice is scored on 300 real launch days.

![Staffing effect and staffing decision](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/hybrid-discrepancy-generators/figures/fig5_intervention.png)

*Figure 5. Left: change in pooled service level when k agents are added to every half hour, each method used as a generator over forty days with the same random numbers at every k; reality replays the same callers. Mean over five histories. Right: share of launch days meeting the 70% target, by agents added, as each method simulates it and as reality delivers it (black); circles mark the k each method chooses.*

On the staffing effect the pattern of the surge repeats. Removing six agents from every half hour lowers the real service level by 0.400. Erlang A says 0.335, the boosting and Gaussian-process hybrids 0.349 and 0.355, and the input correction 0.335. The structural slot says 0.381, and with the input correction 0.392. Adding six agents raises the real service level by 0.161; the slot with the input correction says 0.162, and Erlang A and the output hybrids about 0.141 to 0.143. The abandonment effects line up the same way: cutting six agents raises real abandonment by 14.3 points, Erlang A says 11.3 and the slot with the input correction 15.1. The redial path, from fewer agents to more abandonment to more calls, is the one that makes staffing cuts hurt more than a queue without redials expects, and only the methods that have the path in them can follow it.

In the launch decision, reality needs k = 4, which meets the target on 82.7% of launch days at a cost of 48 extra agent-hours a day. Erlang A chooses k = 1 and meets the target on 60.3% of real launch days. The boosting hybrid chooses k = 2 (69.7%), the input correction k = 3 (74.7%) and the structural slot alone k = 2 (69.7%). The slot with the input correction chooses k = 5, meets the target on 88.7% of days, and pays 12 agent-hours a day more than necessary. It is the only method on the safe side of the requirement, and it gets there by being slightly pessimistic about the launch: with a redial probability estimated at 0.68 on this history, a little above the truth, it expects a little more redialling than reality delivers.

The more interesting comparison is between the slot alone and the slot with the input correction. Alone, the slot gets the average launch day almost right, but its days are all alike, because its demand model has no day-to-day variation. The requirement is a share of days, and a share of days depends on how bad the bad days are. In this decision the input correction contributes what the slot cannot, the spread of days, and the slot contributes what the input correction cannot, the response of demand to congestion. Neither is enough alone, which is the same conclusion the earlier ablation reached by switching reality's mechanisms on and off, now reached with mechanisms estimated from the table.

## A diagnostic for where the error lives

The surge result suggests a check that does not need a simulated world to run, only the model and the data, and it is worth spelling out because it decides which of the three corrections is worth trying first.

A mechanistic generator can be run in two modes. In replay mode it is handed the real inputs, here each half hour's offered calls and agents, and asked for the outputs. In generation mode it produces its own inputs from its demand model and its staffing rule and then the outputs. Comparing each mode with the data separates two kinds of error. If the model is wrong in replay mode, the mapping from inputs to outputs is wrong, which is the case an output correction is built for. If it is right in replay mode and wrong in generation mode, the mapping is fine and the inputs it generates are wrong, and the correction has to go upstream.

At normal demand the two modes already disagree, though by little. Erlang A replayed on real days' inputs gives a pooled service level of 0.802 against a real 0.797, while as a generator it gives 0.830. That gap of about three points is the first sign that the trouble is in the inputs, and it is visible in normal data, before any surge. The surge then makes it large: seventeen points in generation mode at m = 1.5 against about one point in replay mode. A data scientist who had computed only the replay-mode residuals, which is what fitting an output correction involves, would have seen a model that is nearly right and a correction with little to do, and would have been surprised by the surge.

The check also says something about Brown and co-authors' finding that Erlang A predicted a real bank's call centre well although its patience was clearly not exponential. Replay mode is where that finding lives. In this experiment, too, swapping the true patience mixture into Erlang A barely changed its forecasts, which suggests that, given the offered load and the staffing, the queue's behaviour depends on the patience distribution mostly through features the calibration gets right. It says nothing about generation mode, where the question is how the offered load itself responds to the queue. A model can pass the first test with honours and fail the second, and the second is the one that decides staffing for a surge.

The structural slot was sized by a statistic designed to see the response of offered calls to congestion, and the design choices in it each address a specific way that response could be confused with something else. Expected calls are rescaled to each day's total so that a busy day, which has both more abandonment and more calls for reasons unrelated to redials, does not produce a slope by itself; the placebo world, with a slope of −0.039, shows how well that worked. Only the previous half hour's abandonments enter, because abandonments in the same half hour are partly caused by the calls being counted, which would bias the slope upwards. The simulated tables go through exactly the same calculation as the real one, so any bias that remains, from the staffing rule's reaction to volume, for instance, appears on both sides of the comparison and cancels. And the same random numbers are used at every redial probability, so that the binding function is smooth enough to invert. Each estimate takes under half a minute on the machine that ran the experiments, almost all of it spent simulating 3,600 days. None of these choices is clever; each one is necessary, and none of them would have occurred to anyone who had not first named the mechanism.

## What each place costs

The three corrections differ less in their sophistication than in what they require and where they fail.

An output correction requires nothing but residuals, and that is its appeal: no knowledge of what is missing, no new mechanism, no simulation-based estimation. In this experiment it bought almost nothing in the regime it was trained in, because Erlang A given the real inputs was already near the noise floor; it made generated days less realistic, because a correction for a mean is not a correction for a draw; it left the surge forecast where it was, because it was learned where the error was absent; and it made the patience parameter unidentifiable, so that the model's one interpretable quantity stopped meaning anything. Its extrapolation was decided by the choice of learner, and three reasonable learners disagreed by up to thirty points of service level at a load twice the usual. Where an output correction earns its keep is where the simulator's error is in the outputs given the inputs and the question stays inside the data: interpolation in a regime the data cover densely, with a learner whose behaviour at the edge has been chosen deliberately. Kennedy and O'Hagan's framework is a calibration and prediction framework, and it is best used as one.

An input correction requires knowing which input the mechanism gets wrong and having data on it. Here it was cheap, a few dozen day-level ratios, and it is the kind of correction a practitioner can defend in one sentence. It did little on its own for the average surge and mattered for the decision, because the decision depended on the spread of days. Its limit is that it resamples what has been seen: the day levels it draws from are the day levels of normal operation, and a surge is not one of them.

A structural slot requires a hypothesis about the missing mechanism and a statistic that can see it. Both came from outside the table, and in a real centre both could be wrong. In return it was the only correction that moved the surge forecast, the staffing effect and the staffing decision towards reality, it switched itself off in a world without the mechanism, and its one parameter stayed interpretable: a redial probability can be checked against call-level logs, a boosted residual cannot be checked against anything. Its error under a wrong shape assumption was modest here, for a reason specific to the question asked.

Box wrote in 1976 that since all models are wrong, the scientist must be alert to what is importantly wrong. The three corrections are three ways of acting on that, and they need different amounts of alertness. They are not exclusive, and the best generator in every test was a combination of an input correction and a structural slot. An output correction on top of both would be the natural next step, and the results above suggest how it should be built: learned from residuals of the generator rather than of the given-inputs replay, applied on a scale that respects the outputs' accounting, with a learner whose extrapolation has been chosen rather than inherited, and with the physical parameters held at values fixed outside the correction so that the correction cannot absorb them.

## What this experiment does not show

The referee is a simulator I wrote. It is richer than every model fitted to it, and none of its parameters were given to the analyst, but the conclusions are about this centre. In particular, the structural slot was the right slot because I knew which mechanism mattered. The experiment tests whether the table can size the mechanism once it has been named, and what a wrong shape does; it does not test what a slot for the wrong mechanism does, which in practice is the more common failure. The output corrections were trained on residuals of Erlang A given the observed inputs, which is the standard formulation but not the only one; a correction trained on generator-level residuals, matching whole simulated days to whole real days, is a harder estimation problem and was not attempted. The Gaussian process was fitted on 400 rows. And everything here concerns a queue whose mechanistic core is close to right; in a domain where the core is poor, the balance between the three corrections could be different.

Two conjectures, stated as conjectures. The first is that output corrections in hybrid models help most when the simulator's error lives in the mapping from inputs to outputs and least when it lives in how the simulator generates its own inputs, which is the case whenever the system has feedback from outcomes to demand. Queues with redials are one instance; epidemics where behaviour responds to prevalence and markets where order flow responds to prices are others. The second is that, for a dormant mechanism, the information needed to size it is usually present in the observed data but not in the statistics a fit uses, so that the work of a structural correction is less in adding the mechanism than in designing the statistic that can see it.

## Reproducing the results

The code is in [github.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/tree/main/hybrid-discrepancy-generators](https://github.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/tree/main/hybrid-discrepancy-generators) and reuses the contact-centre simulator in the neighbouring folder `mechanistic-vs-data-driven-generators`. The hybrids are in `src/hybridgen`, with tests of each component against cases with a known answer. `experiments/run_all.py` runs the six experiments and writes `results/summary.txt`, which contains every number quoted here; it takes about an hour on two cores, and `--quick` runs everything at small sizes in a few minutes, which is what CI does on every change. All randomness flows from one master seed.
