# How Long New Hires Stay, by Age at Hire: Survival From the Start Date in Four Decades of U.S. Job Records

## Survival curves, restricted mean tenure, censoring bounds and a calibrated career model, built from the NLSY79 job-duration tables, including a change of estimand between two releases of the same table

Among the reasons given for hesitating over a candidate in their fifties, one has the form of a forecast. The hire will not stay long enough. Retirement is closer, the reasoning goes, so whatever the employer spends on recruiting, onboarding and training will be recovered over fewer years than it would be with a candidate of thirty. Most of the other reasons in that family are judgements about people, and they are hard to test because they are vague. This one is a statement about a random variable: the time between the day a person is hired and the day that person leaves the employer. It can be wrong in a measurable way, and the data needed to check it exist.

What follows treats the forecast as a survival-analysis problem. The question is not whether older workers are loyal, or whether younger workers change jobs a lot, but something narrower and more useful: for a job that starts when the worker is a given age, what is the distribution of the time until that job ends, and what does that distribution imply for an employer who has to recover a fixed cost within some horizon? The answer is built from the tables the U.S. Bureau of Labor Statistics publishes from the National Longitudinal Survey of Youth 1979, a single cohort of Americans born between 1957 and 1964 whose every job has been recorded with its start and end dates since 1978. Those tables give, for jobs started at each age, the share that ended within one, two, five, ten and fifteen years. Six releases of the same table, from 2015 to 2025, follow the cohort from their late forties into their sixties, which is exactly the age range the forecast is about.

Three findings come out of the analysis, and they are of different kinds. The first is substantive: at every horizon the data reach, jobs started at 45 to 58 last longer than jobs started at 25 to 34, by a wide margin in the first two years, which is the window in which hiring costs are usually recovered. The second is about how far the data can be pushed: past the last published cell, the comparison only reverses if the older hires leave at rates several times higher than the rates they actually showed, and in one case only if all of them leave at once. The third is methodological, and it would change the answer to other questions asked of the same table: between the 2019 and the 2021 releases the published shares for the same cohort and the same jobs fell by eight to thirteen points, because the statistic changed from a share of jobs to an average over workers, and that change was announced only by a change in wording. An employer needs the first of those two statistics, not the second.

The rest of the article builds these results in order: the quantity the question needs, the data, the change of estimand, the survival curves, the censoring near the survey date, the bounds beyond the data, the cost of keeping a position filled, a career model that reproduces both versions of the table, the cross-sectional statistic that is usually quoted instead, and what none of this can say.

## The quantity the question needs

Let T be the time a hire stays with the employer, measured from the start date, and let a be the worker's age on that date. The survival function S_a(t) is the probability that a job started at age a is still in progress t years later. The hazard h_a(t) is the rate at which jobs still in progress at t end, per unit of time. Everything an employer can reasonably want to know about retention is a functional of S_a.

The functional that matters for a hiring decision is rarely the mean or the median of T. An employer who spends a fixed amount on a hire recovers it over the time the hire stays, but only up to the point where the employer stops counting: the horizon of a training programme, the payback period a finance department uses, the life of the project the person was hired for. The natural summary is the restricted mean, the expected time with the employer within the first H years after the hire, which is the area under the survival curve from 0 to H. In clinical trials this quantity is called the restricted mean survival time and is recommended precisely because it answers a question with a stated horizon, does not require the curve to reach zero, and does not depend on hazards being proportional (Royston and Parmar, 2013). For a hiring decision it has a direct reading: if the hire is worth a fixed amount per year of presence once trained, the restricted mean within H is proportional to the value recovered within H.

A second functional answers a related question. An employer who needs a position filled for H years, and who will refill it every time the incumbent leaves, cares about the expected number of hires that takes, because each hire carries the fixed cost again. If every replacement is drawn from the same survival curve, that number is one plus the renewal function of the distribution of T, a classical object of renewal theory (Cox, 1962). It depends only on the distribution of T up to H, which matters below, because the published tables stop at different horizons for different ages.

Two features of the question restrict which data can answer it. The clock starts at the hire. A cross-section of current employees, with the time each has been with the employer so far, describes jobs that have already survived to the date of the survey, and long jobs are over-represented in it because they are in progress on more dates; this is the length-biased sampling that renewal theory calls the inspection paradox. And the unit is the hire. An employer samples job starts, not people: a worker who changes jobs four times in ten years appears four times among an employer's hires and only once among the workers of an economy. A statistic that gives each worker one vote answers a different question from one that gives each job start one vote, and the difference turns out to be large.

## The records: one cohort, followed week by week

The National Longitudinal Survey of Youth 1979 started with a nationally representative sample of 12,686 young men and women who were 14 to 22 years old when first interviewed in 1979 (BLS, 2004). They were surveyed every year until 1994 and every two years since. The survey records the start and end dates of every job, which lets the Bureau of Labor Statistics construct a week-by-week work record for each respondent from 1 January 1978 to the most recent interview. A job is defined as an uninterrupted period of work with a particular employer, so the definition is employer-based rather than position-based, and a person who leaves an employer and later returns is counted as starting a new job (BLS, 2025a). In the 2022–23 round, 6,413 people were interviewed, a retention rate of 64 percent of the original sample and 75 percent of those still living; the published results are weighted to correct for the original oversampling, for nonresponse and for permanent attrition.

Every two years the Bureau releases the same set of tables, among them Table 2, the duration of employment relationships with a single employer by age at the start of the job. For each band of starting ages it gives the cumulative percent of jobs that ended in less than one, two, five, ten and fifteen years, and the percent of jobs still in progress at the latest interview. Cells that most of the cohort was not yet old enough to complete are left blank. In the 2025 release, the bands are 18–24, 25–34, 35–44, 45–54 and 55–58; for the last two the ten- and fifteen-year cells are blank, and for 55–58 the five-year cell as well.

Because this is one cohort, age at hire and calendar time move together. Jobs started at 25 to 34 began between 1982 and 1999; jobs started at 45 to 54, between 2002 and 2019, a period that includes the 2008–09 recession; jobs started at 55 to 58, between 2012 and 2022. A difference between age bands is therefore also a difference between periods, and nothing in a single cohort can separate the two. A later cohort, the NLSY97, born 1980–84, has reached its late thirties and is used below only as a check on the ordering at younger ages.

For the comparison with the statistic that is usually quoted, the Bureau's biennial report on employee tenure gives the median number of years that employed wage and salary workers have been with their current employer, from the January supplement of the Current Population Survey. In January 2026 the medians were 1.5 years at ages 20–24, 3.0 at 25–34, 4.7 at 35–44, 7.0 at 45–54, 9.6 at 55–64 and 9.9 at 65 and over (BLS, 2026a). They have barely moved in a decade: the 25–34 median was 2.8 in 2016 and the 55–64 median was 10.1.

All the published values used here were transcribed from six NLSY79 releases (2015, 2017, 2019, 2021, 2023 and 2025), one NLSY97 release (2026) and the 2026 tenure report, and every value was checked against two renderings of the same release, the HTML table and the PDF.

## Two releases, two statistics

Read side by side, the six releases of Table 2 should agree wherever they overlap. The cohort is the same, the jobs are the same, and a job started at 18 to 24, between 1978 and 1989, was long finished by the 2016–17 interview behind the 2019 release. The only reason a cell should move between releases is new follow-up for jobs started near the survey date, plus small changes in the weights as attrition proceeds.

The cells do agree within each half of the series. The share of jobs started at 18 to 24 that ended within a year is 69.1 percent in the 2015 and 2017 releases and 70.1 percent in 2019. It is 60.8 percent in 2021 and 2023 and 60.7 percent in 2025. Between 2019 and 2021, the same cell falls by 9.3 points. The same happens in every band. For jobs started at 25 to 34 the first-year share goes from 53.0 to 41.9 percent, a fall of 11.1 points; for 35 to 44, from 35.8 to 25.6, a fall of 10.2 points; for the oldest band, from 29.4 percent (jobs started at 45 to 52, in the 2019 release) to 21.5 percent (45 to 54, in 2021), a fall of 7.9 points. At two years the falls are 8.9, 12.1, 13.3 and 11.6 points. Before and after the break, the series are stable to within a point.

The text of the releases changes at the same point. The 2015, 2017 and 2019 releases describe the statistic as a share of jobs: of the jobs that workers began at 18 to 24, 69 percent ended in less than a year. From 2021 on, the sentence becomes a statement about workers: the average worker had 61 percent of those jobs end in less than a year. No release states that the method changed. The most economical reading is that the earlier releases pool all job starts in an age band and compute the share that ended within t years, while the later releases compute that share separately for each worker and then average the shares across workers. This is an inference from the wording and the magnitude, and the career model below tests it.

The two statistics differ for a reason that does not require workers to differ from each other. Take any population in which everyone faces the same exit hazard, and look at jobs started within a ten-year age band. A worker whose first job in the band ends after three months has nine and three-quarter years left to start another; a worker whose first job lasts eight years has two. Within a fixed window, the number of jobs a worker starts is negatively correlated with the durations of those jobs, so the people with many starts are, mechanically, the people whose jobs were short. Pooling the starts weights each worker by the number of starts and therefore over-represents short jobs relative to an average in which each worker counts once. The effect is a cousin of the inspection paradox, acting on counts instead of lengths.

The size of that effect can be computed. The career model described later in the article, run with workers made essentially identical (a frailty variance of 0.001) and calibrated only to the pooled cells, produces worker-averaged first-year shares of 61.0 percent for jobs started at 18 to 24, 43.1 for 25 to 34, 26.7 for 35 to 44 and 20.8 for 45 to 54. The published worker-averaged values in 2025 are 60.7, 41.7, 25.3 and 21.4. The averaging rule alone accounts for the break to within one and a half points in every band. Figure 1 shows both halves of the argument: on the left, the published first-year share release by release, with the break between 2019 and 2021; on the right, for each band, the pooled value, the published averaged value and what averaging produces when workers are identical.

For the question in this article, the pooled statistic is the right one. An employer's hires are a sample of job starts. The probability that a particular hire, made at a particular age, will still be there after a year is the share of job starts at that age that lasted a year, with each start counted once. A worker-averaged share answers a question about workers, such as how a typical person's jobs in their forties turned out, and it is the right statistic for that question. Both versions are used below, because the pooled one stops at 52 and the averaged one reaches 58, but the distinction is kept explicit throughout, and the conclusions are checked under each.

There is a lesson here that has nothing to do with age. A published series can change its estimand without changing its title, its column headers or its table number, and the only trace can be a sentence in the text. A pipeline that ingests the 2019 and the 2021 tables as consecutive observations of one quantity will report a ten-point improvement in job stability that never happened. The defence is cheap: compare overlapping cells across vintages before using them, because cells describing completed events in the distant past should not move.

## Survival from the start date

With the estimand settled, the published cells give points on S_a(t) at one, two, five, ten and fifteen years. Between those points the analysis assumes a constant hazard, so the curve is piecewise exponential; this is the simplest interpolation that reproduces the published values exactly, and it affects only the restricted means, not any published survival value. Figure 2 shows the resulting curves under both estimands.

![Figure 2](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/tenure-by-age-at-hire/figures/fig2_survival_from_hire.png)

*Figure 2. Share of jobs still in progress t years after the start, by age at the start of the job. Left: pooled over jobs (2019 release). Right: averaged per worker (2025 release). Points are published cells; lines assume a constant hazard between them. Source: BLS, NLSY79 Table 2; author's calculations.*

Under the pooled statistic, of every hundred jobs started at 25 to 34, 47.0 were still in progress after one year, 30.4 after two, 14.6 after five, 7.9 after ten and 5.5 after fifteen. Of every hundred started at 45 to 52, 70.6 were in progress after one year and 53.7 after two, where the published series for that band stops. The 35–44 band sits in between, with 64.2 after one year, 46.2 after two and 25.2 after five, and the youngest band, 18 to 24, is far below everyone, with 29.9 after one year and 16.5 after two. The median duration of a job started at 25 to 34 is 0.92 years; at 35 to 44, 1.76 years; at 45 to 52 it exceeds the two years the table reaches.

Under the worker-averaged statistic every curve is higher, as the previous section implies, and the ordering is the same. After one year, 58.3 percent of jobs started at 25 to 34 are in progress, against 74.7 percent at 35 to 44, 78.6 percent at 45 to 54 and 76.2 percent at 55 to 58. After two years, 42.8 percent against 60.0, 65.5 and 61.8. After five, 26.0 percent at 25 to 34 against 44.1 at 45 to 54. The worker-averaged median is 1.50 years at 25 to 34, 3.32 at 35 to 44 and 4.05 at 45 to 54.

The ratio that most directly answers the employer's forecast is the first-year exit share. Under the pooled statistic, jobs started at 25 to 34 end within a year 1.80 times as often as jobs started at 45 to 52; jobs started at 18 to 24, 2.38 times as often. Under the averaged statistic the corresponding ratios against the 45–54 band are 1.95 and 2.84.

The 55–58 band deserves a sentence of its own, because it is the closest the data come to the hire the forecast is about. Its first-year exit share, 23.8 percent, is a little higher than that of the 45–54 band, 21.4 percent, and its two-year share, 38.2 percent, a little higher than 34.5. Both are far below the 25–34 values of 41.7 and 57.2. Something happens at the end of working life, and it shows up as a slight rise in early exits, but it is small next to the difference between hires in their late fifties and hires in their late twenties.

The later cohort points the same way where it can be compared. In the NLSY97 release of 2026, 61.4 percent of jobs started at 18 to 24 ended within a year, against 32.4 percent of jobs started at 25 to 38 (BLS, 2026b). The age bands do not match those of the earlier cohort, so the levels cannot be compared, but the ordering by age at hire is the same two decades later.

## Where the difference sits: the first two years

The interval hazards show how the curves separate. Under the pooled statistic, jobs started at 25 to 34 end at a rate of 0.755 per year in their first year, 0.436 in the second, 0.244 between years two and five, 0.123 between five and ten and 0.072 between ten and fifteen. Jobs started at 45 to 52 end at 0.348 per year in the first year and 0.274 in the second. Under the averaged statistic, the first-year hazard is 0.540 at 25 to 34 and 0.241 at 45 to 54, a ratio of 2.2; between years two and five it is 0.166 against 0.132, a ratio of 1.26. Most of the difference between hires of different ages is concentrated in the first two years, and by years two to five the hazards are close.

Two facts about these hazards are worth stating, because both shape the interpretation. First, in every age band the hazard falls with tenure: the longer a job has lasted, the less likely it is to end in the next year. This is one of the most stable regularities in labour economics. With monthly data from the NLSY itself, Farber (1994) found that the hazard of a job ending rises over the first three months and declines steadily after that; the yearly cells used here are too coarse to see the early peak, and the constant hazard assumed within the first year averages over it. Second, a falling hazard has two explanations that a table of this kind cannot separate. One is genuine duration dependence: a match is an experiment, the worker and the employer learn whether it is good, and the matches that survive the learning period are the good ones (Jovanovic, 1979). The other is sorting: some workers leave jobs quickly and some do not, and as time passes the jobs still in progress belong increasingly to the second kind, so the hazard of the pool falls even if no individual hazard does (Blumen, Kogan and McCarthy, 1955; Vaupel, Manton and Stallard, 1979). Heckman and Singer (1984) showed how hard it is to tell the two apart from duration data alone.

For an employer the distinction matters less than it seems, because both mechanisms produce the same practical conclusion: the first year is where hiring money is lost. If 41.7 percent of hires at 25 to 34 leave within a year and 21.4 percent at 45 to 54, the employer of the younger hire pays the fixed cost of a hire again within the year about twice as often. The age difference is largest exactly where the money is.

For the restricted means, the expected time with the employer within the first two years after the hire is, under the averaged statistic, 1.27 years for a hire made at 25 to 34, 1.54 at 35 to 44, 1.61 at 45 to 54 and 1.56 at 55 to 58. Under the pooled statistic it is 1.08 years at 25 to 34, 1.35 at 35 to 44 and 1.46 at 45 to 52. Within five years, under the averaged statistic, it is 2.29 years at 25 to 34 and 3.23 at 45 to 54. A hire made in the mid-forties delivers, within five years, about 40 percent more time with the employer than a hire made in the late twenties or early thirties.

## Jobs started near the survey date

The cells for the oldest bands are the ones most exposed to censoring. A job started at 58 by someone interviewed shortly after turning 59 can have been observed for barely a year, and if it is still in progress at the interview it cannot be counted among the jobs that ended within two years, even if it is about to end. The published tables do not correct for this. They count completed jobs that ended within t years as a share of all jobs started, with jobs still in progress in the denominator, and they leave a cell blank only when most of the cohort could not have completed it. For the cells that are published, censoring biases the share of jobs that ended downwards, and the question is how much.

The answer can be bounded without any model of who leaves, using only the timing of the survey. Birth years in the cohort run from 1957 to 1964. Suppose births are spread evenly over those years, interviews evenly over the 2022–23 round, and starting ages evenly within each band, and apply the band's own exclusion rule: the 55–58 band leaves out anyone not yet 59 at the interview. Then the share of job starts in the band with less follow-up than a cell requires is a matter of arithmetic. For the 55–58 band, 1.8 percent of starts had less than one year of follow-up and 7.1 percent less than two. For the 45–54 band, 2.6 percent had less than five years, and none had less than two. Widening the assumed interview window to the whole of 2022 and 2023 changes the five-year figure to 2.7 percent and leaves the others unchanged.

If every one of those insufficiently observed jobs was still in progress at the interview and would have ended before the threshold, the true share would be the published share plus the short-follow-up share; that is the worst case, and the true share cannot be higher. This is a bound in the sense of partial identification (Manski, 2003): it uses only what the design guarantees and makes no assumption about the jobs that were not observed long enough. The two-year exit share of the 55–58 band is therefore at most 45.3 percent instead of the published 38.2; its one-year share at most 25.6 instead of 23.8; and the five-year share of the 45–54 band at most 58.6 instead of 55.9. Even at these upper bounds the oldest hires leave less often than hires made at 25 to 34, whose shares are 41.7 percent at one year, 57.2 at two and 74.0 at five. The 45–54 cells at one and two years are not affected at all.

The vintages give a second, empirical check. The first-year share for jobs started from age 45 was 21.5 percent in the 2021 release, 21.4 in 2023 and 21.4 in 2025, and the two-year share 34.7, 34.9 and 34.5, while the band's follow-up grew by four years and its upper age moved from 54 to 56 and back. If censoring were biasing those cells, more follow-up would have raised them. Survey attrition is a different matter: a respondent who stops being interviewed takes the rest of his or her work history along, the weights correct for this only on observed characteristics, and nothing in a published table can check whether people who drop out of the survey also leave their jobs at different rates.

## Past the last published cell

The tables stop where the cohort's age stops them: at five years for hires made at 45 to 54 and at two years for hires made at 55 to 58 under the averaged statistic, and at two years for hires made at 45 to 52 under the pooled one. An employer with a ten-year horizon wants more than that, and this is where the retirement argument has its force. A person hired at 50 who stays seven years is 57; one hired at 56 who stays seven years is 63. The data cannot say what happens next. They can say how bad it would have to be.

Three scenarios bracket the restricted mean beyond the last cell. In the worst case, every hire still in the job when the data stop leaves on that day; the restricted mean then stops growing at that point. In the most favourable case, nobody leaves afterwards, and it grows linearly. In between, the last observed exit rate simply continues. None of these is a forecast; the worst case is not even plausible, since it assumes that a group with a falling hazard suddenly empties. Its use is that any conclusion that survives it is safe. Figure 3 shows the restricted mean as a function of the horizon, under the averaged statistic, with the worst case and the continued exit rate drawn beyond the last cell for the two oldest bands.

*Figure 3. Expected years with the employer within the first H years after the hire, by age at the start of the job, under the worker-averaged statistic. Solid lines use published cells only. Beyond the last cell of the two oldest bands, the dashed line continues the last observed exit rate and the dotted line assumes every remaining hire leaves at once. Source: BLS, NLSY79 Table 2 (2025); author's calculations.*

Start with hires made at 45 to 54. Within ten years, their restricted mean is 3.23 years in the worst case, 4.84 if the last observed exit rate continues and 5.44 if nobody leaves after year five. A hire made at 25 to 34 delivers 3.31 years within ten. So even if every 45–54 hire still present after five years walked out on the morning of the fifth anniversary, the older hires would deliver within ten years almost exactly what the younger hires deliver. A cleaner way to state this is a break-even exit rate: the constant rate at which the 45–54 hires present at five years would have to leave for their ten-year restricted mean to fall to that of the 25–34 hires. That rate is 5.36 per year, which means that more than 99 percent of them would have to be gone within twelve months. Over fifteen years the comparison becomes possible: the break-even rate is 0.589 per year, 44 percent of the remaining hires leaving every year, against an observed rate of 0.132 per year in years two to five.

Against hires made at 35 to 44 the margin is much thinner. Within ten years the 35–44 hires deliver 4.59 years, more than the worst case of the older group and slightly less than its continued-rate scenario, and the break-even exit rate for the 45–54 hires is 0.211 per year, 19 percent of the remaining hires per year, about 1.6 times the rate observed in years two to five. Within fifteen years it is 0.136 per year, close to the observed rate. Whether a hire at 50 beats a hire at 40 over ten or fifteen years depends on what happens after the data stop, and the data do not say. The robust comparison is with hires in their late twenties and early thirties.

For hires made at 55 to 58 the data stop at two years, with 61.8 percent still in place and an observed exit rate of 0.209 per year in the second year. Within five years, a 25–34 hire delivers 2.29 years. The 55–58 hires reach that only if the ones still present at two years leave at 0.771 per year from then on, 54 percent of them every year, 3.7 times the rate they showed in their second year. Taking the one- and two-year shares at their censoring upper bounds, the break-even rate becomes 0.577 per year, 1.9 times the correspondingly adjusted second-year rate. Within ten years, the break-even rate against the 25–34 group is 0.328 per year, 28 percent of the remaining hires annually. Against hires made at 35 to 44, the 55–58 hires deliver more within five years only if their exits after year two stay below 0.172 per year, a little under the 0.209 they showed in their second year; within that horizon the comparison with the 35–44 group is close to even.

The same exercise under the pooled statistic, with data stopping at two years for hires made at 45 to 52, gives a break-even rate of 2.006 per year against the 25–34 group over five years, 87 percent of the remaining hires leaving every year, and 0.657 per year over ten. Against the 35–44 group the break-even rates are 0.405 and 0.277 per year.

Put together, the bounds give a statement that does not depend on any assumption about retirement. Within every horizon the data cover, five years for hires made at 45 to 54 and two years for hires made at 55 to 58, and under either statistic, the older hires are expected to stay with the employer longer than hires made at 25 to 34. For hires made at 55 to 58 over five years, the reversal needs exit rates 3.7 times those observed in the second year (1.9 times at the censoring bounds). For horizons of ten to fifteen years, it needs the older hires to leave at rates between about one and a half and four and a half times those they showed while the data could see them, or, for hires in their mid-forties over ten years, to leave all at once.

## The cost of keeping a position filled

The restricted mean treats a single hire. An employer usually thinks about positions: the job has to be done for the next five years, and every departure means another round of recruitment. The expected number of hires needed to keep one position filled for H years, with each replacement drawn from the same survival curve, depends only on the curve up to H, so for horizons the data cover it needs no assumption beyond the interpolation between cells.

Within two years, under the pooled statistic, a position filled by hires made at 25 to 34 takes 2.34 hires on average; filled by hires made at 45 to 52, it takes 1.64. Under the averaged statistic the figures are 1.93 for hires at 25 to 34, 1.43 for hires at 45 to 54 and 1.49 for hires at 55 to 58. Within five years, under the averaged statistic, 2.76 hires at 25 to 34 against 1.90 at 45 to 54; under the pooled one, 3.70 hires at 25 to 34 and 2.66 at 35 to 44. A position filled by hires made at 18 to 24 needs 3.18 hires within two years and 5.53 within five under the pooled statistic.

Beyond the published cells this needs the model of the next section, which extends the curves to retirement. Figure 4 shows the result for all bands up to ten years. Under the model, keeping a position filled for ten years takes 5.61 hires at 25 to 34, 3.84 at 35 to 44, 3.33 at 45 to 54 and 3.74 at 55 to 58; at 18 to 24, 8.96. A position staffed with hires in their late twenties and early thirties costs 1.43 times as many hires as one staffed with hires in their late forties and early fifties within two years, 1.57 times within five and 1.68 times within ten. Against hires made at 55 to 58 the ratios are 1.34, 1.56 and 1.50. If the fixed cost of a hire is C, the difference over a decade is about two and a quarter C per position against the 45–54 group and almost two C against the 55–58 group, before anything is said about productivity.

*Figure 4. Expected number of hires needed to keep one position filled for H years, if every replacement is hired at the same age. The 55–58 curve bends upwards after about five years, where retirement begins to dominate, rises above the 45–54 curve and approaches the 35–44 curve by ten years; it stays far below the 25–34 curve throughout. That is what the retirement argument looks like when it is measured: a real effect, visible at long horizons, and smaller than the turnover of younger hires, since even at ten years a position staffed from the 55–58 band needs almost two hires fewer than one staffed from the 25–34 band.

## A model that reproduces both tables

Two of the steps above needed something the published tables do not give directly: the pooled statistic for ages the 2019 release does not reach, and the curves beyond the last published cell. Both come from a career model, and since a model can make any table look like anything, it has to earn its use by reproducing cells it was not fitted to.

The model follows simulated people from age 18. Each job ends at a rate that depends on the person's age when the job started (the five BLS bands) and on how long the job has lasted (constant within the intervals zero to one, one to two, two to five, five to ten, ten to fifteen and more than fifteen years). Each person carries a multiplicative frailty drawn once from a gamma distribution with mean one, so some people leave every job faster than others, in the spirit of mover–stayer models. Between jobs there is a gap, exponential with a mean of 0.35 years, and each person leaves the labour force at an age drawn from a normal distribution with mean 64 and standard deviation 3, kept between 55 and 75. Observation ends at each person's 2022–23 interview, birth years are spread over 1957–64, and the BLS exclusion rules for the youngest and oldest bands are applied. The model then computes both statistics the BLS publishes, the pooled share and the worker-averaged share, exactly as the two kinds of release define them.

The model was fitted to 23 published numbers. Twenty are interval cells: the pooled cells of the 2019 release for jobs started at 18 to 24 and 25 to 34 at all five horizons, at 35 to 44 at one, two and five years, and at 45 to 52 at one and two; the averaged cells at ten and fifteen years for 35 to 44, at five years for 45 to 54, and at one and two years for 55 to 58. Two are the shares of jobs still in progress at the survey for the two oldest bands, 20.8 and 37.6 percent, which carry the only information about exits after the last duration cell. The last one is the averaged first-year share at 25 to 34, which pins down the frailty variance. The largest error on a fitted number is 0.72 points.

The test is the 14 published cells the model was not fitted to: the averaged cells at every horizon for 18 to 24, at two to fifteen years for 25 to 34, at one, two and five years for 35 to 44, and at one and two years for 45 to 54. The mean absolute error on those cells is 1.04 points and the largest is 2.60, for jobs started at 18 to 24 at five years. Two looser checks also hold. The model's share of jobs still in progress at the survey is 0.9, 2.5 and 7.7 percent for the three younger bands, against 1.5, 4.0 and 9.7 published; and the number of jobs held per person within each age band, a statistic the model was never shown, comes out at 5.2, 4.4, 3.0, 2.5 and 1.5 against the published 5.6, 4.5, 2.9, 2.2 and 1.3.

A first version of the model failed these checks, and the way it failed was informative. It assumed that, beyond the last pooled cell, the exit rate of each band stayed at its last observed value; it then ended too many of the jobs started at 35 to 44 within ten years and left far too few of them in progress at the survey. The published ten- and fifteen-year cells say that exit rates keep falling well after five years of tenure, and the version used here is fitted to them.

Three results of the model are worth stating separately. The frailty variance that reproduces the gap between pooled and averaged statistics is 0.056, which is small: the averaging rule alone explains nearly all of the gap, as shown earlier, and within this model the gap leaves little room for large, lifelong differences between movers and stayers. That conclusion is specific to the model; heterogeneity that varies over the life cycle, or that is correlated with age at hire, would not show up in this parameter. Second, the calibrated base exit rates beyond the last cell, before frailty and on top of retirement, are 0.071 per year after five years of tenure for jobs started at 45 to 54 and 0.052 per year after two years for jobs started at 55 to 58. Third, the published share of 55–58 jobs still in progress cannot be reached if retirement happens on average at 62: with that assumption, even with no exits other than retirement after two years of tenure, the model leaves at most 32.3 percent of those jobs in progress at the survey, against 37.6 percent published. Within the model, the data on ongoing jobs are not compatible with exits concentrated in the early sixties.

The decision quantities are insensitive to the two assumptions the tables cannot pin down. With the mean gap between jobs set to 0.2 or 0.6 years, or the mean retirement age set to 62 or 66, and everything recalibrated, the ten-year restricted mean of a 45–54 hire stays between 3.83 and 3.89 years, that of a 55–58 hire between 3.37 and 3.59, and that of a 25–34 hire at 2.28 to 2.29; the hires needed to keep a position filled for ten years stay between 3.28 and 3.35 for the 45–54 band, between 3.66 and 3.86 for the 55–58 band and between 5.53 and 5.55 for the 25–34 band.

Under the model, followed to retirement and weighted by hires, 47.0 percent of jobs started at 25 to 34 are still in progress after one year, 14.7 percent after five and 7.9 percent after ten. For jobs started at 45 to 54 the figures are 70.3, 29.7 and 17.5 percent; for 55 to 58, 65.9, 32.3 and 6.0 percent. The ten-year figure for the oldest band is where retirement shows: ten years after a hire at 55 to 58 the worker is 65 to 68, past the model's mean retirement age.

## The statistic that is usually quoted

The figures on job tenure that circulate most widely are cross-sectional: older workers have a median of almost ten years with their current employer, workers in their late twenties and early thirties about three. Read naively, these numbers support the conclusion of this article, and that is the problem with them. Up to the mid-forties they would look much the same if hire durations did not depend on age at all.

A cross-section of current jobs is a length-biased sample of all jobs. A job that lasts ten years is in progress on ten times as many survey dates as a job that lasts one year, so it is ten times as likely to be caught by a survey, and the time it has lasted so far, when it is caught, is on average half its length. In a stationary population of jobs with survival curve S, the tenure of a job found in progress has density proportional to S itself (Cox, 1962). On top of that, a worker's tenure cannot exceed the time since that worker started working, so the young are mechanically confined to short tenures. Neither effect has anything to do with how long a job started at a given age lasts. Hall (1982) used exactly this kind of cross-sectional table to infer the importance of long jobs over a working life, which is a legitimate use; inferring from it how long a new hire will stay is not.

The career model makes the point concrete. Figure 5 shows the CPS medians for January 2026, the same statistic computed in the model, the same statistic in a counterfactual version of the model in which every age at hire has the hiring curve of the 25–34 band, and the median time a new hire stays, by age at hire.

![Figure 5](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/tenure-by-age-at-hire/figures/fig5_stock_vs_flow.png)

*Figure 5. Median years with the current employer by age (stock), from the CPS (January 2026) and from the career model, together with the counterfactual stock when every age has the 25–34 hiring curve, and the median duration of a new hire by age at hire (flow). Source: BLS, Employee Tenure in 2026; author's calculations.*

In the counterfactual, where a hire at 50 behaves exactly like a hire at 30, the median tenure of current jobs still rises from 1.8 years at 20–24 to 3.3 at 25–34, 4.3 at 35–44 and 4.6 at 45–54 and beyond. Between 20–24 and 45–54 the published median rises by 5.5 years; the counterfactual, with no difference at all between hires of different ages, rises by 2.8, about half of it. Beyond that, the counterfactual flattens while the published medians keep rising, to 7.0 at 45–54 and 9.6 at 55–64; the model with age-specific hiring curves follows them part of the way, to 5.8 and 7.3. The steep rise at older ages in the cross-section does reflect, in part, the longer durations of later hires, but it is mixed with accumulated careers in a way the cross-section cannot unmix.

The gap between stock and flow is large at every age. The median tenure of current workers aged 25 to 34 is 3.0 years; the median time a hire made at 25 to 34 stays is 0.92 years under the pooled statistic. For workers aged 55 to 64 the stock median is 9.6 years; in the model, the median stay of a hire made at 55 to 58 is 1.90 years. The ratio of the stock medians, 9.6 to 3.0, is about 3.2; the ratio of the flow medians, 1.90 to 0.92, is about 2.1. An analyst who answered the hiring question with the tenure report would have the sign right, the magnitude wrong, and no way of knowing whether the sign was right for the right reason.

The model also misses something in the stock. Its medians fall short of the published ones by 1.2 years at 45–54 and 2.3 years at 55–64. The model is calibrated to one cohort's flows and assumes that every cohort follows them, while the CPS cross-section in 2026 mixes cohorts whose careers began between the 1970s and the 2000s; and long careers with one employer may depend on characteristics that a single gamma frailty, constant over a life, does not represent. The direction of the miss suggests that the real survival curves have longer tails than the model gives them, which, if anything, would favour the older hires in the comparisons above.

## What these numbers cannot say

The first limit is the oldest one in the analysis of observational data. These are durations of jobs that employers actually filled with people of a given age. They are not durations of the jobs that would result if an employer hired more people of that age. If employers screen older candidates more severely, the older people who are hired are positively selected, and their retention overstates that of the marginal older candidate. If, instead, older candidates accept jobs below their previous level more often, through necessity, the opposite may hold. A large field experiment on hiring itself, with fictitious résumés that differ in age, found robust evidence of age discrimination in callbacks against older women and weaker evidence against older men (Neumark, Burn and Button, 2019), which is consistent with stricter screening and therefore with some positive selection among the older people hired. The tables cannot measure how large that selection is.

The second limit is the one-cohort design. Hires at 25 to 34 in this cohort happened between 1982 and 1999, and hires at 45 to 54 between 2002 and 2019, in different labour markets. Whatever changed in job stability between those periods is attributed here to age. The tenure report suggests that the cross-section has been stable over the last decade, and the later cohort shows the same ordering by age at hire, but neither settles the question.

The third limit is that duration is not value. A hire who stays longer is not necessarily more productive, cheaper or better trained; ramp-up time, wages and productivity all may differ with age, and none of them is in these tables. The restricted mean is proportional to value only under the assumption that a year of presence is worth the same at every age. What the analysis does establish is narrower: the specific argument that an older hire is a worse investment because the investment is recovered over fewer years runs in the wrong direction for every horizon the data cover.

The fourth limit is that the tables do not separate voluntary from involuntary exits, nor job changes from exits to unemployment or out of the labour force. An employer cares about all of them, since a vacancy costs the same whatever caused it, but an analyst trying to explain the age gradient would need to know which exits drive it.

The fifth limit is geography. These are U.S. data, from a labour market with weak employment protection and employer-provided health insurance, both of which are known to affect job mobility. The method transfers; the numbers do not, and a European employer would need the same tables from a European panel.

With those limits stated, two conjectures follow from the pattern, and they are offered as conjectures, not results. The first is that most of the age gradient in first-year exits is a gradient in job shopping: early in a career, workers sample matches and leave the bad ones quickly, as matching models predict, and by the mid-forties most have stopped sampling. This could be tested with NLSY79 microdata, which record the reason for leaving each job. The second is that a candidate's own history of job durations would absorb most of the predictive content of age: if age matters because it proxies for the propensity to sample, the record of whether a person has in fact sampled is a better predictor than the year of birth. This too could be tested with the microdata, by fitting a hazard model for each new job with age at start and the durations of the person's previous jobs as covariates, and checking how much of the age effect survives.

## For the analyst who gets this question

The question of whether a type of hire will stay long enough comes to data scientists in many forms, about age, about candidates from particular sectors or schools, about internal transfers, and the structure of a sound answer is the same in all of them.

The clock has to start at the hire. Any statistic computed on current employees describes the jobs that survived to the survey, and it inherits the length bias of the cross-section; it is the wrong denominator for a forecast about new hires. Inside a company this means building the analysis from start dates in the HR system, with every past hire included, including those who left in their first weeks and are the easiest to lose from the records.

The unit has to be the hire. A forecast about the next hire is a forecast about a draw from the population of starts, and each start counts once. Statistics that average within people first, as the later releases of the NLSY79 table do, answer a different question, and the difference was eight to eleven points at one year in every age band here.

The horizon has to be stated. A comparison of medians, or of mean tenures to infinity, hides the fact that an employer recovers costs over a finite window and that the comparison can depend on the window's length. The restricted mean within the payback horizon, and the expected number of hires per position within the planning horizon, answer the question that is actually being asked, and they can be computed from the survival curve up to the horizon alone.

Censoring has to be bounded, not ignored and not modelled away. When data stop before the horizon, worst cases and break-even exit rates turn the gap into a statement about how extreme the unobserved future would have to be to reverse the conclusion. That statement is often enough for the decision, and when it is not, it says exactly what additional data would settle it.

Vintages have to be compared. When the same published table exists in several releases, overlapping cells that describe completed events should not move; when they do, the definition probably changed, and the change has to be found before the series is used.

The last point concerns what such an analysis is for. In the United States, the Age Discrimination in Employment Act of 1967 prohibits age discrimination in hiring against people aged 40 and over, and in the European Union Council Directive 2000/78/EC prohibits age discrimination in employment, allowing differences of treatment only where they are objectively and reasonably justified by a legitimate aim. An analysis like this one is not an input to a hiring rule by age. Its use is the opposite: to check whether a widely held quantitative belief that would justify such a rule survives contact with the data. This one does not. A retention model for hiring decisions, where one is justified at all, belongs on the person's own record, not on a proxy as coarse as a birth year, and the second conjecture above suggests that it would predict better too.

## What the records say

The forecast that a hire made in the fifties will not stay long enough to repay the cost of hiring has a precise form, and four decades of job records allow it to be checked. For every horizon the data reach, and under either way of counting, jobs started at 45 to 58 last longer than jobs started at 25 to 34. Within two years, the window in which hiring costs are usually recovered, a position staffed with hires in their late twenties and early thirties needs 1.3 to 1.4 times as many hires as one staffed with hires in their late forties or fifties, which means about twice as many replacements. Beyond the data, the comparison reverses only if the older hires leave at rates several times those they showed while observed, and for hires in their mid-forties over ten years, only if they all leave at once. Retirement is visible in the records, at the end of a decade for the oldest hires, and it is small next to the turnover of the young.

Along the way, the same table turned out to contain two different statistics in two halves of its history, and the cross-sectional figure that is quoted most often turned out to answer a different question from the one it is used for. Both are ordinary hazards of working with published data, and both have ordinary defences: compare overlapping vintages, start the clock at the event the question is about, and count the unit the decision is about.

## Code and data

Every number and figure in this article is produced by the code in [github.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/tree/main/tenure-by-age-at-hire](https://github.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/tree/main/tenure-by-age-at-hire). The folder contains the transcribed BLS tables with their sources, `tenure_numbers.py`, which prints every quantity quoted here in nine sections (the six releases and the break between them, survival curves and restricted means, censoring bounds, worst cases and break-even exit rates, hires per position, the calibrated career model with its out-of-sample checks, stock versus flow, the employer's view from the model, and the sensitivity analysis), and `figures.py`, which draws the five figures.
