# The Arithmetic of Regression to the Mean: Reliability, Selection Thresholds, Non-Normal Tails and the Designs That Separate It From an Effect

## Part 2 of 4. A working model of where regression to the mean comes from and how large it will be: measurement error, transient states and drift; the closed-form fall for a selected group; what changes when the distributions are skewed, heavy-tailed or mixed; Kelley's shrinkage; two real time series; and five designs compared on a screened population

The first article in this series followed regression to the mean through its history and recomputed the data that made it: Galton's mid-parents and children, Pearson and Lee's fathers and sons, a replication of Secrist's grouping on a century of baseball teams. It established, from the numbers, that the regression runs in both directions of time, that it does not narrow the distribution, that its amount depends on what is conditioned on, and that an intervention given only to extremes cannot be evaluated by measuring the extremes again.

This article turns those observations into a model a data scientist can compute with. It asks where the imperfect correlation that produces regression comes from, and why the answer matters for what happens over more than two occasions. It gives the closed-form amount of regression to expect from a selection, checks it by simulation, and shows how much of it a design can remove before any data are analysed. It then leaves the normal distribution, because real data are skewed, heavy-tailed and mixed, and the regression in such data is not a constant fraction of anything. It looks at shrinkage, the estimator that builds the regression into the prediction, at two real time series that behave in opposite ways, and finally at the designs that separate regression from the effect of an intervention, compared on a simulated screening programme and on a small real trial. ## The model: something that persists, and something that does not

The simplest model that produces regression has one equation. Each unit has a stable component, its true score T, and every measurement adds noise that is independent of T and of the noise in other measurements: X = T + e. Let the true scores have variance σ²_T and the noise variance σ²_e. The share of the variance of a measurement that is stable, R = σ²_T / (σ²_T + σ²_e), is what psychometrics calls the reliability, and under this model it is also the correlation between two measurements of the same units. If the measurements are standardised, so that both have unit variance and mean zero, the best prediction of the second from the first is R times the first, and the best estimate of the true score is the same.

The script simulates a million units with reliability 0.5 and selects those whose first measurement is more than two standard deviations above the mean, 2.27 per cent of them. Their first measurement averages 2.376 standard deviations above the mean. Their second averages 1.181, and their true score 1.187. The second measurement of the selected group is, up to simulation noise, an unbiased measurement of their true score; it is the first measurement that was biased, because the selection picked units whose noise happened to be large and positive along with units whose true score was high. With reliability 0.8 the same selection gives a first measurement of 2.374, a second of 1.898 and a true score of 1.898. Regression to the mean is the noise that the selection chose, and the second measurement simply stops choosing it.

This way of putting it removes most of the mystery. Nothing happens to the selected units between the two measurements. The first measurement of the group is too extreme, as an estimate of what the group is, by an amount that can be computed, and the second measurement reveals it. The estimate that corrects it in advance is Truman Kelley's, from his 1927 book on educational measurement, and it is one line (Kelley, 1927):

```python
def kelley(x, reliability: float, mean: float):
    """Kelley's estimate of a true score from an observed score: the observed deviation shrunk towards the
    group mean by the reliability."""
    return mean + reliability * (np.asarray(x) - mean)
```

The reliability in this model is a test-retest correlation: the correlation between two measurements of the same units on two occasions, with no reason for anything to have changed between them except the noise. It is not the same as the internal consistency of a questionnaire, which measures agreement between items on one occasion and ignores day-to-day variation, and it is not the same as the agreement between two instruments. For the purpose of predicting regression, the relevant correlation is between the measurement used to select and the measurement used to evaluate, taken in the way and at the interval they will actually be taken.

The rest of this article complicates the model in the directions real data take it. The first complication is that "noise" is not one thing.

## Three reasons two measurements disagree

Two measurements of the same unit can disagree because the instrument is imprecise, because the unit is in a different state on the two occasions, or because the unit has changed. The three reasons can produce the same regression between two occasions and different patterns over several, and the difference matters for anyone who measures more than twice.

The script simulates three panels of 20,000 units over twelve occasions, each standardised to unit variance. In the first, every unit has a stable trait and every measurement adds independent error, with reliability 0.5. The correlation between occasion 1 and occasion 1 + k is 0.505, 0.504, 0.496, 0.509 and 0.503 for k = 1, 2, 3, 5 and 10: it is the same at every distance, because the error does not persist at all and the trait persists completely. In the second panel there is no stable trait and no measurement error, only a transient state that follows a first-order autoregression with coefficient 0.7, so that each occasion keeps 70 per cent of the previous one's deviation and adds a new shock. The correlations are 0.699, 0.488, 0.340, 0.156 and 0.018: the regression continues with every step, and after ten occasions almost nothing of the original deviation is left. The third panel mixes the two, with 40 per cent of the variance in a stable trait, 40 per cent in the autoregressive state and 20 per cent in measurement error. Its correlations, 0.676, 0.595, 0.532, 0.465 and 0.409, fall at first and then settle towards the trait's 0.4, matching the theoretical 0.680, 0.596, 0.537, 0.467 and 0.411.

*Figure 1. Left: correlation between the first occasion and an occasion k steps later, in 20,000 simulated units under three models (points) and their theoretical values (lines). Right: regression slopes between two points of a random walk k steps apart, observed from step 51 onwards: later on earlier, and earlier on later.*

The left panel of Figure 1 draws the three curves, and their shapes are a diagnostic. If a group selected for an extreme first measurement regresses on the second occasion and then stays where it is, the disagreement between occasions was mostly measurement error or a very short-lived state, and the second measurement is already a good estimate of where the group will remain. If it keeps drifting back for several occasions, there is a transient component with memory, and a single follow-up measurement understates the regression still to come. If it drifts back and then levels off above the mean, there is a stable component, and the level it settles at is the selected group's real difference from the population. With only two occasions, the three cases are indistinguishable; with four or five, they usually are not. This is one of the cheapest improvements available to any before-and-after analysis: measure the selected group more than once afterwards and look at the shape.

The shape can even be fitted, though less precisely than one might hope. Under the mixed model, the correlation at lag k is the trait share plus the state share times a^k, where a is the autoregressive coefficient: three unknowns that the correlations at lags 1, 2 and 3 determine exactly. Applied to the simulated mixture, whose true values are a trait share of 0.4, a state share of 0.4 and a = 0.7, the three correlations give a trait share of 0.331, a state share of 0.452 and a = 0.764. Even with 20,000 units, small sampling errors in three correlations are amplified by the algebra that inverts them, and the estimated share of the variance that will never regress is 17 per cent too low. More occasions, and a model fitted to all of them at once, do better; the qualitative reading of the curve is robust long before the parameters are.

The random walk is the case with nothing to regress to. Each step adds an independent shock to the previous position, so the expected value of the next position, given the current one, is the current one. The script simulates 20,000 walks and observes them from step 51 onwards. The slope of the position k steps later on the current position is 0.9996, 1.0008 and 0.9959 for k = 1, 5 and 10: no regression at all, whatever the distance. Yet the slope in the other direction, of the earlier position on the later one, is 0.9807, 0.9087 and 0.8359, because the variance of the walk grows with time (from a standard deviation of 7.10 at the first observed step to 7.75 ten steps later) and the later position is the noisier predictor. The right panel of Figure 1 shows the asymmetry. Regression to the mean, in the sense of the previous article, requires a stable distribution: the same mean and spread on both occasions. Where the spread grows, regression can appear in one direction and not the other, and where there is no stable mean, a group that starts high has no reason to come back. John Nesselroade, Stephen Stigler and Paul Baltes worked through these cases for the study of development and change (Nesselroade, Stigler and Baltes, 1980); a data scientist meets them whenever a metric trends. A business metric that grows with the customer base, a cumulative count, a price level or the size of a codebase behaves more like the walk than like a stable distribution, and a month selected for being unusually high on such a metric will not, on average, be followed by a return to any earlier level. The quantities that do regress are the deviations from the trend, and the first step in any analysis of an extreme value in a trending series is to decide what the trend is and measure the deviation from it, as the temperature example below does with the seasonal cycle.

## How much regression a selection produces

For two measurements with a joint normal distribution, equal spreads and correlation ρ, the amount of regression to expect from a selection has a closed form, and it is the single most useful formula in this series. Select the units whose first measurement is above a cut-off z, measured in standard deviations from the mean. The mean of the selected units on the first measurement is λ(z) = φ(z) / (1 − Φ(z)), where φ and Φ are the standard normal density and distribution function; in econometrics this ratio is called the inverse Mills ratio. Their mean on the second measurement is ρλ(z). The expected fall is therefore (1 − ρ)λ(z) standard deviations: the share of the selected deviation that is lost is 1 − ρ whatever the cut-off, and the size of the selected deviation grows as the selection becomes stricter. The formula, with its translation into practical units, is the basis of the methods for estimating regression effects in epidemiology (Davis, 1976; Barnett, van der Pols and Dobson, 2005).

```python
def inverse_mills(z):
    """E[Z | Z > z] for a standard normal Z: phi(z) / (1 - Phi(z))."""
    return stats.norm.pdf(z) / stats.norm.sf(z)


def expected_regression(rho: float, top_share: float, sd: float = 1.0) -> tuple[float, float]:
    z = stats.norm.isf(top_share)
    lam = inverse_mills(z)
    return sd * lam, sd * (1 - rho) * lam
```

The numbers are worth having in mind. Selecting the top half of a population on one measurement gives a group 0.798 standard deviations above the mean; the top fifth, 1.400; the top tenth, 1.755; the top twentieth, 2.063; the top hundredth, 2.665. With a correlation of 0.5 between the two measurements, those groups are expected to fall back by 0.399, 0.700, 0.877, 1.031 and 1.333 standard deviations; with a correlation of 0.9, by 0.080, 0.140, 0.175, 0.206 and 0.267; with a correlation of 0.3, by 0.559 up to 1.866. A simulation of two million pairs with correlation 0.6 checks the formula: the top fifth averages 1.4006 on the first measurement against a formula value of 1.3998 and falls by 0.5590 against 0.5599; the top twentieth averages 2.0642 against 2.0627 and falls by 0.8228 against 0.8251.

In natural units, take a measurement with a standard deviation of 15 and a test-retest correlation of 0.6, and select the top tenth. The selected group stands 26.32 units above the population mean on the screening measurement and is expected to fall by 10.53 units on the next one, with no change in anybody. Any before-and-after comparison of that group that reports a fall of around ten units has, so far, reported nothing.

The formula also says how to make the regression smaller before any data are analysed. The reliability of an average of k parallel measurements is given by the Spearman–Brown formula, kR / (1 + (k − 1)R), from two papers of 1910 (Spearman, 1910; Brown, 1910). With single measurements of reliability 0.6, the average of two has reliability 0.750, of three 0.818 and of five 0.882. Selecting the top tenth on the average of k measurements rather than on one, the share of the selected deviation that is lost on a new measurement falls from 0.400 to 0.250, 0.182 and 0.118, and the expected fall, in standard deviations of a single measurement, from 0.702 to 0.392, 0.273 and 0.170. A simulation of selection on the mean of three measurements gives a fall of 0.2732 against the formula's 0.273. The right panel of Figure 2 shows the effect: the selected group is a little less extreme on the selection variable when the selection uses more measurements, and it keeps far more of its deviation afterwards.

A second design removes the regression from the comparison instead of shrinking it. Select on one measurement and use a second, independent measurement taken before any intervention as the baseline. Simulating a top-10% selection with reliability 0.6, the selected group averages 1.7531 standard deviations on the selection measurement, 1.0514 on a second pre-intervention measurement and 1.0518 on a third. The change from the selection measurement to the third is −0.7013, entirely regression; the change from the second to the third is +0.0004, which is nothing, as it should be. The second baseline has already regressed, because it was not used to select anybody. Taking more than one baseline measurement is among the standard recommendations for dealing with regression to the mean in epidemiology (Barnett, van der Pols and Dobson, 2005), and the common practice of confirming an abnormal screening value with a repeat before acting on it is related but different: if the repeat is also used to decide who is selected, the selection rests on two measurements, which reduces the regression without removing it. Only a baseline that played no part in the selection has already regressed.

### Unequal spreads and other variables

Two generalisations of the formula cover most of what a data scientist meets. The first is that the two measurements need not have the same spread. The expected value of the second, given the first, is its own mean plus ρ times the ratio of the two standard deviations times the first measurement's deviation, and the regression is only guaranteed in standard units. If the second measurement is more spread out than the first, a selected group can keep more of its deviation in raw units than the correlation alone would suggest, or even all of it, while still regressing in standard units. The weights of the treated anorexia patients below are an example of the direction: their spread rose by about 70 per cent, and the slope of the final weight on the initial weight, 0.85 and 0.91 in the two treated groups, is much closer to one than the correlations of 0.49 and 0.54. A comparison in raw units of a group's deviation before and after an intervention that changes the spread mixes the regression with the change in spread, and the two have to be separated before either is interpreted.

The second generalisation is that the selection variable and the outcome need not be the same quantity. Select the units in the top 5 per cent of a score, and measure a different outcome correlated with the score at ρ. The selected group's expected deviation on the outcome, in the outcome's standard deviations, is ρ times its deviation on the score, which is 2.063 standard deviations for a top-5% selection. With a correlation of 0.3, the group is expected to stand 0.619 standard deviations above the outcome's mean, not 2.063, and a fall of 1.444 standard deviations from what the score seemed to promise is built in. This is the situation of every model-based selection: the leads with the highest predicted probability of converting, the patients with the highest predicted risk, the applicants with the highest predicted performance. If the correlation between the score and the outcome is modest, the selected group's outcome will be much less extreme than its scores, and the shortfall is regression, not a failure of the model. A model that is calibrated already predicts the shortfall; the error lies in reading the score of the top group as a forecast of the top group's outcome on the scale of the score.

### Where you start and how much you change

The same arithmetic produces one of the most persistent misreadings in physiology and medicine. Plot the change between two measurements against the first measurement, and the points slope downwards: those who started high fell, those who started low rose. Joseph Wilder named the pattern in German in 1931, the Ausgangswertgesetz, and restated it in English in 1957 as "the law of initial value", reading it as a property of physiological regulation (Wilder, 1957). Part of what it describes can be physiology. All of it, in the simplest case, is regression. With no change in anyone and two measurements of equal spread and correlation ρ, the correlation between the first measurement and the change is exactly −√((1 − ρ)/2). In the script's simulation it is −0.5001 for a reliability of 0.5, against a theoretical −0.5000, and −0.3167 for a reliability of 0.8, against −0.3162. A change that depends on where it starts is the default, not a finding.

Peter Oldham proposed the remedy in 1962: correlate the change with the mean of the two measurements instead of the first (Oldham, 1962). The covariance between the mean of the two measurements and the change is half the difference between their variances, so when nothing changes and the spreads are equal, the correlation between the change and the mean is zero; in the simulation it is +0.0002 and −0.0005. A non-zero Oldham correlation therefore says something real: that the spread of the second measurement differs from the spread of the first, which is what happens when units respond differently to whatever happened between the two occasions. The test is equivalent to the Pitman–Morgan test of equal variances used for Pearson and Lee's fathers and sons in the first article.

## Beyond the normal distribution

The formulas above assume a joint normal distribution, and under it the expected second measurement is a straight line in the first, so the regression is the same fraction of the deviation everywhere. That property is fragile. The script simulates pairs of measurements that share a component and add independent noise of equal variance, so that the correlation between them is 0.50 in the first three cases (the fourth has a reliability of 0.5 within each of two groups), and varies the distribution of the parts. Figure 3 shows the mean of the second measurement for each value of the first.

![Figure 3](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/regression-to-the-mean/part2/figures/fig3_nonnormal.png)

*Figure 3. Mean of the second measurement for each value of the first (points), against the straight line with the same correlation and the line of no regression, for four simulated populations of two million pairs. Top left: a skewed (lognormal) shared component with normal noise. Top right: a heavy-tailed (Student t, 3 degrees of freedom) shared component with normal noise. Bottom left: a normal shared component with heavy-tailed noise. Bottom right: two equal groups with means −2 and +2, each with reliability 0.5 within the group.*

When the shared component is skewed, here lognormal, the top tenth on the first measurement stands 3.659 above the mean and 2.319 above it on the second, keeping 0.634 of its deviation where the correlation would suggest 0.5. The top hundredth keeps 0.881. When the shared component is heavy-tailed, a Student t with three degrees of freedom, the top tenth keeps 0.472 and the top hundredth 0.732. In both cases the regression is smaller at the extremes, because an extreme value of the sum is more likely to come from the component with the long tail, and the long tail belongs to the part that persists. The skewed case is also asymmetric: the bottom tenth of the lognormal population, where the persistent component is bunched against its lower limit and the noise does most of the work, stands 2.772 below the mean on the first measurement and only 0.700 below on the second, keeping 0.253 of its deviation against the top tenth's 0.634. In a skewed metric, the units selected for being unusually low regress far more than the units selected for being unusually high.

Reverse the roles and the regression is larger at the extremes. With a normal shared component and heavy-tailed noise of the same variance, the top tenth keeps 0.529 of its deviation and the top hundredth only 0.264. The bottom-left panel of Figure 3 shows the mean of the second measurement rising with the first up to a first measurement of about 3, roughly two standard deviations, and then turning back towards zero: beyond that point, an extreme first measurement is more likely to be a large error than a large true value, and the more extreme it is, the less of it survives. This is the case that matters most in practice. Business metrics with occasional large errors, from logging faults, bots, one-off orders or data entry, have heavy-tailed noise, and the units at the very top of a ranking on such a metric can be the ones most likely to be there by accident.

The bottom-right panel shows the other way the straight line fails: a population made of two groups with different means. With group means of −2 and +2 and a within-group reliability of 0.5, the overall correlation is 0.833. Units whose first measurement is near +2 come almost entirely from the upper group (97.9 per cent of those between 1.5 and 2.5) and barely regress at all: 1.996 on the first measurement, 1.958 on the second. Units near +4 regress halfway back, from 3.920 to 2.957, not towards the overall mean of zero but towards their own group's mean of 2. The single straight line through the whole population, with slope 0.833, predicts 3.27 for them, too high, and for units near +2 it predicts 1.66, too low. The question "regression towards which mean?" has a precise answer: towards the expected value given everything that is known about the unit. Here the group is not observed, but the first measurement reveals a good deal about it, and the conditional mean reflects that.

There is a general result behind all four panels. Myra Samuels showed that for any pair of measurements with the same marginal distribution, of which exchangeability (a joint distribution that is the same in either order) is a special case, the mean of the second measurement over the units whose first measurement is above any cut-off cannot exceed the mean of the first (Samuels, 1991). She called this reversion, to distinguish it from regression in the strict sense, in which the conditional mean of the second measurement at every value of the first lies between that value and the mean. Reversion of a group selected for a high first value is therefore universal under that condition; how much reversion, and whether it is a constant fraction of the deviation, depends on the distributions. A data scientist estimating the regression of a selected group should estimate it among units like the selected ones, not borrow a correlation computed over the whole population.

## Shrinkage: putting the regression into the estimate

Kelley's formula is the regression to the mean made into an estimator. If the reliability is known and the measurements are normal, the best estimate of a unit's true score, in mean squared error, is not its observed score but the observed score shrunk towards the mean by the reliability. In a simulation of 1,000 units with reliability 0.5, repeated 2,000 times, the mean squared error of the observed score as an estimate of the true score is 0.5000 and that of Kelley's estimate 0.2504, 0.501 of it, matching the theoretical values of 1 − R = 0.5 and R(1 − R) = 0.25.

Two properties of the shrunk estimate are worth stating because they are often misunderstood. The first is that shrinkage towards a single mean with a single reliability does not change the ranking of the units, because it is a monotone transformation of the observed scores. The true top 50 of the 1,000 units are found in the observed top 50 only 39.4 per cent of the time on average, and Kelley's estimate finds exactly the same ones, 39.4 per cent. Shrinkage corrects the predicted level of the selected units, which is what a before-and-after comparison needs, and does nothing for the choice of whom to select, which needs more reliable measurement. The second property is that the mean towards which the estimate shrinks matters as much as the amount. In a population of two groups with means −1 and +1 and a within-group reliability of 0.5, shrinking every unit towards the overall mean, with the overall reliability of 0.663, gives a mean squared error of 0.6688; shrinking each unit towards its own group's mean, with the within-group reliability, gives 0.5025; the raw scores give 1.0085. For the top 5 per cent on the observed score, 96.7 per cent of whom come from the upper group, the mean true score is 2.203; shrinking to the overall mean predicts 2.320 and shrinking to the group means 2.217.

These are the ideas that Bradley Efron and Carl Morris made famous half a century after Kelley, with the empirical Bayes interpretation of the James–Stein estimator, in which the amount of shrinkage is itself estimated from the spread of many units' observed values (Efron and Morris, 1975). The connection to regression to the mean is direct: a shrinkage estimator is a prediction of each unit's next measurement that has already regressed. Whenever a data scientist ranks many units on noisy metrics, the shrunk estimates are the better forecasts, and the gap between the raw and the shrunk estimate for the units at the top of the ranking is the regression that a naive analysis will later "discover".

Kelley's formula assumes a single reliability for everyone, and much real data violates that assumption in a systematic way: rates computed from units of very different sizes. A conversion rate for a store with 20 visits and one for a store with 2,000 visits are not equally reliable, and the extremes of a ranking by rate are dominated by the small units, whose rates are noisiest. The script simulates 2,000 units with true rates drawn around a mean of 0.10, sizes spread evenly on a logarithmic scale from 20 to 2,000, and two periods of independent binomial counts. The top 5 per cent of units by their period-1 rate, 101 units, come 60.4 per cent from the smallest quarter of units by size and 8.9 per cent from the largest quarter; their median size is 51 against 195 for all units. Their period-1 rate averages 0.214, their true rate 0.144 and their period-2 rate 0.152. Shrinking each unit by its own reliability, which ranges from 0.16 for the smallest units to 0.95 for the largest (computed here with the true spread of rates between units, which in practice would itself be estimated), changes the picture: the top 5 per cent by shrunk rate has a median size of 491, a true rate of 0.155 and a period-2 rate of 0.156, and it contains 50.0 per cent of the true top 5 per cent against 41.0 per cent for the ranking by raw rate. When reliabilities differ, shrinkage does change the ranking, because it shrinks the small units more, and the change is towards the units that are really at the top. It is the problem that funnel plots address with control limits that widen as precision falls, and that empirical Bayes estimates address by shrinkage, whenever hospitals, schools or stores are ranked on rates measured with unequal precision, and it returns in the last article of this series.

## Two real series: one regresses quickly, one never does

The model says that the amount of regression depends on what persists, and two real series show the extremes of that dependence.

The first is the daily mean temperature at JFK airport in New York in 2013, from the hourly readings in the `nycflights13` weather data (Wickham, 2021). The script removes the seasonal cycle with a two-harmonic curve, which leaves daily anomalies with a standard deviation of 5.92 degrees Fahrenheit. The correlation of the anomaly with itself one day later is 0.630; two days later, 0.235; three days later, 0.063; after that it hovers around zero and turns slightly negative. A first-order autoregression with the observed lag-one correlation would predict 0.397 for two days and 0.250 for three, so the anomalies lose their memory faster than that simple model implies. The 19 hottest days of the year relative to the season, the top 5 per cent, averaged 12.33 degrees above the seasonal curve. The next day averaged 7.46 above, about 0.60 of the anomaly and close to the 7.77 that the lag-one correlation of 0.630 predicts; the day after, 0.56 above; three days later, 2.60 below. The 19 coldest days averaged 12.20 below the curve and the following days 8.05, 5.16 and 2.22 below, a slower return than for the hot days, though with 19 days in each group the difference between them is not well determined. Weather regresses to its seasonal mean within a few days, and the top panel of Figure 4 shows how. The correlations at five to seven days, −0.045, −0.117 and −0.136, are slightly negative, which is consistent with warm and cold spells alternating over about a week as weather systems pass; that reading is a conjecture the script does not test, and with one year of data the small negative values could also be noise. For the question of this article it does not matter: whatever the mechanism, an anomalous day carries little information about the anomaly three days later.

The second series is the German DAX stock index at the daily close from 1991 to 1998, 1,860 trading days, distributed with R (`EuStockMarkets`). In levels, the slope of tomorrow's close on today's is 1.0014: no regression whatever. The days on which the index closed in its top 10 per cent for the period (among the days with twenty trading days still to come) averaged 4,928; twenty trading days later the index averaged 5,021, higher still, and nowhere near the period's mean of 2,531. A level that drifts has no mean to return to, and the bottom-right panel of Figure 4 shows the points following the line of equality rather than bending towards the mean. In daily returns the picture is the opposite. The lag-one correlation of daily log returns is −0.0004: the regression is complete within a day. After the 5 per cent largest daily gains, which averaged +2.277 per cent, the next day's return averaged +0.124 per cent, with a standard error of 0.112 per cent over 93 days, against an overall mean of +0.065 per cent, a difference well within the noise of daily returns. The bottom-left panel shows the next day's mean return, decile by decile of today's, scattered around the overall mean with no trend.

The two series make the practical point of this section. Whether a group selected for an extreme value will come back, how fast, and towards what, depends on which components of the series persist, and that is a property to be measured, not assumed. A data scientist who assumes regression in a trending series will predict a return that does not come, and one who assumes persistence in a series of independent shocks will mistake every extreme day for a new level.

## Separating regression from an effect

Everything so far concerns what happens with no intervention. The practical problem is the reverse: an intervention is given to a group selected for an extreme measurement, the group is measured again, and the analyst must say how much of the change is the intervention. The script simulates the standard case in medical statistics, treating people selected for a high screening measurement (Bland and Altman, 1994). A measurement has a population mean of 140, a standard deviation of 15 and a test-retest reliability of 0.6, numbers chosen to be loosely in the range of a single blood-pressure reading, though the simulation is not a model of any particular test. People whose screening value exceeds 160, 9.1 per cent of the population, are selected and given a treatment that lowers the measurement by exactly 5 units. Each replication screens 20,000 people, and there are 1,000 replications. Five designs estimate the effect; the left panel of Figure 5 shows their estimates.

![Figure 5](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/regression-to-the-mean/part2/figures/fig5_designs.png)

*Figure 5. Left: estimates of a treatment effect of −5 from 1,000 simulated screening programmes (population mean 140, SD 15, reliability 0.6, selection above 160), by design. Right: change in weight against weight before treatment for the 72 patients of the anorexia trial (MASS::anorexia), by group, with least-squares lines.*

The naive design treats everyone selected and compares their follow-up measurement with their screening value. It estimates the effect at −15.78 on average, with a standard deviation across replications of 0.29: 3.16 times the true effect, and wrong with great precision. The formula gives the reason exactly: the selected group's mean screening value is 166.97, and the expected regression is (1 − 0.6) times its deviation of 26.97 from the population mean, 10.79 units. The naive estimate is the effect plus the regression.

The other four designs recover the effect. A randomised control group, formed by treating a random half of the selected and following up the other half untreated, gives −5.000 on average with a standard deviation of 0.60; both arms regress by the same expected amount, and the difference between them is the effect. Subtracting the predicted regression from the naive change, using the known reliability, gives −4.995 with a standard deviation of 0.29, the most precise of the five; but it is only as good as the reliability it assumes, and an error of 0.05 in the reliability shifts the estimate by 1.35 units. A second, independent baseline measurement before treatment gives −4.995 with a standard deviation of 0.33; the second baseline has regressed already, as the previous section showed, and the change from it is the effect. And a regression discontinuity design, which follows up everyone, treated or not, and compares the follow-up just above and just below the screening threshold with local linear fits ten units either side, gives −4.94 on average with a standard deviation of 0.76.

How well the reliability is known is the whole question for that design. The script repeats the screening simulation with the reliability estimated each time from a pilot sample of people measured twice, not selected. With a pilot of 50 people, the corrected estimate averages −4.92 but varies with a standard deviation of 2.46 across replications; with 100 people, 1.83; with 500, 0.81; with 2,000, 0.50. The design is more precise than the randomised control, whose standard deviation is 0.60, only when the reliability rests on a pilot of more than about a thousand people, and with a pilot of a few hundred it is less precise as well as more dependent on assumptions. It also takes the population mean as known, here 140. Published test-retest reliabilities often come from studies of modest size, and many business measurements have none at all; an analyst who borrows or estimates one should carry its uncertainty into the corrected estimate.

The regression discontinuity deserves a comment because it turns the problem on its head. Selection on a threshold is what creates the regression, and it is also what makes a clean comparison possible: people just above and just below 160 on the screening measurement differ in treatment and hardly at all in anything else, including in how much they will regress, because the regression is a smooth function of the screening value and has no jump at the threshold. Donald Thistlethwaite and Donald Campbell introduced the design in 1960 for exactly this kind of problem, the effect of an award given to students above a score (Thistlethwaite and Campbell, 1960). It needs follow-up data on the people below the threshold, which a screening programme often does not collect, and it is the least precise of the four valid designs here, because it uses only the people near the threshold. But it needs neither randomisation nor an assumed reliability.

```python
def local_linear_jump(x, y, cutoff, bandwidth):
    """Difference at the cut-off between linear fits to the points within `bandwidth` on each side
    (uniform kernel), with its standard error."""
    left = (x < cutoff) & (x >= cutoff - bandwidth)
    right = (x >= cutoff) & (x < cutoff + bandwidth)
    fits = []
    for m in (left, right):
        b, se = ols(np.column_stack([np.ones(m.sum()), x[m] - cutoff]), y[m])
        fits.append((b[0], se[0]))
    return fits[1][0] - fits[0][0], math.sqrt(fits[0][1] ** 2 + fits[1][1] ** 2)
```

The bandwidth, ten units on each side here, is the design's main tuning choice. A narrower window compares people who are more alike in their screening values and relies less on the straight lines being right, at the cost of fewer people and a noisier estimate; a wider one does the opposite. In this simulation the expected follow-up is exactly linear in the screening value, because everything is normal, so the straight lines are right at any width. In real screening data the relation can bend, for the reasons the section on non-normal distributions gave, and the usual practice is to choose the bandwidth by a data-driven rule and to show that the estimate does not depend much on it.

The anorexia data, from a trial of treatments for young women with anorexia distributed with the R package `MASS` (Hand et al., 1993), show the within-group pattern in real data. The 72 patients were weighed before and after one of three conditions: a control condition (26 patients), cognitive behavioural treatment (29) and family therapy (17). The control group's mean weight barely moved, from 81.56 to 81.11 pounds, but the change depended strongly on the starting weight: the slope of the change on the starting weight is −1.134 (standard error 0.167), and the correlation between the two weights is −0.161. The lightest controls gained and the heaviest lost. In the treated groups the slope is much flatter, −0.152 and −0.091, and the correlations between the two weights are +0.492 and +0.538. The right panel of Figure 5 shows the three groups. Whatever produced the unusual pattern among the controls, a within-group analysis that reported the gains of the lightest patients as improvement would be reporting, in large part, regression. Oldham's correlation separates the two readings. Among the controls, the correlation between the change and the starting weight is −0.810, but between the change and the mean of the two weights it is −0.185, small for 26 patients, and the spread of weights narrowed from 5.71 to 4.74 pounds. In the two treated groups the correlation between change and starting weight is close to zero, −0.101 and −0.064, while Oldham's correlation is +0.549 and +0.546: the spread of weights rose by about 70 per cent, from 4.85 to 8.35 pounds with cognitive behavioural treatment and from 5.02 to 8.48 with family therapy. Some treated patients gained a great deal and others little, a heterogeneity of response that the comparison of means does not show.

Between the groups, if the allocation to conditions was random, as the analysis of such trials assumes (the package documentation does not say), all three standard estimates of the treatment effects are unbiased; they differ in precision. For cognitive behavioural treatment against control, the difference in weight after treatment is +4.59 pounds (standard error 1.86), the difference in change scores +3.46 (2.06) and the analysis of covariance, which regresses the final weight on treatment and the initial weight, +4.24 (1.84), with a coefficient of 0.305 on the initial weight. For family therapy the three estimates are +9.39 (2.02), +7.71 (2.39) and +9.03 (2.03). The analysis of covariance is the standard recommendation for randomised trials with a baseline measurement, because it adjusts for the baseline by exactly as much as the data say the baseline predicts the outcome, which is the regression coefficient, while the change score adjusts by a coefficient of one, which is right only when the regression of the follow-up on the baseline has a slope of one; Andrew Vickers and Douglas Altman suggest change scores as a reasonable alternative only when the correlation between the two measurements is high, above about 0.8 (Vickers and Altman, 2001; Senn, 2006). In this trial the coefficients are 0.305 and 0.211, far from one, and the change score is the least precise of the three. In observational comparisons the choice between change scores and covariance adjustment is a different and much harder question, Lord's paradox, and it is not settled by precision.

## What the model adds

The history in the first article showed that regression to the mean exists, runs both ways and does not shrink the distribution. The model in this one says how large it is and where it comes from, and it yields seven practical rules.

The amount of regression of a selected group is (1 − ρ) times its selected deviation when the measurements are jointly normal with equal spreads, and the selected deviation is computable from the share selected. A top-10% selection on a measurement with test-retest correlation 0.6 regresses by 0.70 standard deviations; any reported change of that size in such a group is, until shown otherwise, regression.

The shape of the regression over several occasions identifies its source. A fall that is complete after one occasion points to measurement error; a fall that continues points to a transient state with memory; a fall that levels off above the mean points to a real difference. Measure more than twice.

Outside the normal distribution the fraction regressing is not constant. It is smaller at the extremes when the persistent component has the heavier tail and larger when the noise does, and units at the top of a ranking on a metric with occasional large errors are the most likely to be there by accident. Estimate the regression among units like the selected ones.

The mean towards which units regress is their expected value given what is known about them, which may be a group mean rather than the population mean. Shrinkage towards the right mean halves the error of the raw scores at reliability 0.5; shrinkage towards the wrong mean recovers only part of the gain.

A change that depends on the starting value is the default, not a finding. With no change in anyone, the correlation between the first measurement and the change is −√((1 − ρ)/2). Correlate the change with the mean of the two measurements instead, and a non-zero correlation then says that the spread changed, which is real information about heterogeneous responses.

When units differ in size, reliabilities differ, and the extremes of a ranking by rate are dominated by the smallest, noisiest units. Shrink each unit by its own reliability; unlike shrinkage with a single reliability, this changes the ranking, and it changes it towards the units that are really at the top.

And the effect of an intervention on a selected group can be separated from the regression by design: a randomised control within the selected, a second baseline, a regression discontinuity at the threshold, or, if the reliability is known well, a predicted regression subtracted from the change. Without one of them, a before-and-after comparison of a selected group measures the regression plus the effect and cannot tell them apart.

The third article applies these rules to real data in which the regression has been mistaken for something else.
