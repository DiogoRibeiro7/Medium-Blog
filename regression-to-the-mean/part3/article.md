# Predicted Against Observed: Regression to the Mean in NHANES Blood-Pressure Readings and Seven Decades of Major League Seasons

## Part 3 of 4. Three systolic readings minutes apart on 10,622 adults, split into drift, regression and residual; the reliability of a batting season from its sampling noise; the sophomore slump; wins beyond the runs; and two dozen selections predicted on one period and checked on another

In the 2009–2010 and 2011–2012 cycles of the US National Health and Nutrition Examination Survey, adults examined in the mobile examination centre sat quietly for five minutes and then had three consecutive blood-pressure readings taken by a physician examiner. Among the 10,622 adults with all three systolic readings recorded, those whose first reading was 140 mmHg or more averaged 153.5 on that reading and 149.8 on the mean of the next two. Nobody was treated between the readings. The fall of 3.8 mmHg happened within a few minutes, in the same chair, under the same cuff.

The thesis of this series is that regression to the mean is not a force acting on individuals but a consequence of selecting on a noisy measurement, that its size can be computed from the correlation between measurements and the selection rule, and that many effects measured after selecting extremes are partly or wholly regression. The first article recomputed the historical data. The second built the arithmetic: the closed-form fall of a selected group, the three sources of disagreement between occasions, what happens to the regression when distributions are skewed or heavy-tailed, and the designs that separate regression from an effect. This article takes the arithmetic to two kinds of real data and asks a narrow question of each: given only what the data say about their own reliability, how well can the regression of a selected group be predicted before it is observed?

The two sources were chosen because they fail in different ways. Blood pressure is a physiological quantity measured by people with instruments, with a known protocol and a systematic change between readings that has nothing to do with selection. Baseball statistics are counts of discrete events, with a sampling model that can be written down exactly, decades of seasons to hold out, and selections with names attached: the Rookie of the Year, the luckiest teams. In both, the regression of a selected group splits into three parts. The first is the change the whole population makes between occasions, called drift here. The second is the regression predicted from the correlation between occasions. The third is what is left over, which is where an effect, if there is one, has to live. Every number below is printed by a script in the series repository, and the predictions in the final sections are fitted on one period and tested on another.

## One sitting, three readings

The NHANES examination protocol for 2009–2012 is short (CDC/NCHS 2011). After resting quietly in a sitting position for five minutes, the participant has three consecutive readings taken; if a reading is interrupted or incomplete, a fourth attempt may be made. The examiners are physicians certified through a training programme. The public files hold the systolic and diastolic value of each reading. The R package NHANES (Pruim 2026), a table built from the public files of the two cycles with the survey weights and design variables, keeps the first three readings and adds an average computed by the rule NHANES documented for the average it reported in its 1999–2000 files (the variable BPXSAR).

Three facts about the readings matter before any selection is made. Every one of the 31,866 systolic readings in the 10,622 complete records is an even number: the values were recorded to 2 mmHg. The final digit is not uniform even among the even digits, with 4 appearing on 23.1% of first readings and 2 and 8 on 18.4% each, a mild digit preference, here for 4. And the package's averaged systolic value equals the mean of the second and third readings for every adult with three readings: under that rule the first reading is left out whenever there are two or more. The decision, which predates this analysis by decades, is a design against exactly the regression studied here, and it will come back.

The adults in the file are a stratified, clustered sample, and statements about the US population need the examination weights. With the four-year weights, the 10,622 adults represent 204.9 million people. All examined adults represent 229.1 million, so 10.6% of the weighted adult population is missing at least one reading and is left out here, with no adjustment for that non-response. The weighted mean of the first systolic reading is 121.80 mmHg, of the second 120.95 and of the third 120.30. The population falls by 0.84 mmHg (standard error 0.09) from the first reading to the second and by 1.50 (SE 0.12) from the first to the third; the mean of the second and third readings sits 1.17 (SE 0.10) below the first. The standard errors come from a delete-one-PSU jackknife within the 29 design strata (Wolter 2007), which has 62 replicates. This fall is the drift. It belongs to everyone, not to a selected group, and any analysis that selects on the first reading and calls the whole fall "regression" counts it twice.

The spread falls too. The weighted standard deviation is 17.42 mmHg for the first reading, 17.11 for the second and 16.83 for the third. The correlations are high: 0.945 between the first and second readings, 0.932 between the first and third and 0.949 between the second and third. The correlation of the first reading with the mean of the other two is 0.951, and the weighted least-squares slope of that mean on the first reading is 0.9145. These are the inputs to every prediction in the next section. Nothing else is used.

## The high readers, taken apart

For a group selected on the first reading, the identity behind the prediction is one line. Write μ₁ for the population mean of the first reading, μ₂₃ for the population mean of the later two, b for the slope of the later mean on the first reading and x̄ for the selected group's mean first reading. If the conditional mean of the later readings is linear in the first, the selected group's later mean is μ₂₃ + b(x̄ − μ₁), and its fall splits exactly into the drift, μ₁ − μ₂₃, and a regression, (1 − b)(x̄ − μ₁). The residual is the observed later mean minus the predicted one, so a positive residual means the group fell less than predicted.

```python
def selection_decomposition(x1, m23, w, sel):
    mu1, mu23 = wmean(x1, w), wmean(m23, w)
    b = wcov(x1, m23, w) / wcov(x1, x1, w)
    xs, ys = wmean(x1[sel], w[sel]), wmean(m23[sel], w[sel])
    pred = mu23 + b * (xs - mu1)
    return {"fall": xs - ys, "drift": mu1 - mu23,
            "regression": (1 - b) * (xs - mu1), "residual": ys - pred}
```

For adults whose first reading was 140 or more, 13.9% of the weighted population, the first reading averaged 153.52 and the mean of the next two 149.76, a fall of 3.76 mmHg (SE 0.29). The drift accounts for 1.17 of it and the predicted regression for 2.71, which puts the predicted later mean at 149.64. The residual is +0.12 mmHg with a standard error of 0.14. The whole fall is drift plus regression to within a standard error, and the regression was computed from a single slope estimated on everyone.

The other thresholds agree to within two standard errors. At or above 130, more than a quarter of the population (27.5%), the fall is 2.93 (SE 0.18), predicted as 1.17 of drift and 1.87 of regression, with a residual of +0.11 (SE 0.09). At or above 160, 3.6% of the population, the first reading averaged 173.36, the fall is 5.12 (SE 0.61), and the regression alone predicts 4.41 of it; the residual is +0.46 with a standard error of 0.40. At the other end, the 5.5% whose first reading was below 100 went up: from 94.27 to 95.86, a rise of 1.59 (SE 0.26). The drift pushed them down by 1.17 and the regression pushed them up by 2.35, and the two together predict 95.45, leaving a residual of +0.41 (SE 0.26). A low first reading rises despite a population that falls, because the regression towards the mean outweighs the drift for anyone far enough below it.

The share of the first-reading deviation that survives to the later readings is 0.918 (SE 0.007) for the group above 140, 0.920 above 130, 0.923 above 160 and 0.900 below 100, against the single slope of 0.915. A linear conditional mean is an assumption, and these numbers are the test of it: within one standard error at three of the four selections, and within two at the fourth.

## The first reading carries something the third does not

The drift is not the only way the first reading differs from the others. If the three readings were a stable pressure plus independent noise of the same size, they would have the same variance, and every pair would have the same covariance. They do not. The variance of the first reading exceeds that of the second by 10.66 mmHg² (SE 2.55), and the second exceeds the third by 9.34 (SE 2.45). The covariance between the first and second readings exceeds the covariance between the second and third by 8.47 (SE 2.09), while the covariance between the first and third equals the covariance between the second and third to within 0.04 (SE 1.25).

That pattern has a simple reading. There is a component that the first two readings share and the third does not have: something that is present at the start of the sitting, smaller by the second reading and gone by the third, and whose size varies between people. Oldham's statistic (Oldham 1962), the correlation of the difference between the first two readings with their sum, which Part 2 showed to be a test of equal variances, is 0.055 here; small, but over ten thousand people it is not zero. Subtracting the covariance of the last two readings from each reading's variance gives the part of each reading not shared with the third reading: 5.49 mmHg as a standard deviation for the first reading, 4.41 for the second and 3.18 for the third itself.

The physiology that would produce such a component is documented. Mancia and colleagues (1983) recorded intra-arterial pressure continuously while a doctor came to the bedside and measured it with a cuff, and found an immediate rise that peaked within one to four minutes and had largely subsided by the end of a ten- to fifteen-minute visit; the size of the rise varied widely between patients. Whether the extra variance of the first NHANES reading is that response, a residue of the walk to the chair that the five minutes of rest did not remove, or something about the procedure itself, these data cannot say. What they do say is that the component is transient, that it varies between people, and that it is therefore a source of regression in exactly the sense of Part 2: a state that does not persist to the next occasion. Whether it counts as "noise" depends on the question. The pressure really was higher during the first reading. It was not the pressure that will be there the next time.

The ordinary noise between readings is not constant either. Estimated from the difference between the second and third readings, the standard deviation of a single reading around the sitting mean rises from 3.48 mmHg among adults whose later mean is below 110 to 4.01 between 130 and 140, 4.63 between 140 and 160 and 4.81 at 160 and above. It also rises with age: 3.40 mmHg for adults aged 18 to 39, 3.94 at 40 to 59 and 4.31 at 60 and over. The right panel of Figure 1 splits the level pattern by age group; within each group the noise is highest at the highest levels, though the estimates for the top level rest on few younger adults and are noisy themselves.

Age changes almost everything except the slope, and the slope stays put for a reason worth seeing. The mean first reading is 114.59 mmHg at 18 to 39, 121.95 at 40 to 59 and 132.86 at 60 and over, and the standard deviation between people grows from 12.00 to 16.61 to 19.77. The within-sitting noise grows more slowly than the spread, so if it were the only difference between the readings the slope would rise with age, from 0.920 in the youngest group to 0.944 and 0.952. The observed slopes are 0.918, 0.915 and 0.917. What holds them down in older adults is the first-reading excess. The variance of the first reading exceeds that of the third by −2.5 mmHg² in the youngest group, which is none at all, by 12.6 in the middle group and by 22.2 at 60 and over, and the drift follows the same gradient: 0.65 mmHg, 0.90 and 2.40. The transient component of the first reading is an older adult's phenomenon in these data.

That matters for who is selected. The adults whose first reading is 140 or more are older than the population, with a mean age of 62.0 against 46.3. Within the oldest group, a third of whom (33.8%) read 140 or more, the selected adults fell from 154.56 to 150.24, of which 2.40 was drift and 1.80 predicted regression, with a residual of −0.12 (SE 0.09). Within the youngest group only 2.7% read 140 or more. A reading of 155 is 22 mmHg above the mean of adults aged 60 and over and 40 above the mean of adults under 40, so the age-specific line predicts a larger regression for the young, 2.85 mmHg against 1.80, as Part 2's result about mixtures says it should. The young did not oblige. Their fall from 149.27 to 147.03 was 1.58 mmHg net of drift, and the residual is +1.26 (SE 0.31) on 124 people, four standard errors above the prediction of their own line and of the pooled one alike. The linear prediction overstates the regression of young high readers. A possible reason, and it is a conjecture, is the one the baseball data below make visible: among young adults a reading of 150 is in a long right tail that contains real hypertension as well as noise, and a single slope cannot know that a tail is more often signal than its distance from the mean suggests.

## Who stays above 140

A fall of 3.76 mmHg in the mean of a selected group sounds small next to the 153.5 it started from. The same fall looks different as a classification at 140 mmHg, the systolic threshold for hypertension in the JNC 7 report (Chobanian et al. 2003). Of the adults at or above 140 on the first reading, 23.0% (SE 1.4%) are below 140 on the mean of the next two. Of those below 140 on the first reading, 1.8% (SE 0.2%) are at or above it on the mean of the next two. The second share is small because it is a share of a large group; in people, the first reading moves 2.11 adults out of the category for every adult it moves in. The weighted share of adults at or above 140 is 13.9% on the first reading and 12.2% on the mean of the next two. At 130, the threshold for stage 1 hypertension in the 2017 American College of Cardiology and American Heart Association guideline (Whelton et al. 2018), 18.8% (SE 0.9%) of the adults at or above it on the first reading are below it on the later mean, 2.6% of those below it move up, the ratio is 2.79, and the share at or above 130 falls from 27.5% to 24.2%.

Part of the asymmetry is the drift, which moves everyone down. With the population fall of 1.17 mmHg added back to the later mean, 19.7% of those at or above 140 on the first reading fall below it and 2.0% of the rest rise above it, a ratio of 1.56 rather than 2.11; at 130 the ratio becomes 1.53. The rest of the asymmetry is the regression, counted in people. A threshold above the mean has more people just below it than just above it, so the same symmetric noise carries more people up across it than down, and selecting on the reading that crossed puts the excess on the wrong side.

Figure 2 shows the same thing as a probability. For each even value of the first reading between 120 and 180, the dots give the weighted share of adults whose later mean is at or above 140. At a first reading of exactly 140 the share is 0.39; a person who reads 140 the first time is more likely than not to read below 140 on the average of the next two. At 142 the share is 0.55, at 146 0.79, and it reaches 0.90 only at 154. A normal model for the first reading and the later mean, using only their means, variances and covariance, draws the blue curve. It has the right location and is visibly too steep: it runs below the dots between 134 and 146 and above them between 156 and 162, because the noise is larger at higher pressures than a single conditional variance allows. Over the whole group at or above 140, the model says 25.2% fall below 140 on the later mean, and 23.0% did.

## The next visit

Everything so far happened within one sitting. The question a clinician or an analyst cares about is usually different: if this person came back next week, what would the reading be? Pressure varies from day to day by more than it varies over three minutes, and the readings in one sitting share that day's level. Their correlation therefore overstates the correlation with a reading on another day, and the regression measured within a sitting understates the regression to the next visit.

NHANES 2009–2012 does not measure anyone twice, so the size of the day-to-day variation has to come from outside. Muntner and colleagues (2011) used the 956 adults in NHANES III (1988–1994) who had readings at three visits, each visit summarised, as here, by the mean of its second and third readings, and reported a mean standard deviation across visits of 7.7 mmHg. A standard deviation from three values underestimates the standard deviation it estimates by the factor c₄(3) = 0.886, so the implied standard deviation of a visit mean is about 8.69 mmHg. Two qualifications go with that number. Dividing a mean of person-level standard deviations by c₄ recovers the average standard deviation, not the root-mean-square one that a variance calculation needs, and Muntner and colleagues found the variability differed between people, so 8.69 is if anything too small. And the NHANES III visits were not identical sittings, at least one of them being part of the home interview, so the figure includes differences of setting and observer as well as day-to-day variation in the person. In 2009–2012 the noise of a mean of two readings within the sitting is 2.72 mmHg, which leaves about 8.25 mmHg of variation from one day to the next. This is a scenario built from another survey, another decade and a subset of adults chosen because they attended three visits; it is labelled as such in what follows and in Figure 2.

Under the scenario, the variance of a person's usual pressure, the part that would persist across visits, is the variance of the later mean (280.6 mmHg²) minus the variance of a visit mean around the usual level (8.69²), or 205.2. The reliability of one visit's mean as a measure of usual pressure is 0.731. A group selected because its first reading was 140 or more, averaging 153.52 on that reading, would average 142.08 at the next visit under the scenario, against 149.76 on the later readings of the same sitting, and 47.0% of it would be below 140. A group selected on the package's averaged value, the mean of the second and third readings, at or above 140, averages 152.71 and would average 144.08 at the next visit, with 41.8% below 140. Discarding the first reading removes the drift and the first-reading transient; it does not remove the regression to the next day, which is larger than both.

The scenario is sensitive to its one external number, which is why Figure 2 draws two others. With 4 mmHg of day-to-day variation, 32.5% of the group selected on the first reading would be below 140 at the next visit; with 12 mmHg, 64.7%. With no day-to-day variation at all, only the within-sitting noise of a second sitting, the share would be 26.9%, close to the 23.0% observed within the first sitting and above it for two reasons: the transient that the first reading shares with the second does not carry over to another sitting, and the normal model, as the check above showed, overstates the share by about two points.

The same arithmetic changes the share of the population above a threshold, which is what a survey reports. A distribution of one-visit means is wider than the distribution of usual pressure, so more of it lies beyond a threshold above the mean. One way to estimate the distribution of usual values from single measurements is the adjustment the National Research Council (1986) proposed for usual dietary intakes: shrink each person's deviation from the group mean by the ratio of the between-person standard deviation to the observed one. Applied within age bands (18–29, 30–39 and so on to 70–80) for each sex, so that each person regresses towards their own group's mean, with the Muntner-based day-to-day variation, it gives 9.2% of adults with usual systolic pressure at or above 140, against 12.2% on the single visit, and 20.0% at or above 130, against 24.2%. The NRC procedure transforms skewed data towards normality before rescaling and transforms back afterwards; the calculation here rescales systolic pressure directly, which assumes it is close to normal within each age and sex group. These are shares of adults by measured pressure, not prevalences of hypertension, which also count people on treatment, and they inherit every assumption of the scenario. They show the direction and order of the correction, not its exact size.

Clinical guidance already contains the remedy, in the form Part 2 called averaging. The 2017 ACC/AHA guideline asks for an average of at least two readings obtained on at least two occasions before the level of blood pressure is estimated. The averaging rule that NHANES documented for its earlier reported values, and that the R package applies, discards the first reading and averages the rest. Both are designs that reduce the regression before any number is computed, and the arithmetic here says how much each of them buys: discarding the first reading removes about 1.2 mmHg of drift and most of the first-reading transient; averaging over occasions is the only one of the two that touches the day-to-day variation, which is the largest source of regression at a next visit.

## One cycle predicts the next, almost

So far the slope used to predict the selected groups was estimated on the same adults. A sharper test is to estimate it on one cycle and predict the other. Fitted on the 5,641 adults of 2009–2010 with that cycle's weights, the line is 10.14 + 0.9028 × first reading. Applied to the 4,981 adults of 2011–2012, it predicts the later mean of the top 10% by first reading at 152.64 mmHg; the observed mean is 154.55. For the top 1% it predicts 182.01 against an observed 185.18; for the bottom 10% it predicts 98.38 against 99.11. In shares of the deviation kept, the held-out selections keep between 0.907 and 0.938, against the training slope of 0.903.

The miss is small and it is systematic: every selection in the new cycle kept more of its deviation than the old line predicted. The cause is visible in the cycles themselves. The noise between the second and third readings is the same in both, 3.81 mmHg in 2009–2010 and 3.88 in 2011–2012. What changed is the first reading. The drift from the first reading to the later mean was 1.67 mmHg (SE 0.09) in 2009–2010 and 0.68 (SE 0.16) in 2011–2012, a difference of 0.98 (SE 0.18). The excess variance of the first reading over the third was 28.8 mmHg² (SE 4.7) in the first cycle and 12.6 (SE 5.7) in the second, a fall of 16.2 with a standard error of 7.4, and the extra covariance of the first two readings fell from 12.4 to 5.4 (difference 7.0, SE 4.1). The fall in the drift is clear; the fall in the transient's variance is about two standard errors, enough to take seriously and not enough to measure precisely. With them the slope rose from 0.903 to 0.925. Whether that reflects new examiners, retraining, a change in the room or in the instructions, the data do not record, and there is no basis here for choosing among those explanations.

The lesson does not depend on the explanation. A reliability is a property of a measurement process, not of a quantity, and when the process changes the regression changes with it. The 2009–2010 line still predicts the 2011–2012 selections to within about 3 mmHg at the extremes and much better in the middle, which is good enough for most purposes and not good enough for a claim that depends on a fraction of a millimetre.

## What a season of batting measures

A batting season is a sample. A regular hitter comes to the plate around five hundred times, and each plate appearance ends in one of a handful of outcomes: an out, a single, a double, a triple, a home run, a walk, a hit by pitch, a sacrifice. If the player's probabilities of those outcomes were fixed for the season, the counts would be a multinomial sample from them, and the sampling variance of any statistic built from the counts could be computed from one season alone, without a second season to compare with. The reliability of the season, the share of the variance across players that is not sampling noise, would follow.

The Lahman Baseball Database (Lahman 2026), read here through the tables of the R package of Friendly and colleagues, has every player's counts by season. The analysis keeps the 16,214 regular seasons from 1955 to 2025, defined as 300 or more plate appearances, and drops 2020, whose 60-game schedule left no one with 300. Each statistic is expressed as a deviation from the mean of that season's regulars, which removes the changes in the run environment from one era to another. For OPS, on-base percentage plus slugging percentage, the sampling variance comes from 400 multinomial draws per player-season with the player's own outcome shares; for the rates, it is the binomial p(1 − p)/n with the right denominator: plate appearances for strikeout, walk and home-run rates, and balls in play for BABIP, the batting average on balls in play.

The standard deviation of OPS across regular seasons is 0.0981, and the sampling standard deviation of a season's OPS is 0.0584. The reliability of one season is therefore 1 − 0.0584²/0.0981², or 0.645. For BABIP the two standard deviations are 0.032 and 0.025, and the reliability is 0.407. For strikeout rate they are 0.051 and 0.016, a reliability of 0.898; for walk rate 0.850 and for home-run rate 0.796.

These numbers use one season at a time. The 11,830 pairs of consecutive regular seasons give the year-to-year correlations to compare them with: 0.593 for OPS, 0.386 for BABIP, 0.839 for strikeout rate, 0.762 for walk rate and 0.745 for home-run rate. Figure 3 puts each correlation against the reliability computed from the sampling model. If a player's true rates were the same in both seasons, the correlation would equal the reliability; every point lies just below that line, and the ratio of the two, the implied correlation of true rates from one season to the next, is between 0.90 and 0.95 for four of the five statistics and 0.897 for walk rate. Most of what changes in a hitter's line from one season to the next is the binomial noise of a few hundred plate appearances, and the amount of it is computable from the first season alone.

The order of the components is the order of their regression. A season in the top tenth of regular seasons, pooled over 1955–2024, by strikeout rate keeps 0.823 of its distance from the mean into the next season; by walk rate 0.750; by home-run rate 0.679; by OPS 0.663; by BABIP 0.438. BABIP is the outlier, and the arithmetic says why without any appeal to defence or luck: the true spread of BABIP between regular hitters is narrow, a standard deviation of about 0.020 once the sampling variance is removed, while the sampling noise on the roughly 370 balls in play of a regular season is 0.025. A season's BABIP is three parts noise to two parts signal. What there is of the signal is stable; its implied correlation from one season to the next, 0.947, is the highest of the five.

## The sophomore slump

The selection with the most famous regression in baseball has a name. The Rookie of the Year was first awarded in 1947, and since 1949 each major league has named one every season, and the winners' second seasons are often worse than their first. The usual stories about why involve pitchers learning the hitter's weaknesses, or the distractions of success; those are conjectures about mechanisms that the counts in Lahman cannot test. Schall and Smith (2000) asked whether major league batting averages and earned run averages regress towards the mean from one season to the next. What the counts can test here is whether the second season of the winners is worse than the regression predicts.

From 1955 to 2024, leaving out 2019 and 2020 because the 2020 season cannot serve as a first or second season, there were 136 awards and 138 winners, the 1976 National League and 1979 American League awards having been shared. Of the winners, 101 had 300 or more plate appearances in the award season. The rookie rule in force since 1971 makes a player a rookie until he exceeds 130 career at-bats (or 50 innings pitched, or 45 days on an active roster) before the season; earlier rules used lower limits, and before 1957 there was no written rule. The at-bat part of the modern rule, applied to every season since 1955, defines the comparison group: 1,482 rookie seasons with 300 or more plate appearances, including all 101 by eventual winners.

Two problems have to be handled before winners can be compared with anyone. The first is survival. Only 67.8% of rookies who did not win had 300 or more plate appearances the next season, against 92.1% of winners; requiring a full second season keeps the non-winners who did well and drops those who did not, which is selection on the outcome. With a lower bar of 100 plate appearances, 89.0% of non-winners and all 101 winners qualify, and that is the main analysis; the other two bars are reported below. The second problem is age. The winners were younger, 22.83 years on average against 24.05, and younger players improve: among rookies who did not win, each extra year of age at the rookie season lowers the next season's OPS deviation by 0.0052.

Among the 1,229 non-winning rookies with 100 or more plate appearances the next season, the next season's OPS deviation is −0.0245 + 0.566 × rookie deviation − 0.0052 × (age − 24). The winners' rookie OPS was 0.0668 above the mean of regulars, and the next season it was 0.0280 above. In raw OPS, the winners went from 0.820 to 0.778. The line, applied to each winner's rookie OPS and age, predicts an average of 0.0193. The winners finished 0.0087 above the prediction, with a standard error of 0.0084: indistinguishable from zero, and if anything on the side of winners holding their level better than other rookies with the same numbers.

![Figure 4](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/regression-to-the-mean/part3/figures/fig4_rookies.png)

*Figure 4. Rookie seasons of 300 or more plate appearances from 1955 to 2024, with 100 or more the next season: rookie OPS against next-season OPS, both as deviations from the season mean of regulars. Grey: rookies who did not win; orange: Rookie of the Year winners. The solid line is fitted on the non-winners at age 24; the dashed line is no regression. The open square is the winners' mean rookie season, the filled square their mean next season, and the blue tick the prediction from the line with each winner's age. Lahman Baseball Database.*

The winners' observed fall was 0.0388 in OPS deviation; the predicted fall was 0.0475. The sophomore slump is regression, and the prediction expected a slightly larger slump than happened. The conclusion does not depend on the survival bar. With one plate appearance as the bar, the winners finish 0.0147 above the prediction (SE 0.0083); with 300, the 93 qualifying winners finish 0.0076 above (SE 0.0076). Selecting the top tenth of rookies by OPS instead of the award winners gives the same picture without a name attached: the 133 rookies in the top tenth fell from 0.1304 to 0.0547, and a simpler line, fitted on all rookies including that top tenth and without the age term, predicted 0.0542.

The composition of the winners' lines shows where the fall came from. Their BABIP was 0.0257 above the mean of regulars as rookies and 0.0110 above it the next season, and their home-run rate went from 0.0070 above to 0.0030 above; each kept a little over 40% of its rookie excess. Their strikeout rate fell towards the mean, from 0.0182 above it to 0.0098, which is an improvement, and their walk rate rose from slightly below the mean to slightly above it. With a hundred players and four components, these shares are too noisy to rank the components by reliability; what they show is that the fall was spread across the line rather than concentrated in one part of it.

That is also a rule for prediction. Among rookies, a regression of the next season's OPS deviation on the rookie OPS deviation, the rookie BABIP deviation and age gives a coefficient of −0.543 (SE 0.091) on BABIP, holding OPS and age fixed. Two rookies with the same OPS, one of whom got there with a BABIP 0.030 higher, should be expected to differ by 0.016 in OPS the next season, in favour of the one whose line rested on the more reliable components. A selection that loads on the noisy part of a composite regresses more than its correlation alone predicts.

Whether the award carries information beyond the rookie line is a conjecture the data cannot settle. The winners did no worse than rookies with the same OPS and age, and the point estimate says slightly better, but the difference is within its standard error. If it were real, a plausible source would be what the voters see and the line does not, such as the scouting reports, the minor-league record and the defence; that too is conjecture. What the data do support is narrower: the award does not pick players who are more likely than their numbers suggest to fall back.

## Wins beyond the runs

A team's winning percentage is a composite of the same kind. Part of it is explained by the runs a team scores and allows; part of it is the order in which those runs arrive, which decides how many one-run games are won and how many blowouts are wasted. Bill James's Pythagorean expectation (James 1980), which he wrote with an exponent of 2 and which is generalised here to R^k / (R^k + RA^k), turns runs into an expected winning percentage. Fitting the exponent to the 1,716 team seasons from 1961 to 2025 (without 2020) by least squares gives k = 1.831. The residual, actual minus expected winning percentage, has a standard deviation of 0.0251, about four wins over 162 games, and is uncorrelated with the expectation (correlation +0.001).

The two components behave very differently from one season to the next. Over 1,656 pairs of consecutive franchise seasons, the year-to-year correlation of the Pythagorean expectation is 0.571 and that of the residual 0.067. The residual is almost pure noise. Winning percentage, the sum of the two, has a correlation of 0.519.

Figure 5 shows what this does to selected teams. The top quarter by winning percentage was 14.5 wins above .500, of which 12.7 came from run differential and 1.8 from the residual. The next season they were 7.4 wins above .500: 7.3 from run differential and 0.0 from the residual. The residual vanished entirely and the run differential regressed with its own slope; a prediction that applies each component's slope to its part predicts a winning percentage 0.0451 above .500 for these teams, and they finished at 0.0455. The bottom quarter mirrors this: 14.9 wins below .500, of which 1.8 was residual; 7.7 below the next season, of which 0.2 was residual.

![Figure 5](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/regression-to-the-mean/part3/figures/fig5_teams.png)

*Figure 5. Wins above .500 over 162 games, split into the part explained by run differential (Pythagorean expectation, blue) and the residual (orange), for three selections of team seasons and for the same teams the next season. Major league teams 1961–2025 without 2020, 1,656 pairs of consecutive franchise seasons. Lahman Baseball Database.*

The selection that makes the point most directly is the top tenth of team seasons by the residual: the teams that won the most games beyond what their runs implied. Their run differential was exactly average (an expectation of 0.0001 above .500), and they were 7.0 wins above .500 entirely through the residual. The next season they were 1.8 wins above .500. The residual had gone, 0.2 wins of it remaining. The components prediction, which expects the residual to keep 7% of itself and the run differential 57%, predicts 0.0029 above .500, about half a win; they finished at 0.0110. The difference is a better run differential the next season, 1.6 wins on average with a standard error of 0.9, which is within two standard errors of zero. A single slope applied to their winning percentage would have predicted 3.6 wins, double what they kept, because it treats their surplus as if it had the average mix of skill and luck. Teams selected for luck keep a quarter of their surplus; teams selected for winning keep half; the difference is the mix of components their selection loaded on.

## Twenty-four predictions, checked on later data

The sections above estimated most slopes on the same data in which the selections were made. The final test is to fit on one period and predict another. For blood pressure that was the 2009–2010 cycle against 2011–2012. For baseball, the slopes come from pairs whose first season is before 1990 (5,266 batting pairs from 1955 and 688 team pairs from 1961), and the selections are made in pairs from 1990 onward (6,564 batting pairs and 968 team pairs). Each prediction is the share of the selected group's deviation that survives to the next occasion. For blood pressure and batting the deviation is measured from the mean of everyone in the held-out data, (mean next − overall mean) / (mean first − overall mean); for teams it is measured from .500, and for the award winners from the season mean of regulars. The Pythagorean exponent used for the team components is fitted on all seasons, including the held-out ones; fitted on 1961–1989 alone it is 1.836 instead of 1.831, which changes nothing that is reported.

Twenty-four selections went into the check: six by systolic pressure; five by OPS and two each by BABIP, strikeout, walk and home-run rate; four of teams; and the Rookie of the Year winners since 1990, predicted from the line fitted on non-winning rookies before 1990. The mean absolute difference between predicted and observed kept shares is 0.042. The 49 winners since 1990 kept 0.509 of their rookie deviation, and the line fitted on earlier non-winners predicted 0.509: an OPS deviation of 0.0463 predicted and 0.0463 observed. Teams in the top quarter by wins kept 0.509 against 0.537 predicted; the luckiest tenth of teams kept 0.201 against 0.093.

The left panel of Figure 6 shows the points against the diagonal. Most sit close to it. The ones that do not fall into three groups, each with its own cause, and two misses that the data here do not explain: the luckiest tenth of teams (observed 0.201 against 0.093, the improvement in run differential already described) and the bottom tenth by BABIP (0.347 against 0.412).

![Figure 6](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/regression-to-the-mean/part3/figures/fig6_calibration.png)

*Figure 6. Left: for 24 selections made in held-out data, the predicted share of the selected deviation that survives to the next occasion against the observed share; the six blood-pressure selections overlap near 0.9. Predictions are fitted on 2009–2010 for blood pressure and on the seasons before 1990 for baseball. Right: for OPS selections from 1990 to 2024, the observed kept share (black) against three predictions fitted on 1955–1989: one slope for everyone (blue), a player-specific reliability from each season's sampling variance (violet), and Tweedie's formula with a density estimated by Lindsey's method (orange).*

The first is the blood-pressure cycle shift already described: all six held-out selections sit above the diagonal by 0.004 to 0.035, because the first-reading transient shrank between the cycles. The second is the home-run and strikeout rates. The top tenth by home-run rate kept 0.671 of its deviation, against a slope of 0.760; the bottom tenth kept 0.807. For strikeout rate the order is the same, 0.825 at the top and 0.872 at the bottom. A binomial rate has a sampling variance p(1 − p)/n that grows with p when p is well below one half, so the hitters with the most home runs and the most strikeouts have the noisiest rates, and they regress more. A reliability computed for each player from his own sampling variance captures this for home-run rate: it predicts 0.668 for the top tenth and 0.843 for the bottom tenth. It does not help elsewhere. For the top tenth by strikeout rate it predicts 0.778, against 0.825 observed and 0.826 from the single slope, and across the ten batting selections its mean absolute error is 0.070, against 0.050 for the single slope.

The third is OPS, where the same fix goes the wrong way. The top 1% of hitters by OPS kept 0.727 of their deviation, the top 5% 0.729 and the top 10% 0.691, against a single slope of 0.618; the bottom 10% kept 0.534 and the bottom 1% 0.512. The top hitters regress less than the slope predicts and the bottom hitters more. The player-specific reliability predicts the opposite, 0.527 for the top 1% and 0.621 for the bottom 10%, because the best hitters, despite more plate appearances (608 on average in the top 1% against 463 in the bottom tenth), have the noisiest OPS: a line full of home runs and walks has a larger sampling variance than a line of singles. The extremes are set by something else.

Two candidates are visible in the data. One is survival: the pairs require 300 plate appearances in the second season, and only 55.5% of the bottom tenth of regular seasons by OPS that were followed by another season reached that bar, against 93.6% of the top tenth. Conditioning on survival removes some of the bottom hitters who stayed bad, which pushes the bottom tenth's kept share down. The other is the shape of the distribution, the result Part 2 derived for skewed populations. The OPS deviations of regular seasons are skewed to the right (skewness 0.307 up to 1989). A plausible reason, offered as an explanation rather than a finding, is that regulars are the hitters managers chose to play, so the distribution of their talent is cut off from below and has a long tail of stars above; an OPS far above the mean is then more often talent than an OPS equally far below. The single slope cannot know the shape. Tweedie's formula can: for an observation x = θ + e with normal noise e of variance σ², the posterior mean of θ is

E[θ | x] = x + σ² × d/dx log f(x),

where f is the density of the observations themselves. Efron (2011) revived the formula, which Robbins (1956) attributed to Tweedie, as a correction for selection bias: it requires no model of the true values, only the marginal density of what was observed and the size of the noise. The density can be estimated by Lindsey's method, a Poisson regression of histogram counts on a polynomial in the bin centre (Efron and Tibshirani 1996).

```python
def lindsey_score(x, bins=80, degree=7):
    """d/dx log f(x) from a Poisson regression of histogram counts on a polynomial."""
    lo, hi = np.quantile(x, [0.0005, 0.9995])
    edges = np.linspace(lo, hi, bins + 1)
    mid = (edges[:-1] + edges[1:]) / 2
    y, _ = np.histogram(x, edges)
    c, s = mid.mean(), mid.std()
    Z = np.vander((mid - c) / s, degree + 1, increasing=True)
    beta = np.zeros(degree + 1); beta[0] = np.log(y.mean())
    for _ in range(200):                                   # IRLS
        eta = Z @ beta; mu = np.exp(eta)
        beta = np.linalg.solve(Z.T @ (mu[:, None] * Z), Z.T @ (mu * (eta + (y - mu) / mu)))
    return lambda v: sum(beta[j] * j * ((np.clip(v, lo, hi) - c) / s) ** (j - 1)
                         for j in range(1, degree + 1)) / s

def tweedie(x, noise_var, score):
    return x + noise_var * score(x)
```

Fitted on the regular seasons up to 1989, with the average sampling variance of OPS (0.00312) as the noise and a persistence of 0.884 fitted on the training pairs, Tweedie's formula predicts kept shares of 0.741 for the top 1% from 1990 onward, 0.680 for the top 5%, 0.664 for the top 10%, 0.641 for the top 25%, 0.584 for the bottom 25%, 0.559 for the bottom 10% and 0.502 for the bottom 1%. The observed shares are 0.727, 0.729, 0.691, 0.617, 0.555, 0.534 and 0.512. The mean absolute difference over the seven selections is 0.025, against 0.078 for the single slope and 0.113 for the player-specific reliability. The result does not depend on the polynomial: with degree 5 the predictions for the top and bottom 1% are 0.714 and 0.505, and with degree 9 they are 0.768 and 0.496.

Because Tweedie's formula uses only the distribution of the first seasons, it captures the shape and not the survival, so its success at both ends says that the shape does most of the work; the survival filter may account for part of the remaining gap at the bottom. The same formula with a common noise variance fails for the rates, and for the reason already given: their noise is not common. For home-run rate it predicts that the bottom tenth will keep 0.323 of its deviation, against 0.807 observed, and the top tenth 0.741, against 0.671; for strikeout rate it predicts 0.760 for the bottom tenth, against 0.872. A low rate is measured precisely and the formula assumes it is measured as noisily as everyone else. A correct treatment would combine the two corrections, a noise variance for each player and a density for the population, which is the full empirical Bayes problem with heteroscedastic noise. The data here show why each half is needed; they do not need the combination to make the point.

## What the data could not settle

Several quantities above rest on assumptions that the data cannot check, and it is worth stating them in one place.

The next-visit numbers for blood pressure use a visit-to-visit variance from NHANES III, measured two decades earlier on adults who attended three visits in more than one setting, converted with a correction that gives an average rather than a root-mean-square standard deviation, and a normal model that is too steep near the threshold and overstates the share falling below it by about two points in the one place it can be checked. The direction of every conclusion drawn from them is robust over the range from 4 to 12 mmHg drawn in Figure 2; the sizes are not.

The interpretation of the first reading's extra variance as a transient response to being measured is consistent with the pattern of covariances and with the physiology described by Mancia and colleagues, and it is still an interpretation. A component shared by the first two readings and absent from the third could also come from the procedure, for instance from how the cuff is applied before the first inflation. The data identify the pattern, not the mechanism. The fall of the transient between the 2009–2010 and 2011–2012 cycles is an estimate, 16 ± 7 mmHg² in the excess variance of the first reading, without a recorded cause.

The usual-pressure shares apply the NRC rescaling without the normalising transformation the NRC procedure calls for, so they assume systolic pressure is close to normal within each age and sex group, and they use the same day-to-day variance for every age and sex; Muntner and colleagues found visit-to-visit variability larger in older adults and in women, so the correction is probably too small for some groups and too large for others. The analysis also leaves out the 10.6% of the weighted adult population without three readings, with no adjustment for non-response.

In baseball, the rookie definition applies the 1971 rule backwards to seasons in which the eligibility rules were different, so the comparison group is an approximation to the rookies of each era. The 300-plate-appearance bar for a regular is the same in every season, including the strike-shortened seasons of 1981 and 1994, which have fewer regulars than the others. The age coefficient is a linear term in a relationship that is not linear across a career. The sampling model treats each plate appearance as independent with fixed probabilities, which ignores the park, the opposing pitchers and in-season changes in a player's skill; the implied correlations of true rates of about 0.9 to 0.95 absorb all of that. The pair samples require 300 plate appearances in both seasons, which conditions on the second season, and that condition is not innocent at the bottom of the distribution, where 55.5% of the bottom tenth of regular seasons by OPS that were followed by another season reached 300 plate appearances in it, against 93.6% of the top tenth.

## What carries over

Every analysis above began from a different place and arrived at the same three-part account: a drift that belongs to the population, a regression that belongs to the selection and is predictable from the reliability, and a residual that is all that can be called an effect. In the blood-pressure readings the residual was within two standard errors of zero at every threshold, though the young high readers fell less than predicted. In the Rookie of the Year winners it was zero within its standard error, slightly positive. In the teams selected for luck it was a better run differential the next season, on the edge of what two standard errors allow. Neither of the named selections, the high readers and the award winners, fell by more than the regression predicted beyond its standard error, and the falls that are folk knowledge, the high readers who calm down and the rookies who slump, are what the correlation between occasions says they should be.

The predictions were good where the assumptions behind them held and wrong in instructive ways where they did not. A single slope predicted the middle of every distribution well. At the extremes it held for blood pressure within a sitting and for the top tenths by BABIP, strikeout rate and walk rate, and it failed for OPS, for the home-run rate, for the bottom tenth by strikeout rate and for young high readers, in directions set by the shape of the distribution and by how the noise depends on the level. When the measurement process changed between periods, the slope changed with it. When a selection loaded on a component of a composite rather than on the composite itself, the regression followed the component's reliability, not the composite's. And a reliability is only as good as the interval it was measured over: one season of batting predicted the next well because the underlying skill changes slowly, while three readings in one sitting overstate the reliability that matters at the next visit by an amount that, in these data, has to come from outside.

For anyone who selects on a measurement, which is anyone who flags the top decile, ranks units or screens a population, the practice that follows from these data is concrete. Estimate the drift from the whole population and subtract it. Estimate the reliability from what is known about the noise, from repeated readings or a sampling model, before looking at the outcome. Predict the selected group's next value from that reliability, among units like the selected ones, and check whether the slope holds at the extremes of the distribution or whether its shape, or noise that depends on the level, bends it. Only then compare with what was observed. A fall that matches the prediction is regression. A fall larger than the prediction is a candidate for an effect, to be tested by the designs of Part 2.

The last article in the series takes this into the places where data scientists select on noisy measurements as a matter of routine: picking the winners of A/B tests, intervening on the worst-performing units, and alerting on the extremes of a metric.
