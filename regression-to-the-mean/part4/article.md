# Where Data Scientists Select on Noise: A/B Test Winners, Programmes for the Worst Performers and Alerts on Daily Metrics

## Part 4 of 4. The winner's curse in a simulated test archive and in choosing the best carrier on a route; the NSW job-training experiment against comparison groups selected the same way; alerts on daily late-flight shares; and what the series adds up to

A dashboard sorted by a metric is a selection. So is the variant that won a test, the list of stores that get a visit from the regional manager, the segment flagged by an anomaly detector and the customer who receives a retention offer because his spending fell. Each of these picks units by the value of a measurement, and each measurement is part signal and part noise. The previous three articles in this series showed what follows: the selected units come back towards the mean on the next occasion, by an amount that depends on the correlation between occasions and on how far into the tail the selection reached, and anything measured on them afterwards includes that return whether or not anything was done.

This article is about the three places where that happens most often in data-science work. The first is picking the winner of an experiment with several arms, where the measured lead of the winner overstates its real lead. The second is a programme aimed at the worst performers, where the improvement after the programme overstates its effect. The third is an alert on a daily metric, where the improvement after the response overstates the response. They are one phenomenon in three costumes, and the arithmetic that sizes it is the same in each: the share of a selected unit's deviation that survives to the next measurement is close to the reliability of the measurement, with corrections for the shape of the distribution and for noise that varies between units.

The data are of three kinds. For experiments, the analysis uses a simulated archive of click-through tests, because in a simulation the true effect of every arm is known and the methods can be checked against it; the assumptions are stated where they are used, and the same procedures are then checked on a real choice among options, the carrier with the best on-time record on each New York route, using the flights that left New York in 2013. For programmes aimed at the worst performers, the data are the National Supported Work demonstration, a randomised job-training experiment from the 1970s whose control group shows what a group selected at its lowest does with no intervention, and two non-experimental comparison groups that show what happens when that control group is replaced by an outside one. For alerts, the data are daily late-arrival shares by carrier and by carrier and airport, again from the New York flights of 2013. Every number below is printed by a script in the series repository.

## Picking the winner of a test

The term comes from offshore oil. Capen, Clapp and Campbell (1971), researchers at the Atlantic Richfield Company, observed that the company winning a lease in a sealed-bid auction was systematically the one whose estimate of the lease's value was most optimistic, and that winners therefore overpaid on average even when every bidder's estimate was unbiased. A test with several arms has the same structure. Each arm's measured click rate is an unbiased estimate of its true rate. The winner is the arm whose estimate came out highest, and among several unbiased estimates the highest is biased upwards. The more arms there are and the noisier the estimates, the larger the bias.

The simulated archive has 6,000 tests. Each has between two and six arms, with 30% of tests having two arms, 25% three, 20% four, 15% five and 10% six. Each test has its own base click rate, drawn from a beta distribution with a mean of 1.5%, and each arm's true rate is the base rate times one plus a relative effect, kept between 0.01% and 50%. The arms receive impressions drawn from a lognormal distribution with a median of 5,000 and a log-scale standard deviation of 0.6, with a floor of 200. Three versions of the archive differ only in the relative effects: in the first they follow a t distribution with three degrees of freedom and a scale of 5%, so that most arms differ from the base by a few per cent and a few by much more; in the second they are normal with a standard deviation of 8%; in the third they are t with a scale of 10%. None of these values is an estimate of any company's experiments. They were chosen to put the signal below the noise in the first two archives and close to it in the third, because the ratio of the two is what decides the answer, and every team can measure that ratio in its own archive.

To have a replication without running each test twice, each arm's impressions and clicks are split at random into two halves. In the simulation, where every click is an independent draw at the arm's true rate, this is exactly what two tests of half the size would have produced. The winner is chosen on half A, and its lead over the other arms of the same test is then measured on half B, where it was not selected. Arms with no clicks in one of the halves are dropped (12, 10 and 35 of roughly 21,000 in the three archives), which leaves 5,998 tests in the first. Leads are measured as differences in log click rate, averaged over tests, and reported as the percentage difference in click rate that the average log difference corresponds to. Because each half has half the traffic, the numbers below describe tests of a median 2,500 impressions per arm.

In the first archive, the winner's click rate in the half in which it won was, on average, 30.1% above the other arms'. In the other half it was 5.2% above. The true lead, which a simulation knows, was 4.4%. Five-sixths of the measured lead was the selection. The half-A winner was the arm with the highest true rate in 48.0% of the tests.

Figure 1 breaks this down by the number of arms. The measured lead grows with the number of arms, from 26.7% with two arms to 35.1% with six, because the maximum of more noisy estimates is higher. The lead in the other half barely moves, between 4.8% and 5.6%, and the true lead rises only from 3.9% to 5.3%. More arms make the winner look better and do little to make it better, which is the same result Part 2 derived for selecting a smaller share of a population: the further into the tail the selection reaches, the larger the selected group's measured deviation, and with low reliability nearly all of the extra deviation is noise.

The overstatement is only half of the cost. With estimates this noisy, the choice itself is often wrong: the arm that won half A was the truly best arm in 48.0% of the tests. Had an oracle picked the truly best arm in every test, the average true lead would have been 10.1%; choosing on half the traffic captured 45% of that. No correction applied after the fact recovers the other 55%, because it was lost when the wrong arm was chosen. Only more traffic per arm, fewer arms or a confirmation stage that can overturn the first choice can recover it, and the reliability says which of these is worth its cost.

The quantity that sizes the problem is the reliability of one arm's estimate, the share of the variance of the arms' measured deviations from their test's mean that is variance in their true deviations. It can be estimated from the archive without knowing any true effect: the variance of the measured deviations across all arms, minus the average sampling variance of one arm's deviation, is the variance of the true deviations. In the first archive this estimate puts the standard deviation of the true deviations at 0.079 on the log scale, against an assumed noise of 0.171 per arm, for a reliability of 0.174. The simulation knows the actual values: 0.073 and 0.172, for a reliability of 0.152. With the full traffic instead of half, the estimated reliability would be 0.296. If the true effects were normal and every arm had the same normal noise, the expected true lead of the winner would be the reliability times its measured lead, whatever the number of arms; this is the formula of Part 2 applied to a selection of one unit from k. The winner kept 0.194 of its measured lead in the other half and 0.164 in truth.

The other two archives follow the same rule. With normal effects and an estimated reliability of 0.168 (actual 0.133), the winner's 30.3% measured lead became 3.7% in the other half against a true 4.4%. With larger heavy-tailed effects and an estimated reliability of 0.429 (actual 0.419), the measured lead of 36.6% became 13.0% in the other half and 13.0% in truth; the winner kept 0.392 of its lead and was the truly best arm in 58.6% of the tests.

Two filters that teams apply to winners change these numbers in a predictable direction. The first is significance. Most teams launch a winner only if its lead is statistically significant, and that filter selects on the same noise a second time. In the first archive, 14.5% of the winners had a lead significant at the 5% level against the average of the other arms. Their measured lead was 64.7%; in the other half it was 12.7%, and the true lead was 11.7%. The significant winners are better than the others, so the filter does useful work, but their measured lead overstates the real one by a factor of about five and a half. The winners that missed significance had a measured lead of 25.0% and a true one of 3.2%. Gelman and Carlin (2014) called this exaggeration factor the type M error of a significant result, and in a low-powered test it is large exactly because only the estimates that happened to be large get through.

The second filter is size. Splitting the first archive into thirds by impressions per arm, the share of the measured lead that is real rises from 0.124 in the smallest third (a median of 2,022 impressions per arm in each half) to 0.162 in the middle and 0.219 in the largest (3,960), and the measured lead falls from 34.9% to 25.4% while the true lead rises from 3.8% to 5.1%. Larger tests have more reliable arms, so they regress less, and the most spectacular leads in an archive of tests come disproportionately from the smallest tests. This is the same funnel that Part 2 showed for units of different size and that the alert section below shows for segments of different size.

The practical question is how to estimate a winner's true lead from the half in which it won, before any replication. The answer is to shrink each arm's measured deviation towards zero by an amount learned from the whole archive, the empirical-Bayes procedure Part 2 introduced with Kelley's formula, and there are two ways to learn it. The simplest assumes the true deviations are normal and shrinks each one by its own reliability, the estimated true variance over the true variance plus that arm's sampling variance. The other estimates the distribution of the true deviations without assuming a shape, by the nonparametric maximum-likelihood method of Kiefer and Wolfowitz (1956): a fixed grid of possible true values, and weights on the grid chosen to maximise the likelihood of all the measured deviations, each with its own sampling variance. The posterior mean of each arm's true deviation then follows from the grid. The grid here has 241 points between −1.2 and 1.2 on the log scale, wide enough to hold all but a handful of the true deviations (one in the first archive and 19 of about 21,000 in the third), and the estimated weights concentrate on a narrow part of it; the method chooses the shape of the prior, including how heavy its tails are, from the data. It needs a sampling variance for each arm, which a click-through rate supplies through the binomial model, and it treats each measured deviation as normal around its true value, which on the log scale is an approximation when an arm has only a few dozen clicks. Jiang and Zhang (2009) and Koenker and Mizera (2014) showed how well, and how quickly, this works for large collections of means, which is what an archive of tests is.

```python
def npmle_prior(d, v, grid, iters=400):
    """Weights on a grid of true values for d_i ~ N(theta_i, v_i), by EM."""
    loglike = -0.5 * (d[:, None] - grid[None, :]) ** 2 / v[:, None]
    like = np.exp(loglike - loglike.max(1, keepdims=True))
    w = np.full(len(grid), 1 / len(grid))
    for _ in range(iters):
        post = like * w
        post /= post.sum(1, keepdims=True)
        w = post.mean(0)
    return w

def posterior_mean(d, v, grid, w):
    loglike = -0.5 * (d[:, None] - grid[None, :]) ** 2 / v[:, None]
    post = np.exp(loglike - loglike.max(1, keepdims=True)) * w
    return (post * grid).sum(1) / post.sum(1)
```

Fitted on half A of the first archive, the nonparametric prior predicts a winner's lead of 4.0%, against a true 4.4% and a replicated 5.2%. The normal prior predicts 5.9%. The same ordering holds in the other two archives: the nonparametric predictions are 4.1% against a true 4.4%, and 12.5% against a true 13.0%, while the normal ones are 5.6% and 16.0%. The simulation can say why the normal prior over-predicts, because it knows the true variance of the effects. The main reason is the estimate of that variance. When the signal is a small part of the measured variance, the estimate is a small difference between two large numbers, and a small error in the noise variance becomes a large error in the signal variance. Here the binomial model puts the noise at 0.171 on the log scale, while the actual noise in the simulated halves is 0.172 in the first archive and 0.174 in the second; with a few dozen clicks per arm, the normal approximation to a log rate is slightly off. That error of 1% to 2% in the noise standard deviation inflates the estimated variance of the true deviations by 16% in the first archive and 28% in the second, and a prior that is too wide shrinks too little. With the actual variance put in, the normal prior predicts 5.2% in the first archive and 4.7% in the second, against a true 4.4% in both. What remains in the first archive is the second reason: with heavy-tailed effects, a normal prior shrinks the genuinely large effects too much and the many small ones too little, and among winners the small ones dominate. In the third archive, where the variance is estimated almost exactly, the normal prior still predicts 15.8% with the actual variance against a true 13.0%, and that excess is all tails.

The nonparametric prior came closer in all three archives, but its advantage should not be taken as general. It relies on the same normal approximation to the noise, and when it is given the binomial variances of the true rates instead of the measured ones it predicts 5.1% in the first archive and 4.9% in the second, no better than the corrected normal prior. Part of its accuracy here comes from errors that happen to offset. The lesson that does generalise is that the size of the shrinkage is decided by an estimate of the signal variance, that this estimate is fragile when reliability is low, and that an archive large enough to estimate the shape of the distribution of effects is also large enough to check the estimate by splitting it, as done here.

Real archives of this kind exist. The Upworthy Research Archive (Matias et al. 2021), a uniquely large public one, holds 32,487 experiments on packages of headline, image and excerpt, with the impressions and clicks of every arm, and the functions in the repository take any table of tests, arms, impressions and clicks without change. Splitting the aggregated counts of a real archive into halves is a weaker check than in a simulation, though. It reproduces the binomial noise and nothing else: the clustering of impressions by user, the novelty of a new headline and changes over the days a test ran all affect both halves alike, so the second half is a model-based check rather than a replication. A confirmation on fresh traffic is the real thing.

*Figure 2. The last row of Figure 2 is real data, and it is there because the same procedure should work outside a simulation. Of the routes flown from New York in 2013, 96 were served by two or more carriers with at least 20 flights each on odd days and on even days of the month. Choosing the carrier with the best on-time share on each route is a selection of a winner among two to five options. Splitting the year by odd and even days gives two halves with the same seasons in each. The carriers' true differences are large relative to the noise: the standard deviation of the true deviations in on-time share is estimated at 0.058, the binomial noise at 0.026, and the reliability at 0.828. Flights on the same day share delays, so the noise is somewhat larger than the binomial model says and the reliability somewhat lower, but not enough to change the picture. The best carrier on odd days led the other carriers on its route by 10.4 percentage points on odd days and by 8.7 on even days, keeping 0.836 of its lead. The normal prior, fitted on odd days only, predicted 8.7 points and the nonparametric one 8.8. The same carrier was best on even days on 80.2% of the routes. This is not an experiment, since carriers on a route fly at different times of day, with different aircraft and different connections, and the comparison measures all of that together; but the arithmetic of choosing a winner does not care why the arms differ, only how reliably the difference is measured, and the predictions from the odd days held on the even ones.

The contrast between the rows of Figure 2 is the point. A carrier's on-time record over a year is a reliable measurement, and the winner's curse on it is a sixth of the lead. A click-through difference between headlines measured on a few thousand impressions is not, and the curse there can be four-fifths of the lead or more. The procedure that sizes it is identical, and the input it needs, the reliability of one measurement, comes from the archive of past selections, not from the test at hand.

Three practices follow. Report a winner's lead with the shrinkage applied, or alongside it, because the unshrunk number is what will be used to forecast the impact of launching the winner, and that forecast will be wrong by the factor the reliability says. When a decision depends on the size of the lead, confirm the winner on fresh traffic, which is the second baseline of Part 2: a measurement that played no part in the selection. And keep the archive. Every past test is information about the distribution of true effects, and that distribution is what turns a measured lead into an estimate of a real one. A team without an archive does not know its reliability, and until it has a few hundred tests to estimate it from, the confirmation stage is the only safe way to report a winner's size. The estimators that do this formally for a single test, conditioning on the fact that the reported arm won, are reviewed by Andrews, Kitagawa and McCloskey (2024); Lee and Shen (2018) describe the bias as it appears when the effects of many launched features are summed.

## Programmes for the worst performers

The second routine is the intervention aimed at the bottom of a ranking: the stores with the worst month, the students with the lowest scores, the customers whose spending fell, the job-seekers with the least work. Whatever the intervention, the group was chosen because its last measurement was low, and a low measurement is partly a low state that will not last. The measurement after the intervention will be higher whether or not the intervention did anything.

The cleanest public demonstration of this is half a century old, and it is worth recomputing because it carries a control group that most business programmes never have. The National Supported Work demonstration (NSW) was a programme of the mid-1970s that gave disadvantaged workers a guaranteed job for nine to eighteen months, with applicants assigned at random to the programme or to a control group. LaLonde (1986) used its samples of women and of men to test whether non-experimental methods could recover the experimental answer, and Dehejia and Wahba (1999) built the subsample of men used here, 185 trainees and 260 controls, for whom real earnings, in 1982 dollars, are available for 1974, 1975 and 1978. The 1978 earnings were measured after the programme. Eligibility required being out of work at the time of applying, so the programme selected people at a low point.

The controls received nothing from the programme. Their mean earnings were $2,107 in 1974, $1,267 in 1975 and $4,555 in 1978. The fall from 1974 to 1975 is the pattern Ashenfelter (1978) found in the earnings of trainees before they enter a programme, now called Ashenfelter's dip: people enter training when their earnings are unusually low. The rise from 1975 to 1978, $3,288 with a standard error of $376, is what the controls did with no training at all. Some of it is the general growth of earnings, and most of it is the recovery of a group selected at its lowest. The recovery is mostly people returning to work at all: 68.5% of the controls had no earnings in 1975 and 35.4% had none in 1978. A group defined by being out of work is, a few years later, a group of which most are in work, programme or no programme.

The trainees went from $1,532 in 1975 to $6,349 in 1978, a rise of $4,817 (SE 608). That is the number a before-and-after evaluation would report. The experimental estimate, the difference between trainees and controls in 1978, is $1,794 (SE 671); the difference between the two groups' changes, which uses the 1975 baseline as well, is $1,529 (SE 715), and the two agree because randomisation balanced the baseline (the trainees earned $265 more in 1975, with a standard error of $305). The before-and-after estimate is 2.7 times the experimental one. Of the trainees' rise, 68% happened to the controls too.

Most programmes aimed at the worst performers have no control group, and the usual repair is a comparison group from outside: other stores, other students, a survey sample. LaLonde's point was that this repair can fail badly, and the earnings data show exactly how. Two comparison groups come with the data, a sample from the Panel Study of Income Dynamics (PSID, 2,490 men) and one from the Current Population Survey (CPS, 15,992 men). Neither was selected at a low point, and their earnings grew by $2,491 and $1,196 from 1975 to 1978, less than the NSW controls' $3,288. A difference-in-differences against them credits the programme with the recovery: $2,327 against the PSID and $3,621 against the CPS, against an experimental $1,794.

The obvious fix is to select the comparison group the way the programme selected its participants. Restricting the comparison groups to men with no earnings in 1975, or with less than $2,000, produces groups that do recover: by $5,426 and $5,460 in the PSID and by $3,978 and $4,727 in the CPS. Against all the trainees, the difference-in-differences estimates become −$609 and −$643 against the PSID and +$839 and +$90 against the CPS. They now miss in the other direction. Part of the reason is that the rule selects more deeply than the programme did: not every NSW participant had zero earnings in 1975, and the NSW controls who did recovered by $4,216, not $3,288. A comparison selected more deeply than the treated group regresses more than the treated group would have.

The fair version of the fix applies the same rule to everyone, trainees, controls and comparison group alike, and compares the difference-in-differences with the experimental estimate for the same subgroup. Among men with no 1975 earnings, the experiment says $1,711 (SE 739); the CPS comparison gives $1,949 and the PSID $501. Among men with less than $2,000 in 1975, the experiment says $1,857; the CPS gives $1,405 and the PSID $672. Among men with less than $2,000 in both 1974 and 1975, the experiment says $2,247; the CPS gives $2,462 and the PSID $3,833. Selected by the same rule, the CPS comparison lands within one standard error of the experiment each time. The PSID misses by $1,200 to $1,600, in both directions.

A low year in the PSID is a different event from a low year in the NSW population. Of the PSID men who earned less than $2,000 in 1975, 29.2% had earned $5,000 or more the year before; among the CPS men under the same threshold the share was 14.0%, and among the NSW controls under it, 4.2%. A plausible reading, and it is no more than that, is that a man in a household survey with a bad year is more often a man with a steady job who lost it briefly, whose earnings come back. The depth of the selection differs too. A threshold of $2,000 sits 1.25 standard deviations below the PSID's mean 1975 earnings and 1.26 below the CPS's, while among the NSW controls, whose mean was $1,267, it sits above the mean. Each population's own regression line predicts its selected group well: the line of 1978 on 1975 earnings within the PSID predicts $5,727 for its low earners, who earned $5,610, and within the CPS $5,612, against $5,100.

This is the thesis of the series in the form that matters for practice. The size of the regression depends on the selection rule, on how deep into its own population the rule reaches and on the correlation in that population, and a comparison group that shares the rule but not the population need not share the regression. The non-experimental literature that followed LaLonde, from Dehejia and Wahba's propensity-score estimates to Smith and Todd's (2005) reassessment of them, is in large part an argument about how much conditioning on pre-programme earnings and characteristics can fix this, and the answer depends on the sample and the method; here one survey reproduces the experiment under a matched rule and the other does not.

What reliably fixes it is a comparison group drawn from the flagged units themselves. The NSW control group is one; in a business setting it is a random holdout among the units that qualify for the intervention. The cost of the holdout is the intervention not given to the units held out, and the precision it buys can be computed from the spread of the outcome, which in a real programme would come from past data before the programme starts; here the spread of 1978 earnings stands in for it.

```python
def holdout_se(sd_treated, sd_control, n, share):
    """Standard error of treated-minus-holdout when `share` of n flagged units is held out at random."""
    return math.sqrt(sd_treated ** 2 / (n * (1 - share)) + sd_control ** 2 / (n * share))
```

Using the spread of 1978 earnings in the NSW data, a standard deviation of $7,867 among trainees and $5,484 among controls, a programme flagging 1,000 people and holding out 10% of them at random would estimate the effect with a standard error of $608; holding out 20% would bring it to $477, and 5% to $816. The bias of the before-and-after estimate in the same data was about $3,000. Even a small holdout estimates the effect with an error several times smaller than the bias it removes, and for a programme that flags 5,000 units a 5% holdout gives a standard error of $365.

When a holdout is impossible, the other designs of Part 2 apply: a regression discontinuity at the eligibility threshold, a second baseline that played no part in the selection, or a prediction of the regression from the correlation between periods in the population the units were selected from, estimated before the programme starts. The last of these needs the right population and the right interval, and the NSW data show how much both matter. The correlation between 1974 and 1975 earnings is 0.675 among the NSW controls, 0.841 in the PSID and 0.870 in the CPS; and within the NSW controls the correlation between 1975 and 1978 earnings is only 0.088, so even the right population's pre-programme correlation would have predicted the wrong amount of regression across the programme years.

The same thing happens without any programme, and it is worth seeing in a form closer to a business scorecard. Take every carrier-route out of New York with at least 20 flights in each half of 2013, 304 of them, and rank them by on-time share from January to June. The worst tenth, 30 carrier-routes, ran at 0.509 against an average of 0.704. From July to December, with nothing done to them by anyone analysing these data, they ran at 0.640 against an average of 0.735: they kept 0.487 of their gap to the mean. The best tenth went from 0.853 to 0.830 and kept 0.640 of theirs. A manager who put the worst tenth on an improvement plan in July would have seen half the gap close by December.

What makes this example useful is that the sampling noise in a half-year on-time share is small. With a few hundred flights per carrier-route, the reliability of a half-year share as a measure of that half-year is 0.905 under the binomial model, which ignores that flights on the same day share delays; noise accounts for about a tenth of the variance, somewhat more once that sharing is allowed for. Yet the correlation between the two halves is 0.671, and the worst tenth closed half its gap. The regression here is not mostly measurement error. It is the second of the three sources in Part 2, a state that does not persist. A reasonable reading, though the data do not record the reasons, is that a carrier's performance on a route was genuinely bad in the first half for reasons that did not last into the second, such as a schedule, a season, a run of weather at the destination or a crew shortage. A reliability computed from sampling noise within a period says how well the period was measured. It says nothing about how much of the period's performance will persist, and only the correlation across the interval that the decision spans can say that. Part 3 met the same problem with blood pressure, where three readings in one sitting are highly reliable and a reading on another day, in a scenario built from an outside estimate of day-to-day variation, is much less so.

## Alerts

The third routine selects on a single unit's latest observation. A metric is monitored daily, an alert fires when it crosses a threshold, someone investigates, something is changed, and the metric is better the next day. Every alert is a selection on an extreme value of a noisy series, and the improvement after it is, until shown otherwise, regression.

The flights that left New York's three airports in 2013 make a good test bed, because the metric is an ordinary operational one and the data contain no interventions: nothing in nycflights13 records anyone responding to anything. Of the 336,776 flights, 26.6% arrived 15 or more minutes late or did not arrive at all. The daily share of late flights across all carriers has a mean of 0.262, a standard deviation of 0.163 and a correlation of 0.407 with the previous day, 0.136 with the day before that and 0.095 with the same day a week earlier. Bad days come in short runs.

The alert rule is the kind a monitoring system would use. For each of the nine carriers with 20 or more flights a day on average, which between them carry 96.8% of the flights, the alert compares the day's late share with the carrier's mean over the previous 28 days and fires when the excess reaches the 95th percentile of the excesses that carrier had from January to June. The prediction of what comes next uses only January to June: for each horizon from one to seven days, a regression with an intercept, pooled over all nine carriers and every January-to-June day, of the late share that many days later, minus the day's baseline, on the day's excess. The slope for the next day is 0.386, and the slopes for two to seven days are between 0.005 and 0.108. The intercepts add between 1.3 and 1.8 percentage points at every horizon. Under that model, a little under 40% of an alert-day excess is still there the next day and almost none of it a week later.

From July to December the rule fired 63 times across the nine carriers, on only 17 distinct days: 41 of the alerts fell in December, when storms delayed every carrier at once, and up to nine carriers alerted on the same day. On an alert day the carrier's late share averaged 0.709, against a baseline of 0.240. The next day it averaged 0.415. The model, fitted on the first half of the year, predicted 0.434. A response team that intervened on every one of those days, and judged its work by the next day's number, would have recorded an improvement of 29 percentage points; the prediction says 28 of them would have happened anyway.

![Figure 4](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/regression-to-the-mean/part4/figures/fig4_alert_path.png)

*Figure 4. The late share of a carrier from two days before to seven days after an alert, averaged over the 63 alerts from July to December 2013 (orange), the prediction from a model fitted on January to June (blue), the share for all flights on the same days (grey) and the carrier's trailing 28-day mean before the alert (dashed). nycflights13.*

Figure 4 shows the whole window. The late share is already above the baseline two days before the alert (0.298 against 0.240) and rises the day before; the fall is almost complete after two days, and from the third day on the carrier settles above both its baseline and the prediction, at around 0.35. The prediction misses there because bad days came in longer runs in the second half of the year than in the first: the correlation of the all-flights share with its value two days earlier was 0.194 from July to December against 0.048 from January to June, and three days earlier 0.205 against 0.038. Most of the alerts belong to one December, and a reasonable reading of the settled level is that those alerts mark the start of a bad spell rather than a single bad day. A model of the regression is only as good as the period it was fitted on, the lesson the two NHANES cycles taught in Part 3.

The grey line says something else. On the alert days, the share of late flights across all carriers was 0.646, almost as high as the alerted carrier's, and the next day it was 0.386. The correlation between a large carrier's daily late share and the all-flights share is 0.858, or 0.818 when the carrier's own flights are left out of the all-flights share, and 87.3% of the alerts fell on days when the all-flights share was in its own top tenth for the second half of the year. An alert on a carrier's late share is mostly an alert on the weather, or on whatever else delays everyone at once. That changes what the alert should be compared with. Applying the same rule to the carrier's share minus the all-flights share, which asks whether the carrier is doing worse than everyone else that day, produces 58 alerts, of which only 5.2% fall on the system's worst days. The carrier-specific excess on those days is 0.181 above its baseline, and the next day 0.053; the model predicted 0.055. The relative excess regresses faster than the raw share, with a next-day slope of 0.303, because much of what persists in the raw share from one day to the next is the condition every carrier shares.

Persistence rules are the usual defence against alerts that resolve by themselves: alert only when the threshold is crossed on two consecutive days. With the threshold set at the 90th percentile of the excess, any breach produced 134 alerts in the second half of the year, of which 32 were second consecutive breaches and 102 were first breaches. The next day, 42.4% of the excess was still present after a second breach and 28.5% after a first one, but with so few alerts that difference is not established: a bootstrap over the alerts gives an interval from −0.07 to +0.36 for it. Two days later the difference is clearer. The late share was 0.358 after a second breach and 0.226 after a first, a difference of 0.132 with an interval from 0.057 to 0.212. A second breach is evidence that the state is lasting, and the alerts it selects are more likely to be worth a response. The cost is a day of delay and three-quarters of the alerts, and whether that trade is worth making depends on what an unnecessary investigation costs against a late one.

The last pattern concerns many segments of different size, the situation of anyone who monitors a metric by region, product, campaign or customer segment. Splitting the flights by carrier and airport gives 34 series, from a few with one flight a day to three with more than a hundred (JetBlue at JFK, ExpressJet at Newark and United at Newark). The 14 series averaging fewer than ten flights a day carry 7.3% of the flights; the 8 averaging 40 or more carry 66.2%. A fixed threshold on the rate, here a late share of 75% or more, produced 309 alerts from July to December, and 69.6% of them came from the small series. A day with three flights, all late, is an alert. Those small-series alerts had a late share of 0.924 on the alert day and 0.415 the next, and on the days they fired the all-flights share averaged 0.451, bad but not exceptional, against 0.631 and 0.664 on the days the middle and large series alerted. Most of them look like the noise of a few flights.

![Figure 5](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/regression-to-the-mean/part4/figures/fig5_segments.png)

*Figure 5. Left: the share of flights in carrier-airport series with fewer than 10, 10 to 40 and 40 or more flights a day, and the share of alerts from each under three rules, July to December 2013. Right: the late share on the alert day and the next day under each rule, with the all-flights share on the alert day. 34 series from New York. nycflights13.*

The textbook repair accounts for the size of each segment and for the day: compare each series' late share with the all-flights share that day, in units of the binomial standard error for that series' number of flights, and alert at a z-score of 3 or more. From July to December this produced 239 alerts, 50.6% of them from the large series and 20.1% from the small, and on the days they fired the all-flights share averaged between 0.140 and 0.296 across the three size groups. The picture has reversed, but partly for a bad reason. The binomial standard error assumes that a series' flights on a day are independent, and they are not: flights on the same day share crews, aircraft and weather. The standard deviation of the binomial z is 1.20 for the small series, 1.46 for the middle ones and 2.38 for the large ones, against 1 if the model were right, and z of 3 or more occurs on 1.9%, 2.9% and 10.6% of their days, against about 0.1% in theory. For the large series the rule flags ordinary bad days.

A calibrated version divides each series' z by that series' own standard deviation of z, estimated from January to June. It fires on 1.0% of the July-to-December series-days, and its 59 alerts come 47.5% from the small series, 45.8% from the middle ones and 6.8% from the large ones. Which of the two is right depends on the question. The binomial rule asks whether a series did worse than the day by more than sampling noise, and for a large carrier that is often true and rarely unusual; the calibrated rule asks whether the series did worse than it usually does relative to the day. All three rules select on noise to some degree and all three regress: the lag-1 correlation of a series' excess over the day is 0.230 for the small series, 0.297 for the middle ones and 0.432 for the large ones, which is Part 2's result about unit size in its operational form. Small units are noisier, their extremes are more often noise, and they regress more.

A further step follows from the same arithmetic. What a response can affect is the part of the excess that would otherwise persist, and the model gives an estimate of it before anyone acts: the next-day slope times the alert-day excess, or, for a segment, that product shrunk further by the segment's noise. An alert on a large excess in a small, noisy segment and an alert on a moderate excess in a large, stable one can carry the same expected persistent excess. Setting the threshold on that forecast rather than on the day's observation ranks alerts by what a response could change, and it builds the regression into the alert instead of leaving it to be discovered in the evaluation.

An alert system built with this in mind does four things that the default does not. It compares each unit with what everyone else did that day, so that shared shocks are not mistaken for local ones. It scales the threshold by the precision of each unit, estimated from the unit's own history rather than from a binomial formula, so that small units do not dominate the alert list and large ones do not flag their ordinary days. It records, for every alert, the prediction of where the metric would have gone without a response, so that the response can be judged against that and not against the alert-day value. And, where the cost of a missed response allows it, it leaves a random fraction of alerts without a response, which turns the alert log into the control group that the evaluation of the responses needs.

That last practice is cheap to size and sobering in its result. Across the 63 alerts of the second half of the year, the next-day late share varied around its prediction with a standard deviation of 0.210. If the alerts were independent, the 126 or so a year that this rate implies would let a 20% random holdout estimate the response's effect on the next day with a standard error of 0.047. They are not independent. The 63 alerts fell on 17 days, and alerts on the same day share the same weather, so the unit of randomisation should be the alert day: withhold all responses on a random fifth of the alert days. With about 34 alert days a year and a standard deviation of 0.197 for the day-average residual, the standard error is 0.085 for a 20% holdout and 0.068 for a 50% one. The smallest effect such a design would detect reliably, at the usual 5% level with 80% power, is about 2.8 standard errors: 24 percentage points with a fifth held out, 19 with half. A year of these alerts can confirm only a response that changes the next day dramatically. Any smaller effect would need several years of alerts or many metrics pooled, and a team that cannot wait that long should at least stop reading the next-day improvement as evidence, since in these data the prediction alone accounts for nearly all of it.

## Writing the selection down before making it

The three routines share a structure, and so do their repairs. In each, a unit is chosen because a measurement was extreme, and something is then measured again. The repairs all have to be decided before the second measurement is taken, because once it is in, any analysis of it is an analysis of a selected group, and the regression is already mixed into the result.

The first thing to write down is the selection rule itself, exactly: the metric, the threshold or the share selected, the period over which the metric was computed, and whether the units were compared with each other or with an absolute standard. The size of the regression depends on the rule, and an analysis that changes the rule after seeing the data, such as moving a threshold so that the flagged group looks better or worse, changes the regression it will have to account for.

The second is the reliability that matters, which is the correlation between the selection measurement and the outcome measurement for units like the selected ones, over the interval that will separate them. A test archive gives it for test winners. A history of past scorecards gives it for vendors, stores or carriers. A history of past alerts gives it for a monitored metric. The scorecard example above shows why the interval matters: a half-year on-time share has a reliability of 0.905 as a measure of its own half-year and a correlation of 0.671 with the next one, and only the second predicts what the flagged units will do.

The third is the prediction itself, made from those two inputs before the outcome is known: the expected later value of the selected group with no intervention. For a test winner it is the shrunk lead; for a group of worst performers it is the regression of later values on earlier ones in the history; for an alert it is the horizon-specific slope. Writing it down makes the evaluation a comparison of the observed outcome with a number fixed in advance, and it makes the size of the regression visible to whoever reads the result.

The fourth is the design that will separate an effect from the regression if an effect is claimed: a holdout among the selected, a confirmation on fresh data, a threshold that allows a regression-discontinuity comparison, or a random fraction of alerts left alone. Each of them costs something, a little traffic, some units not treated, some alerts not answered, and each can be sized beforehand from the spread of the outcome, as the NSW and alert examples above show. None of them can be added afterwards.

## What the four articles add up to

The series began with Galton's sweet peas and his tables of stature, and with the observation that children of tall parents are on average less tall than their parents, while parents of tall children are on average less tall than their children. The regression runs in both directions of time, it does not narrow the distribution, and its amount is set by the correlation between the two measurements. Those three facts, recomputed from Galton's own data and from Pearson and Lee's, were the first article. Secrist's reading of the same arithmetic as a law of business, the triumph of mediocrity, and Hotelling's reply, were the warning that came with it.

The second article turned the facts into a model. The fall of a selected group is (1 − ρ) times its selected deviation when the measurements are jointly normal, the selected deviation is computable from the share selected, and the model extends to skewed and heavy-tailed populations, to several occasions and to units of different size. It also listed the designs that separate a regression from an effect: a randomised control within the selected, a second baseline, a regression discontinuity, and a predicted regression subtracted from the change.

The third article tested the model on blood-pressure readings and on seven decades of baseball, by predicting the regression of selected groups before looking at it, on data held out from the fit. The predictions were good where the assumptions held and wrong in instructive ways where they did not: when the measurement process changed, when the distribution was skewed, when noise depended on the level.

This article has applied the same arithmetic to the selections data scientists make every week. The winner of a test keeps about the reliability of one arm's estimate of its measured lead. A group selected at its lowest recovers by an amount that depends on the population it was selected from, so the only comparison that recovers by the same amount is drawn from the same pool. An alert selects one day of one series, and most of its excess is gone the next day.

The thesis was stated at the start of the first article: regression to the mean is not a force acting on individuals but a consequence of selecting on a noisy measurement; its size can be computed from the correlation between measurements and the selection rule; and many effects measured after selecting extremes are partly or wholly this. The four articles have shown the first clause in the history, the second in the mathematics and in held-out predictions, and the third in heights, blood pressure, rookie seasons, test winners, training programmes and alerts. In every case the regression was predictable before it happened, and in every case it could be separated from an effect by a design chosen before the data came in. What it cannot be is ignored.

None of this says that interventions on selected units do not work. The NSW programme raised its trainees' 1978 earnings by about $1,800 against a randomised control, an effect that is real and that a before-and-after comparison would have overstated by a factor of 2.7. The winning arms of the simulated tests were, on average, better than the others; they were not as much better as they looked. The alert responses in the flight data were never made, so nothing can be said about them, which is the point: the next-day improvement that would have been credited to them was there without them. Regression to the mean does not make effects disappear. It makes them impossible to read from the outcome of a selected group alone, and it tells the analyst, before any outcome is in, how much of that outcome to expect for free.
