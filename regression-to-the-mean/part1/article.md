# Galton's Heights, Pearson's Fathers and Secrist's Firms: The History of Regression to the Mean, With the Data Recomputed

## Part 1 of 4. How a pattern in sweet peas and human stature became the most frequently misread result in statistics: Galton's tables and notebook, Pearson and Lee's fathers and sons, Secrist's "triumph of mediocrity" and the flight instructors' theory of praise, each recomputed or replicated on public data

Regression to the mean is the only statistical phenomenon with a name that sounds like a law of nature and behaves like an accounting identity. The name suggests a force: something that pulls the tall towards the average, the successful towards the ordinary, the lucky towards their deserts. What it describes is a property of prediction. Whenever two measurements are imperfectly correlated, the expected value of one, given an extreme value of the other, is less extreme. That is all. The amount of regression follows from the correlation and from how far out the conditioning value lies, it runs in both directions of time, and it implies nothing about whether anything is changing.

This series argues that most of what goes wrong with the idea goes wrong in the same way. Somebody selects units because a measurement of them was extreme, measures them again, finds them less extreme, and attributes the change to something: to heredity, to competition, to a treatment, to a policy, to their own praise or blame. The selection on a noisy measurement guarantees the change; the attribution adds nothing to it. The size of the guaranteed change can be computed, and the later articles in this series show cases in which it accounts for a large part of what was measured.

This first article tells the history through the data that made it, following the outline of Stephen Stigler's historical account (Stigler, 1997). It starts with Francis Galton's sweet peas and his family records of human stature, recomputes his 1886 table and the notebook behind it, turns to the fathers and sons that Karl Pearson and Alice Lee measured, and then to two famous misreadings: Horace Secrist's 1933 discovery that business tends towards mediocrity, replicated here on a century of baseball teams, and the flight instructors who concluded that praise makes performance worse. Every number computed for this article is printed by a script in the series repository, linked at the end; the data are downloaded from a pinned version of a public repository and checked against digests.

## Sweet peas, 1875 to 1877

Galton came to regression through plant breeding, not human measurement. In the mid-1870s he had sweet-pea seeds sorted by size and distributed to friends for planting, and in a lecture at the Royal Institution in February 1877 he described the arrangement: "Each set contained seven little packets, and in each packet were ten seeds, precisely of the same weight." Two sets failed, and "the final result was this: that I obtained the more or less complete produce of seven sets, that is, of 7 × 7 × 10, or 490 carefully weighed seeds" (Galton, 1877).

The produce of large seeds was smaller than its parents and the produce of small seeds larger, and Galton saw that the shortfall was proportional. In the same lecture he wrote that "reversion followed the simplest possible law; the proportion being constant between the deviation of the mean weight of the produce generally and the deviation of the parent seed, reckoning in every case from one standard point." He called it reversion then, and regression later.

He also noticed the fact on which the rest of the subject depends, though he did not yet make it central: the produce of every size of parent was scattered just as widely. He wrote of finding "the family variability of the produce of the little seeds to be equal to that of the big ones; but so it was, and I thankfully accept the fact."

Galton printed the seed data as a table in the appendix to his 1886 paper on stature, and again in Natural Inheritance (Galton, 1889), with the sizes as mean diameters in hundredths of an inch and each row scaled to 100 seeds (Galton, 1886, Table VI). Parents of diameter 21 produced seeds averaging 17.5; parents of diameter 15 produced seeds averaging 15.3. A six-unit difference between parents became a 2.2-unit difference between their produce, 0.367 of it. The least-squares slope of Galton's observed means on the parental diameter is 0.343, with a standard error of 0.076 from only seven points; the slope of his smoothed column is 0.318. His own summary was the round number: for each unit of increase in the parent seed, the filial seed showed "a mean increase of only one-third". The point at which the produce would resemble its parents, where the fitted line crosses the line of equality, is 15.39 hundredths of an inch for the observed means and 15.55 for the smoothed ones; Galton put it at about 15.5.

The figures are less settled than the round number suggests. When Karl Pearson recomputed the means from Galton's full table for his biography of Galton, he obtained 17.26 for the largest parents and 15.98 for the smallest, and the slope of his means on the parental diameter is 0.210, not a third (Pearson, 1930). The direction and the proportionality are robust; the coefficient depends on how the table is summarised. That is worth noting because the coefficient was soon treated as a constant of heredity, and it is not even a stable property of one table.

## Stature, 1886

Galton's paper on human stature, published in the Journal of the Anthropological Institute in 1886 and expanding the presidential address he had given to the anthropological section of the British Association at Aberdeen the year before, generalised the seed result and gave it the name it still carries (Galton, 1886). His data were the heights of parents and their adult children, collected from family records that he had solicited from the public. He described them as "the heights of 930 adult children and of their respective parentages, 205 in number"; the table in the paper counts 928. Two choices in the paper shaped everything that followed. Women's heights were converted to a male scale: "The factor I used was 1·08, which is equivalent to adding a little less than one-twelfth to each female height." And the two parents were summarised by their average, "the 'mid-parental' height", which Galton argued was "all we need care to know about them". With those two choices made, the result fits in one sentence: "the height-deviate of the offspring is, on the average, two-thirds of the height-deviate of its mid-parentage."

Galton read the result as a fact about heredity, and the title of the paper, with its "regression towards mediocrity", carried that reading into the vocabulary. The recomputation below shows why the reading does not survive the data.

## The 1886 table, recomputed

The data that Galton used are distributed with the R package `HistData` as `Galton`: 928 adult children and the mid-parent heights of their families, both recorded in the classes Galton used, with daughters' heights already multiplied by 1.08 and the classes centred on non-integer values, which the package documentation explains by "the strong bias toward integral inches" in the reported heights. The script recomputes what Galton computed and a few things he did not.

The mean mid-parent height is 68.31 inches, with a standard deviation of 1.79; the mean child height is 68.09 inches, with a standard deviation of 2.52. The least-squares slope of the children's heights on the mid-parents' is 0.646, with a standard error of 0.041: within rounding, Galton's two-thirds. For every inch by which a mid-parent stands above the mean, the children stand on average about two-thirds of an inch above it. The table of mean child heights by mid-parent class shows the same thing without a model. Children of mid-parents in the 72.5-inch class average 71.88 inches, 3.80 above the mean of all children, against their parents' 4.19 above the mean of all mid-parents; children of mid-parents in the 64.5-inch class average 65.40, 2.69 below the children's mean, against 3.81 below for their parents. Grouping more coarsely, the 134 children of mid-parents at 70.5 inches or more stand 2.09 inches above the children's mean while their parents stood 2.87 above the mid-parents' mean, which is 0.728 of the parental deviation; the 181 children of mid-parents at 66.5 inches or less stand 1.50 inches below, 0.574 of their parents' 2.62.

The same table can be read the other way. Group the children by their own height and ask how tall their mid-parents were. The answer is the regression of mid-parent on child, and its slope is 0.326, with a standard error of 0.021. Children in the 73.2-inch class, 5.11 inches above the mean of all children, had mid-parents averaging 70.71 inches, 2.40 above the mean of mid-parents; children in the 63.2-inch class had mid-parents at 66.62, 1.68 below. Tall children have parents who are less tall than they are, relative to the mean, just as tall parents have children who are less tall. Figure 1 draws both regressions on the same axes: the line of mean children for given mid-parents is flatter than the dashed line along which children would deviate exactly as much as their parents, and the line of mean mid-parents for given children is steeper. Neither generation is regressing towards the other. Each, conditioned on an extreme value of the other, sits closer to its own mean.

![Figure 1](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/regression-to-the-mean/part1/figures/fig1_galton.png)

*Figure 1. Galton's 928 adult children against the mid-heights of their parents (daughters' heights multiplied by 1.08, points jittered within Galton's classes). Circles: mean child height in each mid-parent class. Squares: mean mid-parent height in each child class. Solid lines: the two least-squares regressions. Dashed line: children deviating from their mean exactly as much as their mid-parents deviate from theirs.*

The two slopes are tied together. The product of the two slopes, 0.2105, is the square of the correlation between mid-parent and child height, 0.459 (95 per cent interval 0.406 to 0.508). Each slope is the correlation multiplied by the ratio of the two standard deviations, the child's over the mid-parent's in one direction and the mid-parent's over the child's in the other. Galton's two-thirds is therefore not a coefficient of heredity in any simple sense. It mixes the strength of the association, measured by the correlation, with the ratio of two spreads, and the spread of mid-parents is much smaller than the spread of children: 1.79 inches against 2.52, because a mid-parent is an average of two people and averages vary less than individuals. Measured in standard units, so that the ratio of spreads drops out, the regression in each direction is the same 0.459.

## Is the regression a straight line?

The two groups at the ends of the 1886 table did not regress by the same proportion. Children of the tallest mid-parents kept 0.728 of their parents' deviation and children of the shortest kept 0.574, where a straight line through the middle would give both the same 0.646. A quadratic term added to the regression of child on mid-parent is +0.035 per square inch, with a standard error of 0.016 (t = 2.15, p = 0.031): the line bends upwards at the tall end. Galton's line is a good summary of the middle of the table and a less good one of the edges, which is where regression matters most.

Amanda Wachsmuth, Leland Wilkinson and Gerard Dallal noticed this bend in 2003 and asked whether it might be an artefact of Galton's pooling of fathers and mothers, sons and daughters on one transmuted scale (Wachsmuth, Wilkinson and Dallal, 2003); they answered the question with Pearson and Lee's tables, which `HistData` distributes alongside Galton's. Galton's own family records cannot decide it. Fitted child by child, the quadratic term is +0.014 for sons and +0.028 for daughters, and +0.022 when sons and daughters are pooled as Galton pooled them, with t statistics of 0.65, 1.52 and 1.50 when the standard errors allow for families. The bend is smaller and less precisely estimated in the child-level records whether the sexes are pooled or not, and the table's t of 2.15 uses standard errors that cannot allow for families, since the table does not record them.

The point for the rest of this series is that the linearity of regression is a property of a model, the bivariate normal distribution, and not of data. When two measurements are jointly normal, the expected value of one given the other is exactly a straight line with slope equal to the correlation times the ratio of standard deviations, and the regression at every point is the same fraction of the deviation. Real data need not oblige. The regression can be stronger at one end than the other, it can depend on how groups are pooled, and a single coefficient can misstate it at precisely the extremes where it is used. A data scientist estimating how much a selected group will regress should estimate it where the selection happens, not borrow the slope from the middle.

## Galton's notebook, child by child

The 928 records in Galton's table are grouped data, already transmuted and rounded into classes. In 2004 James Hanley published the family records from Galton's notebook, in a transcription made by Beverly Shipley in 2001, and they are now in `HistData` as `GaltonFamilies` (Hanley, 2004). The notebook lists 963 children; 29 of them have no numerical height, and the transcription in the package has 934 children in 205 families, 481 sons and 453 daughters, with each parent's height recorded separately. The mid-parent in the file is exactly the father's height plus 1.08 times the mother's, divided by two. The ratio of the mean heights of sons and daughters in these records is 1.0800 to four decimals; whether Galton derived his factor from that ratio, the records alone cannot say. Hanley compared the multiplicative adjustment with the alternative of adding a constant to daughters' heights and found that the choice makes little difference: slopes of 0.71 and 0.69, and the same correlation, 0.50, to two decimals.

Working child by child, without the transmutation, the regressions separate by the child's sex. The slope of sons' heights on the mid-parent is 0.713 and of daughters' heights 0.661. On the father alone, the slopes are 0.447 for sons and 0.381 for daughters; on the mother alone, 0.365 and 0.318. Correlations with a single parent run from 0.305 (daughters with mothers) to 0.428 (daughters with fathers). The standard errors need care, because children of the same family share both their parents and much else, and are not independent observations. Allowing for correlation within families raises the standard error of the sons' slope on the father from 0.048 to 0.055, and of the daughters' slope on the mother from 0.047 to 0.074. Galton's data carry less information than their 934 rows suggest. The same correction is routine, or should be, whenever the rows of a data set are nested in larger units, such as customers in households, sessions in users or patients in hospitals: the number of independent pieces of information is closer to the number of clusters than to the number of rows, and a standard error computed as if every row were independent overstates the precision of any regression, including a regression towards the mean.

The single-parent slopes make the point about the spread of the predictor more sharply. The standard deviation of fathers' heights is 2.65 inches, of mothers' heights multiplied by 1.08 is 2.52, and of mid-parents 1.91, about 0.72 of the fathers', because fathers' and mothers' heights are nearly uncorrelated in these families (+0.097) and averaging two nearly independent quantities shrinks the spread by a factor close to the square root of two. A child regresses more strongly towards the mean relative to one parent than relative to the average of both, because the average of both is the better-informed predictor. The degree of regression depends on what is being conditioned on, which is a first hint that it is a property of the prediction and not of the people.

### What families add

Galton's paper says something that the single-parent slopes cannot show: "The child inherits partly from his parents, partly from his ancestry." Children of the same parents resemble each other more than their parents' heights alone would predict. Hanley's second question about the records was exactly this: how large is the familial component beyond the parental heights?

The script answers it with a regression of each child's height on the father's height, the mother's height and the child's sex, and then asks how strongly the residuals of brothers and sisters are correlated. The coefficients are 0.393 for the father, 0.318 for the mother and 5.2 inches for being a son, with a residual standard deviation of 2.16 inches. The intraclass correlation of the residuals within families, from a one-way analysis of variance over 205 families (172 of them with two or more children), is 0.186. About a fifth of what the parents' heights leave unexplained is shared by siblings.

That number changes what "the mean" in regression to the mean refers to. Given only the parents' heights, a child's expected height regresses towards the population mean by the amount the parental correlation implies. Given the heights of the child's siblings as well, it regresses towards a mean specific to the family, which sits away from the population mean in the same direction as the siblings. There is no single mean to which every unit returns. There is an expected value conditional on whatever is known, and each additional piece of information moves it. The second article will make this precise with the formula that Truman Kelley wrote for test scores in the 1920s, in which an observed score is pulled towards a group mean by an amount that depends on the reliability of the measurement (Kelley, 1927), and which is an early form of the shrinkage estimators used in data science today.

## From regression to correlation

Two years later Galton found the way out of the asymmetry. Measuring pairs of bodily dimensions in the same people, the cubit (from elbow to fingertip) and the stature, the length of the head and the stature, the height of the knee and the cubit, he saw that the regression of one on the other and of the other on the one became the same number once each measurement was expressed in units of its own variability. He defined the relation in words that already contain the modern idea: "Two variable organs are said to be co-related when the variation of the one is accompanied on the average by more or less variation of the other, and in the same direction." And he gave the symmetric coefficient its role: "This decimal fraction is consequently the measure of the closeness of the correlation" (Galton, 1888). Pearson put the coefficient on the product-moment footing it has had since.

The correlation dissolves the puzzle of the two slopes in the 1886 table. The regression of children on mid-parents, 0.646, and of mid-parents on children, 0.326, are the same correlation, 0.459, multiplied by opposite ratios of the standard deviations. In standard units both regressions have slope 0.459, and the expected standardised deviation of either generation, given the other, is 0.459 times the given one. The regression is the shortfall of that coefficient from one. Nothing in it refers to heredity, to generations or to time. It would be the same if the heights were measured in the other order, or if the "parents" were a second measurement of the same people taken with a tape measure that slips.

That last remark is the modern reading, and it is worth stating plainly because the history obscured it for decades. Any two measurements that are imperfectly correlated will show regression, whatever the reason for the imperfection: a genuine change between the two occasions, a component of each that the other does not share, or error in measuring either. Heights of parents and children differ for all three reasons. A pilot's two consecutive landings, a firm's profits in two years and a patient's two blood-pressure readings differ for the same reasons in different proportions. The proportion matters for what the regression means, and the second article of this series is about how to tell them apart. But the existence and the size of the regression follow from the correlation alone.

## Pearson and Lee: the spread does not shrink

If tall parents have children who are less tall, and short parents children who are less short, it seems to follow that each generation should be less variable than the last, and that after a few generations everybody would be of middling height. Galton himself saw that this does not happen, and the reason is the key to the whole subject: the children of middling parents are spread out too, some of them well above and some well below, and their spread exactly replaces what the extremes lose. The clearest data on the point are the family records that Karl Pearson's laboratory collected in the 1890s and that Pearson and Alice Lee analysed in their 1903 study of inheritance, distributed in `HistData` as `PearsonLee` (Pearson and Lee, 1903). The package takes four tables from the paper, for fathers and mothers against sons and daughters, as frequency tables in one-inch classes; the frequencies include halves, a feature of the published tables that the documentation does not explain, and the counts below are sums of those frequencies.

For 1,078 pairs of fathers and sons, the fathers in the package's table average 67.20 inches with a standard deviation of 2.720, and the sons 68.16 inches with a standard deviation of 2.747. The levels deserve a caution: the package's father–daughter table gives fathers a mean of 67.70 and its mother–son table gives sons 68.64, and other copies of Pearson's data agree with those higher values, so the father–son table's classes appear to be labelled half an inch low. Nothing below depends on the absolute level, since it all concerns deviations from the means, correlations and spreads, and the groups below are described by the share of each generation they contain rather than by height. The correlation is 0.514. The slope of sons on fathers is 0.519 and of fathers on sons 0.509, and both are close to the correlation because the two standard deviations are almost equal. A test of equal variances that accounts for the correlation between the pairs, Pitman's and Morgan's, gives t = 0.38 with a p-value of 0.70: the spread of the sons' generation is indistinguishable from the spread of the fathers'. The other three combinations in the data give similar correlations, between 0.494 and 0.514. In the one other pairing of the same sex, mothers and daughters, the daughters are if anything more variable than their mothers, 2.61 against 2.41 inches (Pitman–Morgan t = 3.53, p = 0.0004): nothing there either suggests a generation narrowing towards mediocrity.

Regression is everywhere in these pairs, in both directions. The 87 tallest fathers, the 8.1 per cent in the table's top classes (a frequency-weighted count), stood 4.91 inches above the fathers' mean; their sons stood 2.66 inches above the sons' mean, 0.542 of the fathers' deviation. The 154 tallest sons, the 14.3 per cent in the same classes of the sons' distribution, stood 4.53 inches above the sons' mean; their fathers stood 2.18 above the fathers' mean, 0.482 of it. Selecting tall sons and looking at their fathers produces the same regression as selecting tall fathers and looking at their sons. Since fathers cannot have regressed towards their sons, regression cannot be a process that happens to the second generation. Figure 2 shows both regressions and the two distributions, which overlap almost exactly apart from the sons' inch of extra mean height.

![Figure 2](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/regression-to-the-mean/part1/figures/fig2_pearson_lee.png)

*Figure 2. Pearson and Lee's 1,078 fathers and sons. Left: the joint frequency table (point size increasing with frequency), with the mean height of sons for each father's height and the mean height of fathers for each son's height, shown where at least ten pairs contribute. Right: the distributions of the two generations, with their standard deviations.*

The replenishment can be counted in the same table. Call a father tall if he is in the table's top classes holding 15.3 per cent of fathers, and a son tall if he is in the top classes holding 14.3 per cent of sons, the sons' cut set one class higher to match the difference in mean height between the generations. Of the tall fathers, 39.8 per cent had tall sons. Of the tall sons, 42.7 per cent had tall fathers, and the other 57.3 per cent, 88 of the 154 tall sons in the frequency-weighted count, had fathers who were not tall. With a stricter threshold, one class higher in each generation and covering about 8 per cent of each, the share of tall sons whose fathers were not tall rises to 70.3 per cent. For comparison, if the two heights were jointly normal with the observed correlation of 0.514, a son of a father in the top fifth would be in the top fifth with probability 0.444, and in the top tenth or twentieth given a father in the same tenth or twentieth with probability 0.333 and 0.252. The further out the threshold, the more of the next generation's extremes come from outside the last generation's.

The reconciliation is the one Galton reached and that every later treatment repeats. Take a son of a tall father. His expected height is closer to the mean than his father's because his father's height is only partly informative about his: the correlation of 0.514 means that most of the variation in sons' heights is not predictable from the fathers'. That unpredictable part is as large for sons of short and middling fathers as for sons of tall ones, and it scatters every group of sons around its expected value. Most of the tallest sons in the next generation come from fathers who were not the tallest, lifted by a large unpredictable component. The extremes are replenished from the middle by the same scatter that pulls the extremes' children back. The distribution of heights stays the same while every conditional mean regresses.

## Secrist and the triumph of mediocrity

In 1933 Horace Secrist, a professor of statistics at Northwestern University, published *The Triumph of Mediocrity in Business* (Secrist, 1933). Secrist's study was large. Its subject was the expense and profit ratios of firms in many lines of business through the 1920s, and its method was to sort the firms into groups on a ratio in the first year and follow each group's average over the following years. Jordan Ellenberg's account of the book, which describes it as "a 468-page volume, thickly marbled with tables and graphs", gives one example: 120 clothing stores ranked by the ratio of sales to expenses in 1916 and divided into six groups of twenty, of which the top group "had lost most of their advantage over the typical shop" by 1922 (Ellenberg, 2014). In every industry the groups converged, and Secrist drew the conclusion his title announced. In the words Ellenberg quotes from him: "Mediocrity tends to prevail in the conduct of competitive business."

Harold Hotelling's review in the Journal of the American Statistical Association the same year was polite about the labour and fatal to the conclusion (Hotelling, 1933). As quoted by Ellenberg, Hotelling granted that the work of compilation "must have been gigantic" and wrote that the diagrams "prove nothing more than that the ratios in question have a tendency to wander about". That result, he went on, was "mathematically obvious from general considerations, and does not need the vast accumulation of data adduced to prove it." Secrist protested, and in an exchange of open letters in the same journal in 1934 Hotelling made the point again and more sharply: the thesis of the book, "when correctly interpreted, is essentially trivial", and to prove it by a long numerical study of business ratios "is analogous to proving the multiplication table by arranging elephants in rows and columns, and then doing the same for numerous other kinds of animals" (Secrist et al., 1934). In the same exchange Hotelling spelled out what Secrist's groups should be expected to do with no change in the underlying firms at all: the variance among the group averages in the following year would be smaller than in the first by a ratio whose average value, for a linear regression and fine grouping, is the square of the correlation between the two years.

The script replicates Secrist's design on a data set with a long panel and a clear measure of performance: the season records of major league baseball teams in the Lahman database, 2,706 team seasons in the American and National Leagues from 1901 to 2025. For every base season from 1961 to 2020, it sorts the teams into quarters by winning percentage, keeps the teams that played in each of the five seasons before and after, and follows each quarter's average distance from the league mean. The result reproduces Secrist's finding and Hotelling's objection in one picture, the left panel of Figure 3.

![Figure 3](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/regression-to-the-mean/part1/figures/fig3_secrist.png)

*Figure 3. Left: major league teams sorted into quarters by winning percentage in a base season (every season from 1961 to 2020), with each quarter's mean distance from the league mean in the five seasons before and after. Right: the standard deviation of winning percentage across teams, season by season, 1901 to 2025.*

Forwards in time, the groups converge, exactly as Secrist's firms did. The best quarter stood 85.3 points of winning percentage above the league mean in the base season (that is, 0.0853); one season later it stood 44.4 above, 0.521 of its base-season advantage, and five seasons later 10.0, 0.118 of it. The worst quarter stood 89.0 below; one season later 39.7 below, 0.446 of its deficit, and five seasons later it was 2.5 above the mean. Mediocrity triumphs. But look backwards from the base season and the same groups converge in the other direction. One season before, the best quarter stood only 41.9 points above the mean, 0.492 of its base-season advantage, and the worst 41.1 below, 0.462 of its deficit. Read from left to right, the backward half of the panel shows teams diverging towards excellence and failure, a triumph of extremes. Both halves come from one and the same set of seasons. The convergence and the divergence are produced by choosing the season in which the groups are formed.

Hotelling's ratio can be checked directly. The variance of the four quarter averages one season after the base season is 0.242 of their variance in the base season. The square of the season-to-season correlation of a team's winning percentage is 0.270. With four coarse groups rather than fine ones, the two need not agree exactly, but they are close: the shrinkage of Secrist's group averages is, to a first approximation, the squared correlation and nothing else. A book of several hundred pages documented a number that can be computed from one correlation coefficient.

The question Secrist wanted to answer, whether the differences between firms were narrowing, has a direct answer that does not involve forming groups at all: the spread across firms in each year. The right panel of Figure 3 plots the standard deviation of winning percentage across teams in each season. It did fall over the century, from an average of 0.099 in 1901 to 1930 to 0.071 in 1961 to 1990, and it has stayed at that level since, averaging 0.073 in 1991 to 2025; the season-to-season correlation of a team's winning percentage since 1961 is 0.520, and the standard deviation is 0.0728 in the first season of each pair and 0.0719 in the second. Some of the spread is luck that any season of 162 games contains. If every game were a coin toss, winning percentages would still vary across teams with a standard deviation of about 0.040, and after removing that component the spread of team strength fell from about 0.090 in the first three decades to about 0.060 since 1961. In the 60-game season of 2020, the coin-toss component alone was 0.065 and the observed spread 0.091, which is why that season stands out in the panel. A real narrowing of differences between teams, if one happened, appears as a falling spread. The convergence of groups formed on a single season appears whether or not one happened.

The same calculation says how much regression to expect from luck alone. From 1961 to 2025, leaving out the short 2020 season, the share of the variance of winning percentage across teams that is not accounted for by coin-toss luck averages 0.674. If team strength were fixed from one season to the next, a team's record would therefore be expected to keep about two-thirds of its distance from the mean in the following season, and to lose about a third to the luck in the first season not recurring. The observed season-to-season correlation is 0.520, so teams keep only about half. The difference is consistent with real changes in strength between seasons, as players age, leave and arrive, though this calculation alone cannot say how much of it each of those accounts for. Secrist's convergence, in other words, has two components here, one of which would occur with no change in any team at all.

The groups themselves show the replenishment that Pearson and Lee's fathers and sons showed. Of the 468 team-seasons in a season's top quarter from 1961 to 2024, 47.2 per cent were in the top quarter again the following season, and 52.7 per cent of the next season's top quarter came from the other three. The spread of winning percentage inside the top quarter was 0.027 in the season the group was formed, because the group was defined by a narrow band of high values, and 0.057 one season later, approaching the spread of the whole league. A group formed on an extreme measurement is homogeneous on that measurement by construction. Measured again, it spreads out, its mean moves back, and its place at the top is taken by others whose own good season is just beginning. Nothing in that sequence requires competition to erode anybody's advantage, though competition may also be doing so.

## The fallacy that does not die

Secrist's error did not end with Secrist. In 1992 Milton Friedman published a short note in the Journal of Economic Literature under the title "Do old fallacies ever die?", pointing out that the same reasoning had reappeared in studies of convergence in national incomes (Friedman, 1992). The question there is whether poorer countries or regions grow faster than richer ones and so converge, and a standard way of answering it was to regress growth over a period on income at the start. A negative coefficient was read as convergence. But income at the start of a period contains transitory components that do not persist to the end, so countries that start unusually high will on average grow less, whether or not the distribution of incomes is narrowing. Danny Quah developed a related critique in a paper titled "Galton's fallacy and tests of the convergence hypothesis" (Quah, 1993), and the growth literature, following Robert Barro and Xavier Sala-i-Martin, distinguishes the regression coefficient, which it calls beta-convergence, from the shrinking of the cross-sectional spread, which it calls sigma-convergence (Barro and Sala-i-Martin, 1992), in the same way that Hotelling distinguished the convergence of Secrist's groups from a fall in the variance across firms.

The persistence has an explanation that is more interesting than carelessness. A regression of a later measurement on an earlier one answers a well-posed question, how much of an initial difference is expected to persist, and its coefficient is a useful number. The fallacy lies in reading the coefficient as a description of what happens to the distribution, or as the effect of something that happened in between. Both readings are natural, because the coefficient looks like a rate of change. Neither follows from it.

## Why every least-squares fit is called a regression

The word outlived its meaning. Galton used "regression" for the specific fact that offspring deviate less than their parents, and the line through the conditional means was, for him, the line of regression of children on parents. Pearson took the coefficient and the line and generalised them, and in 1897 George Udny Yule extended the method to several variables, fitting by least squares (Yule, 1897). From then on the word attached to the procedure, not to the phenomenon. A data scientist who fits a linear regression of revenue on advertising spend is using Galton's word for a situation in which nothing regresses towards anything.

The history has a practical residue all the same. Every least-squares line of y on x has a slope smaller in standardised units than the line of x on y would imply, unless the correlation is perfect, and every prediction from such a line is closer to the mean of y, in standard units, than the value of x it starts from is to the mean of x. In that sense every regression fit does what Galton's did. The phenomenon was never peculiar to heredity: it is built into the least-squares predictor of any imperfectly correlated variable, and it is the reason that well-calibrated predictions of extreme cases look timid. A model that predicted the full deviation for every extreme input would be overconfident on average, and a model that is calibrated will disappoint anyone who reads its predictions for the extremes as forecasts of equally extreme outcomes.

## Praise, punishment and the flight school

The most quoted example of the fallacy in psychology comes from Daniel Kahneman and Amos Tversky, who used it in their 1973 paper on the psychology of prediction as a problem for the reader (Kahneman and Tversky, 1973). In the version circulated as a research bulletin the year before, which presents it as a problem that had actually arisen in the experience of one of the authors, it reads (Kahneman and Tversky, 1972): "The instructors in a flight school adopted a policy of consistent positive reinforcement recommended by psychologists. They verbally reinforced each successful execution of a flight maneuver. After some experience with this training approach, the instructors claimed that, contrary to psychological doctrine, high praise for good execution of complex maneuvers typically results in a decrement of performance on the next try. What should the psychologist say in response?"

Their answer is the shortest correct account of regression in the literature: "Regression is inevitable in flight maneuvers because performance is not perfectly reliable and progress between successive maneuvers is slow. Hence, pilots who did exceptionally well on one trial are likely to deteriorate on the next, regardless of the instructors' reaction to the initial success." And then the observation that makes the example matter outside flight schools: "We normally reinforce others when their behavior is good and punish them when their behavior is bad. By regression alone, therefore, they are most likely to improve after being punished and most likely to deteriorate after being rewarded. Consequently, we are exposed to a lifetime schedule in which we are most often rewarded for punishing others, and punished for rewarding."

The bulletin calls it "This true story", and Kahneman later told it at greater length, as something that had happened while he was teaching instructors in the Israeli Air Force, in his Nobel autobiography and again in Thinking, Fast and Slow (Kahneman, 2011). The structure of the instructors' inference is the same as Secrist's. Units are selected on an extreme measurement (an excellent or a terrible manoeuvre), something is done to them that depends on the selection (praise or shouting), they are measured again, and the change is attributed to the intervention. The selection guarantees the change, and the intervention, applied only to the extremes, has no comparison group that would separate it from the regression.

The design that would have answered the instructors' question is easy to state. After each exceptionally good manoeuvre, praise the cadet or say nothing, at random; after each exceptionally bad one, criticise or say nothing, at random. Every group selected on an extreme manoeuvre regresses on the next try, praised or not, so the comparison of interest is between the praised and the unpraised good manoeuvres, and between the criticised and the uncriticised bad ones. Both members of each comparison were selected the same way and regress by the same expected amount, and the difference between them is the effect of the feedback. The regression is not removed by this design; it is made identical in the two arms, which is all that is needed. This is the logic of every control group, and it is why a before-and-after comparison of a group selected for its extreme first measurement measures nothing on its own.

## What the history establishes

Read together, the historical cases establish five things, and each of them is computed above rather than asserted.

Regression is symmetric in time. Galton's children regress on their mid-parents with a slope of 0.646 and the mid-parents on the children with a slope of 0.326; Pearson and Lee's sons regress on their fathers and the fathers on their sons by almost the same amount; Secrist's design on baseball teams produces convergence forwards and backwards from the season used to form the groups. A process that runs equally backwards in time cannot be a cause acting forwards.

Regression does not shrink the distribution. The spread of sons' heights equals the spread of fathers', and the spread of team strength in baseball since 1961 has been constant while every group formed on a single season converged. Where a distribution does narrow, as team strength did between the early decades of the century and the 1960s, the narrowing appears in the spread and has to be explained separately.

The amount of regression is a property of the prediction, not of the units. It depends on what is conditioned on: children regress more relative to one parent than to the mid-parent, because the mid-parent carries more information. It depends on how the data are summarised: Galton's sweet peas give a third from his own means and a fifth from Pearson's. And it is fixed by the correlation, which is why Hotelling could call Secrist's thesis trivial.

The extremes of a second measurement are mostly not the extremes of the first. Of Pearson and Lee's tall sons, 57.3 per cent had fathers who were not tall, and 70.3 per cent under a stricter threshold; of the teams in a season's top quarter, 52.7 per cent had not been there the season before. Any ranked list rebuilt from a new measurement, a top-ten list of products, salespeople, branches or models, will turn over in the same way, and much of the turnover is the same arithmetic. A list whose membership persisted completely would be evidence that the measurement is nearly perfectly reliable, which few business measurements are.

And an intervention applied only to extremes cannot be evaluated by remeasuring the extremes. The flight instructors, Secrist and any before-and-after evaluation in medicine, education or business that selects its subjects for an extreme first measurement all compare a group selected for an extreme first measurement with itself at a second measurement. The comparison measures the regression plus the intervention, and without a comparison group selected the same way there is no means of separating them.

The second article of this series turns these observations into a model: what determines the correlation, how measurement error, real change and stable differences between units each contribute to it, how to compute the regression to expect from a given selection, and the designs and estimators that separate it from the effect of an intervention.
