# Simpson's Paradox in Analytics Practice: Ramped A/B Tests, Mix Shifts in KPIs, Conditional Fairness Metrics and Automated Reversal Scans

## Part 4 of 4. Four places where a data scientist meets the paradox in ordinary work, each worked through on public data with the code that does the computation: randomised experiments and their ramp-ups, decomposition of a business metric into mix and rate, fairness metrics computed in aggregate and within strata, and a scanner that searches a data set for reversals and then tries to discredit what it finds

The first three articles in this series dealt with the paradox as an object of study: the classic tables and how much evidence they carry, the causal structures that decide which table answers which question, and real data sets in which the reversal changes a conclusion. This one is about the paradox as a working hazard. A data scientist rarely sets out to find a Simpson reversal. They meet one when an experiment's overall result disagrees with its segments, when a dashboard metric moves while every component of it moves the other way, when a fairness audit reports a disparity that shrinks or grows depending on what it controls for, or when a colleague's automated report lists "paradoxes" found in a table.

Each of these is the same arithmetic in a different setting. A rate for a whole population is a weighted average of rates for its parts, and two such averages can disagree with their parts whenever the weights differ. What changes from one setting to the next is what the weights are, why they differ, and what the analyst is entitled to do about it. The four sections below take one setting each, on public data: a randomised e-mail experiment with 64,000 customers, every flight that left New York in 2013, the COMPAS recidivism data published by ProPublica, and the same flight data searched exhaustively for reversals. Every number is printed by a script in the series repository, linked at the end, and the core functions are short enough to show in full.

## One identity behind all four

Everything below rests on one decomposition. Take two groups, A and B, and a stratifying variable with strata s. Group A's overall rate is the sum over strata of its share of members in stratum s times its rate there, and the same holds for B. The difference between the overall rates splits exactly into two parts. The mix part is the sum, over strata, of the difference between A's and B's shares, multiplied by the average of the two groups' rates in that stratum. The within part is the sum of the difference between A's and B's rates, multiplied by the average of the two groups' shares. A reversal happens when the two parts have opposite signs and the mix part is larger.

The four settings differ in which part the analyst wants and why. In a randomised experiment, the design forces the mix part to be close to zero, and a reversal is a sign that the design has been broken. In a business metric, both parts are of interest, and the decomposition is the answer rather than a correction. In a fairness audit, which part is legitimate is the whole question. In an automated search, a reversal is a candidate whose mix and within parts need standard errors before either means anything.

## A/B tests: what randomisation guarantees and what it does not

### The guarantee

Randomisation is usually described as balancing confounders between the arms of an experiment. For Simpson's paradox it does something more specific. Write the aggregate difference between treatment and control as a sum over strata of any pre-treatment variable. It splits, by the decomposition above, into a within part, the stratum differences averaged with the average of the two arms' stratum shares, and a mix part, which is driven by the difference between the treatment arm's stratum shares and the control arm's. Randomisation with a fixed allocation makes the two arms' stratum shares equal in expectation, so the mix part is zero in expectation and small in any large experiment. The aggregate difference is then, up to chance imbalance, a weighted average of the stratum differences, and a weighted average cannot lie outside the range of the numbers it averages. A full Simpson reversal between the arms of a properly randomised experiment, over a variable measured before treatment, can only come from chance imbalance, and in a large experiment the imbalance is tiny.

The data for this section are a real experiment that has become a standard teaching data set. In 2008 Kevin Hillstrom published, on his MineThatData blog, the records of an e-mail test as a data-mining challenge (Hillstrom, 2008). In his description, the data set "contains 64,000 customers who last purchased within twelve months"; one third "were randomly chosen to receive an e-mail campaign featuring Mens merchandise", one third an e-mail featuring women's merchandise, and one third no e-mail, and "During a period of two weeks following the e-mail campaign, results were tracked." The script reads the original file from a public copy and confirms the published structure: 21,307 customers received the men's e-mail, 21,387 the women's e-mail and 21,306 no e-mail. A sample-ratio test against equal thirds gives a chi-square statistic of 0.203 and a p-value of 0.904, and chi-square tests of independence between the arm and each of six pre-treatment covariates (the band of dollars spent in the past year, whether the customer's zip code is urban, suburban or rural, the channels the customer bought from, whether the customer was new in the past twelve months, and whether the customer had bought men's or women's merchandise in the past year) give p-values between 0.358 and 0.934. Nothing suggests a broken randomisation.

The men's e-mail raised the share of customers who visited the website within two weeks from 10.62 to 18.28 per cent, a difference of +7.66 points with a 95 per cent interval from +7.00 to +8.32; the women's e-mail raised it to 15.14 per cent, +4.52 points (+3.89 to +5.16). Purchases rose from 0.57 per cent to 1.25 and 0.88 per cent, differences of +0.68 (+0.50 to +0.86) and +0.31 (+0.15 to +0.47).

The identity is easy to check. For the men's e-mail against no e-mail, the script computes the difference in visit rates within the strata of each covariate and averages them with weights equal to each stratum's share of the two arms combined. By spending band, the stratum differences range from +6.15 to +9.90 points, and their weighted average is +7.644, against an aggregate of +7.659. The largest gap between the aggregate and such a weighted average, over all six covariates, is 0.026 points. The left panel of Figure 1 shows the stratum differences scattered around the aggregate, as a weighted average's components must be.

### Segment results are heterogeneity, not paradox

The right panel of Figure 1 compares the two e-mails with each other. In aggregate the men's e-mail produced 3.14 more visits per hundred customers than the women's (+2.43 to +3.84). Among customers who had bought men's merchandise in the past year, the advantage was +5.98 points (+5.03 to +6.93); among those who had not, it was −0.34 points (−1.40 to +0.72), a small and uncertain advantage for the women's e-mail. The difference between the two subgroup effects, +6.32 points, has a z statistic of 8.72. The pattern runs the other way by past purchases of women's merchandise: +5.81 points for customers who had not bought it and +0.94 (−0.06 to +1.95) for those who had.

A subgroup in which the treatment that wins overall loses, or ties, is not Simpson's paradox. The aggregate here lies between the subgroup effects, as the identity requires, and the subgroup results describe heterogeneity of the effect, the kind of thing that uplift modelling and targeting exist to exploit: a retailer would send the men's e-mail to customers who buy men's merchandise and might send either to the others. What the subgroup results need is the usual discipline of subgroup analysis. These two interactions are very strong and are what one would expect a priori, which is why they are credible. With six binary or categorical covariates and three pairs of arms, an analyst who looks for subgroups in which the ranking changes will find some by chance, and the credible ones are those that were specified in advance, are large relative to their standard errors, and replicate.

### When allocation varies: ramp-ups

The guarantee rests on a fixed allocation. Online experiments routinely break it. A new feature is often exposed first to a small share of traffic, to catch bugs and crashes before many users see them, and then ramped up to an even split. If the analysis pools the days of the ramp, the treatment arm is drawn mostly from the days of the larger share and the control arm from all days, and the two arms' stratum shares, with day as the stratum, are no longer equal. The mix part of the decomposition is no longer zero, and it is driven by whatever differs between the days.

Thomas Crook, Brian Frasca, Ron Kohavi and Roger Longbotham gave the standard example in their 2009 list of pitfalls in online experiments (Crook et al., 2009). Their first table reports a test in which the treatment received 1 per cent of the traffic on a Friday and half of it on the Saturday, with a million customers each day. On Friday the control converted 20,000 of 990,000 visitors, 2.02 per cent, and the treatment 230 of 10,000, 2.30 per cent; on Saturday the control converted 5,000 of 500,000, 1.00 per cent, and the treatment 6,000 of 500,000, 1.20 per cent. The treatment was better on both days. Over the two days the control converted 25,000 of 1,490,000 visitors, 1.68 per cent, and the treatment 6,230 of 510,000, 1.22 per cent (the paper prints 1.20, a slip that does not affect the reversal). The script decomposes the total difference of −0.46 points into a mix part of −0.68, which comes from the treatment's visitors being concentrated on the worse day, and a within part of +0.23. The authors' explanation is the one this series has given throughout: "The reason this seems unintuitive is that we are dealing with weighted averages, and the impact of Saturday, which was a day with an overall worse conversion rate, impacted the Treatment more." Their remedy is blunt: "The simplest solution, which we use, is to throw away the data from the ramp-up period, which is usually short relative to the experiment." Compared within each day and combined by Mantel–Haenszel weights, the same counts give a treatment effect of +0.203 points with a standard error of 0.021. Ron Kohavi, Diane Tang and Ya Xu return to the problem in their textbook on online experiments, in a section on Simpson's paradox and in a chapter on ramping exposure (Kohavi, Tang and Xu, 2020).

The size of the problem can be written down. With two days of equal traffic, a treatment share a on the first and one half on the second, the pooled treatment-control difference is a weighted average of the two daily effects plus a mix part, and the mix part is exactly the change in the average conversion rate from day 1 to day 2 multiplied by a factor that depends only on the allocation: the share of the treatment arm drawn from day 1 minus the share of the control arm drawn from day 1. The script computes the multiplier: −0.645 for a ramp at 1 per cent, −0.564 at 5 per cent, −0.476 at 10 per cent, −0.330 at 20 per cent, and zero at an even split. A ramp at 1 per cent therefore transfers about two-thirds of any change in the baseline between the days into the estimated effect, with the opposite sign. In the published example the average conversion rate fell by 1.06 points from Friday to Saturday, and −0.645 times 1.06 is the mix part of −0.68 points found above. If the baseline falls from day 1 to day 2 by more than about one and a half times the daily effect, the pooled comparison reverses.

The re-enactment in the script makes this concrete with Hillstrom's real outcomes. It sets up a test of the men's e-mail (treatment) against the women's e-mail (control), run over two days of 10,700 customers each. On day 1 the treatment gets 1 per cent of traffic and the visitors are returning customers; on day 2 the split is even and the visitors are new customers, as they might be after an acquisition campaign. The customers are drawn without replacement from the real arms of the real experiment, so every outcome is a real customer's response; only the schedule is constructed. The rates are known from the full experiment: returning customers visited at 21.16 per cent with the men's e-mail and 17.39 with the women's, new customers at 15.41 and 12.92. The men's e-mail is better on both days, by 3.77 and 2.49 points. The average baseline falls by 5.10 points from day 1 to day 2. With the multiplier of −0.645, the mix part is −3.29 points; the two daily effects, averaged with the weights the pooling gives them, contribute +2.93; and the expected pooled comparison is 15.53 per cent against 15.89, a difference of −0.36 points.

One draw of the re-enactment gives 5,457 treated customers and 15,943 controls. Day by day, the men's e-mail is ahead by 7.84 points on day 1, with an interval from −0.42 to +16.11 that reflects only 107 treated customers, and by 1.94 points on day 2 (+0.63 to +3.26). Pooled, it is behind by 0.69 points (−1.80 to +0.42, z = −1.22). The Mantel–Haenszel risk difference, which compares the arms within each day and then combines the days with weights proportional to the product of the two arms' daily sizes over their sum, gives +2.17 points (+0.87 to +3.47, z = 3.26); its standard error uses the variance estimator of Sato, which remains valid when strata are few and large as well as when they are many and small (Greenland and Robins, 1985; Sato, 1989).

```python
def mh_difference(sa, na, sb, nb):
    """Mantel-Haenszel risk difference a - b with the Sato variance; weights na * nb / (na + nb)."""
    pa, pb = sa / na, sb / nb
    w = na * nb / (na + nb)
    d = np.sum(w * (pa - pb)) / w.sum()
    p_num = np.sum((na ** 2 * sb - nb ** 2 * sa + na * nb * (nb - na) / 2) / (na + nb) ** 2)
    q_num = np.sum((sa * (nb - sb) + sb * (na - sa)) / (2 * (na + nb)))
    var = (d * p_num + q_num) / w.sum() ** 2
    return d, math.sqrt(var)
```

Over 2,000 draws, the pooled difference averages −0.36 points, matching the calculation, and lies between −1.09 and +0.36 in 95 per cent of them. It is negative in 83.7 per cent of draws and never significantly positive; in 2.1 per cent it is significantly negative. The Mantel–Haenszel difference averages +2.55 points, with 95 per cent of draws between +1.61 and +3.48, and is significantly positive in 99.4 per cent of them. Figure 2 shows both distributions. A test that would have found the better e-mail almost every time if analysed by day finds a tie or a small loss if pooled.

![Figure 2](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/simpsons-paradox/part4/figures/fig2_ramp.png)

*Figure 2. Left: the share of a change in baseline between two days that leaks into a pooled treatment-control comparison, as a function of the treatment's share of traffic on day 1, with an even split on day 2 and equal traffic. Right: 2,000 re-enactments of a two-day ramp on Hillstrom's real outcomes, men's against women's e-mail, analysed by pooling the days and by the Mantel–Haenszel risk difference by day.*

The variation across draws understates what a real test would show, because the draws come from a finite set of customers and the day-1 control arm uses almost all of them; the point of the repetition is the systematic gap between the two analyses, not the width of either distribution. Note also what the Mantel–Haenszel estimate is: a weighted average of the two daily effects in which day 2 carries almost all the weight, because day 1 has only 107 treated customers. It estimates something close to the effect among new customers, +2.49 points, and not the effect in the full experiment, +3.14. Stratifying by period removes the bias from the mix; it does not turn a ramp into a representative sample of all the traffic the feature will eventually see.

Three practical defences follow. The first is to analyse only the period with a stable allocation, discarding the ramp, at the cost of some data. The second is to stratify by period, as above, when the ramp data are worth keeping. The third is the sample-ratio check that the Hillstrom data passed so easily. The pooled re-enactment has 5,457 treated customers and 15,943 controls; tested against the 50/50 split that the experiment was eventually configured for, the chi-square statistic is 5,138. Aleksander Fabijan and co-authors, in a taxonomy of the causes of such mismatches, describe a sample ratio mismatch as "the situation when the observed sample ratio in the experiment is different from the expected" and treat it as one of the most useful indicators of data-quality problems in an experiment (Fabijan et al., 2019). A sample-ratio mismatch is the symptom of exactly the problem that produces a Simpson reversal in an experiment: the arms no longer have the same composition, along at least one dimension, and the dimension is often time.

Ramps are the textbook case, but any mechanism that makes the allocation vary with something related to the outcome does the same: different allocation on different platforms, an eligibility rule applied after randomisation, a bucketing change partway through a test, or a holdout group defined differently from the treatment group. In each, the first diagnostic is the comparison of the arms' composition along every variable that can be measured before treatment, which is what the balance tests above did for Hillstrom's data.

## Decomposing a metric: mix against rates

Most reversals that a data scientist meets are not in experiments. They are in dashboards, where a rate is reported for a whole business and for its segments, and where the two can move in different directions because the segments change size. The standard tool is the identity set out at the start of this article, the decomposition that the first article applied to every classic table. In demography it is called the Kitagawa decomposition, after Evelyn Kitagawa's 1955 paper (Kitagawa, 1955); related forms appear in economics as shift-share analysis, and in analytics it usually goes by mix and rate, or mix and performance. The whole of it fits in a few lines.

```python
def kitagawa(pa, wa, pb, wb):
    """Split the difference between two weighted averages, sum(wa * pa) - sum(wb * pb), into a mix part
    (different weights at average rates) and a within part (different rates at average weights)."""
    mix = np.sum((wa - wb) * (pa + pb) / 2)
    within = np.sum((pa - pb) * (wa + wb) / 2)
    return mix, within
```

The two parts add up exactly to the difference, because the cross terms cancel when both are evaluated at the average of the two groups. Other conventions evaluate the mix part at one group's rates and the rate part at the other group's weights, and then the split depends on which group is the reference, the same asymmetry that the second article met with natural direct and indirect effects. The symmetric version is the conventional default when neither group is a natural baseline.

The data for this section are every flight that departed the three New York airports in 2013, as published in the `nycflights13` package: 336,776 scheduled departures, of which 327,346 have an arrival delay recorded; the other 9,430 were cancelled or diverted and are left out (Wickham, 2021). The US Bureau of Transportation Statistics counts a flight as on time "if it operated less than 15 minutes later than the scheduled time shown in the carriers' Computerized Reservations Systems", with arrival performance measured at the gate (Bureau of Transportation Statistics, 2021), and the script uses the same rule: an arrival delay of less than 15 minutes. By that definition 75.53 per cent of the recorded flights arrived on time.

### A monthly KPI

Suppose the on-time rate is a monthly KPI on a dashboard. Over 2013 it ranged from 66.45 per cent in December to 86.28 per cent in September. The first question to ask of any movement in such a number is whether it reflects a change in performance or a change in the mix of what is being measured. The script answers it in two ways.

The first is standardisation: recompute each month's rate with each month's own rates in each cell of carrier, origin airport and departure-hour band (before 9, 9 to 12, 12 to 15, 15 to 18, and 18 onwards), but with the cells weighted by their share of the whole year's flights. If the monthly movements came from the mix, from more evening flights in summer or more flights by a late-running carrier, the standardised series would be flat where the raw series moves. It is not. The largest difference between the raw and the standardised rate in any month is 0.42 points, in March, and the two series in the left panel of Figure 3 are almost indistinguishable.

The second is the decomposition of each month-to-month change. From November to December the on-time rate fell by 15.44 points; the mix of cells contributed +0.11 and the rates within cells −15.58, with +0.02 from cells that appear in only one of the two months. From August to September it rose by 10.88 points, of which +0.04 was mix. Over the eleven month-to-month changes the absolute mix parts add up to 1.18 points and the absolute within parts to 60.15.

![Figure 3](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/simpsons-paradox/part4/figures/fig3_monthly_kpi.png)

*Figure 3. Left: share of flights from New York arriving less than 15 minutes late, by month of 2013, as reported and standardised to the year's mix of carrier, origin airport and departure-hour band. Right: each month-to-month change split into the part from different rates within cells and the part from a different mix of cells.*

A decomposition that finds nothing is a result, and a useful one. It says that the monthly swings in punctuality at New York in 2013 came from conditions that affected flights in every cell, of which weather and congestion are the obvious candidates, and not from changes in which carriers flew when. It also says that, for this KPI and these cells, a Simpson reversal between months is very unlikely, because the mix term that would have to outweigh the rate term is two orders of magnitude smaller. That is worth knowing before anyone builds a mix-adjusted version of a dashboard: here it would cost complexity and change nothing. The check takes a few lines and should be run before the adjusted metric is built, not after.

### Comparing carriers

Comparisons between carriers are a different matter, because carriers differ systematically in where and when they fly. The on-time rate was 73.66 per cent at Newark, 76.30 per cent at JFK and 76.86 per cent at LaGuardia, and it fell steadily through the day, from 86.55 per cent for departures before 9 in the morning to 65.13 per cent for departures from 6 in the evening onwards. A carrier that flies mostly from Newark in the evening starts with a handicap that has nothing to do with how it runs its operation.

The script compares every pair of the nine largest carriers, 36 pairs, and splits each difference with the decomposition above, over the cells of origin airport and departure-hour band that both carriers fly. It then attributes the mix part to the two factors. The attribution has a subtlety: the mix part computed over origin alone and the mix part computed over hour alone do not add up to the mix part computed over both, because the factors overlap (a carrier's airports and its schedule are correlated). The Shapley value from cooperative game theory resolves this in the standard way (Shapley, 1953), by averaging each factor's contribution over the two orders in which the factors can be added.

```python
def shapley_mix(mix_of) -> dict[str, float]:
    """Shapley attribution of a mix effect to two factors. mix_of(set of factors) returns the mix part of a
    Kitagawa decomposition over the strata defined by that set (zero for the empty set)."""
    a, b = "origin", "hour_band"
    m_a, m_b, m_ab = mix_of({a}), mix_of({b}), mix_of({a, b})
    return {a: (m_a + (m_ab - m_b)) / 2, b: (m_b + (m_ab - m_a)) / 2, "joint": m_ab}
```

Figure 4 shows eight of the pairs. In four of the 36, the part within cells has the opposite sign to the reported difference: United against Endeavor Air (UA and 9E), JetBlue against Envoy Air (B6 and MQ), Delta against US Airways (DL and US) and American against US Airways (AA and US).

![Figure 4](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/simpsons-paradox/part4/figures/fig4_carrier_gaps.png)

*Figure 4. Differences in the on-time arrival rate between pairs of carriers flying from New York in 2013 (diamonds), split into the part from the mix of origin airports, the part from the mix of departure hours (Shapley attribution) and the part within cells of airport and hour. Carrier codes: UA United, 9E Endeavor Air, B6 JetBlue, MQ Envoy Air, EV ExpressJet, DL Delta, AA American, US US Airways.*

United and Endeavor Air show the pattern most clearly. United's on-time rate was 77.49 per cent and Endeavor's 74.43, a difference of +3.06 points in United's favour. The mix part is +3.59 points, of which +2.41 comes from airports and +1.18 from hours, and the part within cells is −0.53. Endeavor flew 79.5 per cent of its flights from JFK and 56.5 per cent of them at 3 in the afternoon or later; United flew 78.7 per cent from Newark and 41.3 per cent at 3 or later. Endeavor was better than United at Newark (83.6 against 77.4 per cent, on only 1,193 Endeavor flights) and at LaGuardia (78.7 against 78.0), and worse at JFK (72.9 against 77.9), where most of its flights were. Within airport-and-hour cells the two carriers are indistinguishable: the difference standardised to the pooled cell sizes is +0.07 points with a standard error of 1.14. Adding the month to the cells moves the within part from −0.53 to −2.37 points, now clearly in Endeavor's favour. Even the sign of the "performance" comparison depends on how finely the schedule is held fixed.

ExpressJet against Delta is the opposite case. The reported difference is −13.12 points; the mix accounts for −4.48 of it, almost all from airports (ExpressJet flew 81.3 per cent of its flights from Newark), and −8.94 remains within cells, with +0.30 from cells only one of the two carriers flies. Most of that gap is not composition. ExpressJet against Envoy Air is the case where almost all of it is: a reported difference of −4.18 points, a mix part of −4.07 (−4.23 from airports, +0.16 from hours) and a within part of −0.49, with a standardised difference of −0.83 points and a standard error of 0.67.

The decomposition does not say which number is right, and this is where the second article's argument returns in a practical form. Whether origin airport and departure hour should be held fixed depends on the question. A traveller choosing between two flights leaving the same airport at the same time wants the within-cell comparison: the airport and the hour are given, and the question is which carrier gets that flight in on time. An analyst ranking carriers on their whole New York operation may reasonably want the reported difference, because where and when a carrier schedules its flights is a decision the carrier makes, and a schedule concentrated at a congested airport in the evening is part of its performance, not a nuisance to be adjusted away. In the language of the second article, the schedule is a mediator of the carrier's effect on punctuality, not a confounder of it. A regulator comparing carriers for consumers might want both. The decomposition makes each of these answers available and makes the choice between them visible; a single league table hides it.

Three practical points come out of the carrier comparisons. First, report the cells' coverage: the within-cell comparison only uses cells that both carriers fly, and for ExpressJet against Delta those cells cover 94.3 per cent of the two carriers' flights. A within-cell difference computed on a small common support can say little about either carrier's operation as a whole. Second, report the standard error of the within-cell difference, because the comparison within cells often rests on far fewer flights than the headline one; Endeavor's advantage at Newark rests on 1,193 flights. Third, report the decomposition for more than one set of cells, because the within part for United and Endeavor moved from −0.53 to −2.37 points when the month was added. If the sign of a comparison depends on the cells, the comparison is not robust enough to act on.

## Fairness metrics in aggregate and within strata

Fairness audits compare a model's behaviour across groups, and every comparison of rates across groups whose composition differs is exposed to the same arithmetic as the comparisons above. The difference is that in a fairness audit the choice between the aggregate and the stratified comparison is not only a causal question but a normative one, and the two are easy to confuse. This section computes the standard metrics for the best-known data set in the field and shows how each one moves as strata are added; it does not try to settle which metric a system ought to satisfy, which is a question for the people accountable for the system, not for a statistic.

The data are the two-year recidivism file that ProPublica published with its 2016 analysis of COMPAS, a commercial risk-assessment tool used in Broward County, Florida (Angwin et al., 2016; Larson et al., 2016). ProPublica's analysis notebook filters the file before fitting its models: it keeps defendants whose COMPAS-scored charge falls within 30 days of the arrest, on the grounds that otherwise the offence may be the wrong one, and drops records without a recidivism flag, ordinary traffic offences and records without a score. The script applies the same filters and keeps 6,172 of the 7,214 defendants in the file, of whom 3,175 are Black and 2,103 white in ProPublica's classification; the other groups are too small for the stratified comparisons below and are left out. Following ProPublica, a defendant is "labelled higher risk" if the decile score is 5 or more, and the outcome is a new arrest within two years.

### Six metrics and one identity

In aggregate, 52.3 per cent of the Black defendants and 39.1 per cent of the white defendants were rearrested within two years, a difference in base rates of 13.2 points. The tool labelled 57.6 per cent of Black and 33.1 per cent of white defendants higher risk. Among defendants who were not rearrested, the false positive rate, the share labelled higher risk, was 42.3 per cent for Black and 22.0 per cent for white defendants; among those who were rearrested, the false negative rate, the share labelled lower risk, was 28.5 and 49.6 per cent. Among those labelled higher risk, the positive predictive value, the share rearrested, was 65.0 and 59.5 per cent; among those labelled lower risk, the negative predictive value was 64.9 and 71.0 per cent. These are close to, but not the same as, the figures in ProPublica's article, which reported that 44.9 per cent of Black and 23.5 per cent of white defendants were "Labeled Higher Risk, But Didn't Re-Offend", and 28.0 and 47.7 per cent "Labeled Lower Risk, Yet Did Re-Offend". The published rates were computed on the whole two-year population of 7,214 defendants, without the filters used for the models, and the script reproduces them there exactly: 44.85 and 23.45 per cent, 27.99 and 47.72 per cent. The choice of which rows enter an audit moves its headline numbers by two points, which is worth remembering before comparing any audit with another.

These numbers are not independent. Alexandra Chouldechova showed that, within any group, the false positive rate is determined by the base rate p, the positive predictive value and the false negative rate (Chouldechova, 2017):

```
FPR = p / (1 - p) × (1 - PPV) / PPV × (1 - FNR)
```

The script checks it for both groups: 0.4234 on both sides for Black defendants and 0.2201 for white defendants. In Chouldechova's words, "if an instrument satisfies predictive parity—that is, if the PPV is the same across groups—but the prevalence differs between groups, the instrument cannot achieve equal false positive and false negative rates across those groups." Jon Kleinberg, Sendhil Mullainathan and Manish Raghavan proved a result of the same kind for risk scores: calibration within groups and balance of the scores for the positive and for the negative class cannot hold together, except when prediction is perfect or base rates are equal (Kleinberg, Mullainathan and Raghavan, 2017). ProPublica's analysis emphasised the error rates. The response by Northpointe, the tool's developer, emphasised predictive parity, which it described as the property that "the probability of recidivating, given a high risk score, is similar for blacks and whites" (Dieterich, Mendoza and Brennan, 2016). Given the base rates, both observations can be true at once, and the identity is why.

### Calibration within score levels

The left panel of Figure 5 shows the rearrest rate at each decile score for the two groups. The curves are close and cross several times. At decile 1, 23.3 per cent of Black and 21.2 per cent of white defendants were rearrested; at decile 6, 58.8 and 58.1 per cent; at decile 8, 71.4 and 75.0. The gaps are largest at decile 3 (41.9 against 34.5 per cent) and decile 10 (83.7 against 70.0 per cent, on only 50 white defendants). Standardised to the pooled number of defendants at each decile, the rearrest rate is 2.75 points higher for Black defendants at the same score, with an interval from +0.04 to +5.46: a small difference at the edge of what the data can detect.

![Figure 5](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/simpsons-paradox/part4/figures/fig5_fairness.png)

*Figure 5. Left: share of defendants rearrested within two years at each COMPAS decile score, for Black and white defendants in ProPublica's two-year file, with exact 95 per cent intervals. Right: differences between Black and white defendants in five metrics, in aggregate and standardised within successively finer strata of prior-offence count, age category, sex and charge degree.*

This is where the connection to Simpson's paradox becomes explicit. The aggregate positive predictive value is an average of the rearrest rates at deciles 5 to 10, weighted by how many defendants of each group sit at each of those deciles. Black defendants were concentrated at the higher deciles, where rearrest rates are higher for everybody, so their aggregate positive predictive value is higher partly through the mix. The aggregate false positive rate is a mix in the same sense: it is the share of non-recidivists whose scores fall at 5 or above, and it depends on how the two groups' non-recidivists are distributed over the variables that drive the score. Aggregate fairness metrics are weighted averages over the strata of whatever the score depends on, and they inherit every property of weighted averages that this series has been about.

### What adding strata does

The right panel of Figure 5 follows five differences as the comparison is standardised within finer strata of the variables most strongly related both to the score and to rearrest: the number of prior offences, in three bands (none, one to three, four or more) and then in seven (0, 1, 2, 3, 4 to 5, 6 to 9, 10 or more); the age category used in the file (under 25, 25 to 45, over 45); and finally sex and the degree of the current charge.

The base-rate difference falls from 13.2 points in aggregate to 7.8 within prior-count bands, 4.0 within prior-count bands by age, 2.9 with the finer prior bands and 2.1 with all four variables, where the interval, −0.8 to +5.0, includes zero. The difference in the share labelled higher risk falls from 24.5 points to 18.3, 11.4, 9.7 and 9.9. The false positive rate difference falls from 20.3 points to 16.7 within prior bands, 11.1 within prior bands by age, 10.3 with the finer bands and 9.9 with all four variables (interval +6.4 to +13.4). The false negative rate difference shrinks from −21.2 to −9.1. The positive predictive value difference falls from +5.5 to +1.1 within prior bands by age, +0.4 with the finer bands and −1.5 with all four variables, its sign turning in the last step with an interval, −6.4 to +3.5, that includes zero.

Three things are visible in these numbers. The first is that none of the error-rate differences reverses: about half of the aggregate false positive rate difference is composition along prior record and age, and about half remains within the finest strata the data support. The second is that the predictive-value difference, central to Northpointe's response, and the base-rate difference both shrink towards zero as the strata get finer; much of the aggregate difference in those two metrics is mix. The third is the lesson of coarse strata from the SAT data in the previous article, in a new setting. Within the band of four or more prior offences, Black defendants had on average 9.49 priors and white defendants 7.61, and 37.3 per cent of the Black defendants in that band had ten or more against 21.4 per cent of the white defendants. A band that wide leaves composition inside it, and the move from three prior bands to seven takes out a further part of each difference. Whether the finest stratification here is fine enough is not something the data can certify; the coverage of the comparison also falls as strata multiply, to 81.4 per cent of the non-recidivists for the false positive rate with all four variables, because strata with fewer than ten defendants of either group are dropped.

### Which strata are legitimate

A stratified fairness metric is the fairness-audit version of the adjusted comparison, and the second article's question applies to it unchanged: is the stratifying variable a confounder of the comparison, a mediator through which the group difference operates, or something else? The answer here is contested, and it is contested for reasons that are not statistical. Prior arrests are a legitimate predictor of future arrests in the narrow sense that they predict them. They are also, potentially, a record of how intensively different groups were policed, in which case conditioning on them conditions on a mediator of the group difference, and a within-strata comparison would understate a disparity that operates through the record itself, much as the Berkeley department-level comparison would understate discrimination if applicants were steered towards departments. The outcome, a new arrest, has the same problem, since it measures arrest and not offending.

The literature has made the connection explicit. Sandra Wachter, Brent Mittelstadt and Chris Russell, writing about European non-discrimination law, proposed "conditional demographic disparity" (CDD) "as a standard baseline statistical measurement": a difference in the shares of a group among rejected and accepted cases, computed within strata of a conditioning attribute and then averaged (Wachter, Mittelstadt and Russell, 2021). Amazon's documentation for the SageMaker Clarify toolkit, which implements the metric, gives the reason directly: a CDD metric "that conditions DD on attributes that define a strata of subgroups on the dataset is needed to rule out Simpson's paradox" (Amazon Web Services, 2026). The stratified differences in Figure 5 are versions of the same idea, and so they inherit the same question: which attributes to condition on. Causal approaches to fairness go further and require the analyst to state which paths from the protected attribute to the decision are acceptable, so that a metric can condition on some variables and not others (Kilbertus et al., 2017; Kusner et al., 2017). What these approaches share is the recognition that the choice of strata in a fairness metric is not a technical default. It is the decision about what the audit is for, and it should be made, written down and defended before the numbers are computed, not chosen afterwards from among the stratifications that give the preferred answer.

For a practitioner, the computations above suggest a minimal standard for reporting. Report the aggregate metric and the base rates together, since the identity ties them. Report stratified versions over a stated, pre-specified set of variables, with the coverage of each stratified comparison and its interval. Report how the result moves as strata are refined, because a difference that halves when two coarse bands are refined is telling you that the bands are too coarse. And state, for each stratifier, the causal role it is assumed to play, because that assumption, not the arithmetic, is what decides which of the numbers answers the audit's question.

## Searching a data set for reversals

The carrier comparison above was chosen by hand. A data scientist asked whether a data set contains reversals can instead search for them: take every pair of levels of a grouping variable, every candidate stratifier, and compare the aggregate with the stratified difference. Tools that do this exist in the data-mining literature, and it is easy to write one. The point of writing it carefully is not the search itself, which is a loop, but the three things the search needs in order to mean anything: a denominator, an evidence filter and a replication.

The scanner in the script takes a binary outcome, a grouping variable and a dictionary of stratifiers. For every pair of groups and every stratifier, it keeps the strata in which both groups have at least 30 records, computes the aggregate difference over those strata and the difference standardised to the pooled stratum sizes, each with a standard error, and flags the comparison if the two have opposite signs. It also flags the textbook form, every stratum one way and the aggregate the other.

```python
def scan(y, entity, strata: dict[str, np.ndarray], min_n: int = 30):
    found = []
    levels = sorted(set(entity))
    for name, s in strata.items():
        cnt = counts_by(y, entity, s)
        for a, b in itertools.combinations(levels, 2):
            common = sorted(k for (e, k) in cnt if e == a and (b, k) in cnt
                            and cnt[(a, k)][1] >= min_n and cnt[(b, k)][1] >= min_n)
            if len(common) < 2:
                continue
            sa, na = (np.array([cnt[(a, k)][i] for k in common], float) for i in (0, 1))
            sb, nb = (np.array([cnt[(b, k)][i] for k in common], float) for i in (0, 1))
            agg, agg_se = difference(sa.sum(), na.sum(), sb.sum(), nb.sum())
            std, std_se = stratified_difference(sa, na, sb, nb)
            d = sa / na - sb / nb
            full = (np.all(d > 0) and agg < 0) or (np.all(d < 0) and agg > 0)
            found.append(dict(stratifier=name, a=a, b=b, strata=len(common), n=int(na.sum() + nb.sum()),
                              agg=agg, agg_se=agg_se, std=std, std_se=std_se,
                              flip=bool(np.sign(agg) != np.sign(std)), full=bool(full),
                              counts=(sa, na, sb, nb)))
    return found
```

Run over the flight data, with carriers, origin airports and months as the groups and origin, departure-hour band, month, quarter, destination, distance band, day of the week and carrier as stratifiers (each grouping variable is left out of its own stratifiers, and months are not stratified by quarter), the scanner makes 964 comparisons. In 21 of them, 2.2 per cent, the aggregate and the standardised differences have opposite signs. One is a full reversal in the textbook sense: ExpressJet against Mesa Airlines (EV and YV) by departure-hour band, where ExpressJet's rate is 1.52 points higher over the three bands both carriers fly, while it is lower in each of the three, by 7.36 points on the standardised scale. The comparison rests on 514 Mesa flights in the three bands, against 27,631 ExpressJet flights, and the standardised side of it carries a z statistic of −2.60 while the aggregate side carries +0.72.

The first thing the denominator does is to put the count in proportion. Twenty-one reversals sounds like a finding; 21 out of 964 comparisons, most of them between pairs whose aggregate difference is close to zero, sounds like what it is. The left panel of Figure 6 plots every comparison, the aggregate difference on the horizontal axis and the standardised one on the vertical. Almost all of them lie close to the diagonal, where adjustment changes little. The reversals sit in the two off-diagonal quadrants, and the right panel, which enlarges the region around zero, shows that nearly all of them are close to the origin. A reversal is easiest when there is little to reverse.

![Figure 6](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/simpsons-paradox/part4/figures/fig6_scanner.png)

*Figure 6. Every comparison made by the scan of the New York flight data: the aggregate difference in on-time rates between two carriers, airports or months, against the difference standardised over a stratifier. Orange: the two have opposite signs. Circled: both are more than 1.96 standard errors from zero. Right: the region around zero, enlarged.*

The evidence filter keeps the reversals in which both the aggregate and the standardised difference are more than 1.96 standard errors from zero. Three of the 21 survive. Endeavor Air against United by origin airport: −3.06 points in aggregate, with a z statistic of −8.18, and +2.75 points standardised over the three airports, z = +3.85. JFK against LaGuardia by departure-hour band: −0.56 points in aggregate (z = −3.02) and +0.76 standardised (z = +4.13). JFK against LaGuardia by carrier: −1.45 points in aggregate (z = −7.61) and +0.56 standardised (z = +2.34). A Bayesian version of the same filter, which draws every stratum rate from its posterior under a uniform prior and counts how often the aggregate and the standardised differences have opposite signs, gives posterior probabilities of 1.000, 0.999 and 0.992 for these three, and no other comparison reaches 0.95. The next one, JetBlue against Southwest by hour, stands at 0.907.

The filter is necessary but not sufficient, because a scan that makes 964 comparisons will produce some extreme ones by chance, and because the standard errors assume that flights are independent, which they are not: flights on the same day share the same weather. The third step is a replication. The script splits the year into odd-numbered and even-numbered days, runs the scan on the odd days, and checks each reversal found there on the even days. The odd days give 931 comparisons and 17 reversals, of which 7 reverse again, in the same direction, on the even days. The two that passed the evidence filter on the odd days, Endeavor against United by origin and JFK against LaGuardia by carrier, both replicate, although the second only weakly: its standardised difference is +0.92 points on the odd days and +0.19 on the even ones. Splitting by day is a crude way of respecting the dependence in the data; a split by week, or a block bootstrap over days, would be stricter.

What do the survivors mean? The Endeavor reversal was examined in the previous section, and it does not survive a finer stratification: standardised over airport and departure hour together, the difference between the two carriers is +0.07 points in United's favour, with a standard error of 1.14. Standardised over airports alone, the comparison weights the cells by the pooled number of flights, and the pooled flights are dominated by United's flights from Newark, the airport where Endeavor flew only 1,193 times. The reversal is real in the sense that the arithmetic is right and the sampling evidence is strong, and it is fragile in the sense that it depends on which stratifier is used and which standard population the stratified comparison is referred to. The JFK reversal is more plausible as a description: JFK's flights were less often on time than LaGuardia's overall, and slightly more often on time within carriers and within departure hours, a pattern consistent, as a conjecture the script does not test further, with JFK carrying a large share of the flights of carriers whose on-time rates were below average: JetBlue, on time 73.05 per cent of the time, flew 77.1 per cent of its flights from JFK, and Endeavor, at 74.43 per cent, 79.5 per cent. But a reversal of half a point to three-quarters of a point, which the even days reproduce only weakly, is not something to put in a report as a finding about the airports.

The same scanner run on Hillstrom's experiment, with the three arms as groups and the six pre-treatment covariates as stratifiers, makes 18 comparisons for visits and 18 for purchases, and finds no reversal in either. The largest difference between an aggregate and a standardised comparison is 0.027 points. That is the randomisation identity again, seen from the other side: in a well-randomised experiment, a scanner that finds reversals between arms over pre-treatment covariates has found a bug in the randomisation or in the data pipeline, not a paradox.

A scanner like this one is a useful tool if its output is read as a list of candidates, each carrying its denominator, its standard errors, its stability across stratifiers and its replication. Read as a list of discoveries, it is a machine for producing the multiple-comparison problem in a new form. The literature on automated detection is consistent with this view: Alipourfard, Fennell and Lerman (2018), searching Stack Exchange data, reported their candidates together with the number of pairs examined, and the counts in the previous article showed how quickly chance reversals accumulate when the strata are small.

## A working procedure

The four settings suggest a short procedure for the moment a data scientist meets an aggregate that disagrees with its parts. It is written here as prose rather than as a checklist, because each step depends on the answer to the one before.

Start by writing the disagreement as a decomposition. Name the groups being compared, the stratifying variable, each group's distribution over the strata and each group's rate within them. Compute the mix part and the within part, with the cells' coverage and with standard errors. This turns an argument about which number is right into two numbers whose sizes can be compared, and it often ends the argument: in the monthly on-time rate the mix part was too small to matter, and in ExpressJet's gap to Delta the within part dominated.

Then ask why the distributions differ. If the groups were randomised, a material mix part is a defect in the experiment, and the sample-ratio test and covariate balance checks are where to look; the remedy is in the analysis plan (analyse the stable period, or stratify by period), not in a choice between tables. If the groups were not randomised, the distributions differ for reasons that belong to the world, and the next question is the second article's: is the stratifying variable a cause of the group difference, a consequence of it, or a filter on who is observed? A carrier's schedule is part of what the carrier does. A defendant's prior record may be partly a consequence of how the defendant's group was policed. The answer decides which part of the decomposition corresponds to the question being asked, and it has to be argued from knowledge of how the data came to exist, since the data cannot settle it.

Then measure the evidence for each part separately. The aggregate difference, the within-stratum differences and their standardised combination have different standard errors, and in most of the reversals in this article the within-stratum evidence was much weaker than the aggregate evidence: the day-1 effect in the ramp rested on 107 treated customers, Endeavor Air's advantage at Newark on 1,193 flights, the textbook reversal between ExpressJet and Mesa on 514 Mesa flights. A reversal whose stratified side is indistinguishable from zero is an arithmetic event.

Then test the stability of the conclusion. Refine the strata and see whether the within part moves; it moved from −0.53 to −2.37 points for United and Endeavor when month was added, and the false positive rate gap in the COMPAS data fell from 11.1 to 10.3 points when three prior-offence bands became seven. Change the standard population and see whether the sign survives. Split the data by time and see whether the pattern replicates. A conclusion that changes sign under any of these is not a conclusion.

Finally, report the decomposition, not only the number that won. The people who use an analysis are better served by "ExpressJet was 13.1 points less punctual than Delta; about a third of that is where and when it flew, and the rest is within the same airports and hours" than by either half alone. The decomposition is short, it is exact, and it lets the reader apply their own answer to the causal question if they disagree with the analyst's.

## What the four parts add up to

The first article in this series found that the classic examples of the paradox carry less evidence than their reputation, and that the decomposition into allocation and within-stratum parts explains every one of them. The second argued that the choice between the aggregate and the stratified table is a causal question, settled by the structure behind the data. The third showed reversals in real data, some decisive and some no more than noise. This one has shown the same arithmetic in four routine tasks of applied data science, and the conclusion is the same in each: a disagreement between an aggregate and its parts is information about the structure of the data, to be decomposed, attributed to a cause, weighed for evidence and tested for stability, and the decision about which number to report is a statement about the question being asked. A weighted average is the most common summary in analytics, and every weighted average carries the possibility of a reversal. The data scientist's job is to know which weights are in it and why.
