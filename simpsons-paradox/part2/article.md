# Choosing Between the Aggregate and the Stratified Table: Causal Structure, Adjustment and the Limits of Data in Simpson's Paradox

## Part 2 of 4. The same counts read under confounding, mediation and collision give opposite answers. This article enumerates every causal structure a three-variable table allows, shows which ones the data can rule out, and works through adjustment, direct and indirect effects, collider bias, the Berkeley admissions and Lord's paradox

The first article in this series ended with a question it did not answer. Every classic example of Simpson's paradox decomposes the same way: the aggregate difference between two groups is a difference in how they are distributed across strata plus a difference in how they fare within strata. That decomposition is arithmetic, and it tells an analyst nothing about which of the two comparisons, the aggregate one or the stratified one, answers the question at hand. Edward Simpson showed this in 1951 with a single set of eight numbers that described a pack of cards in one paragraph and a medical treatment in the next. For the cards, the combined table gave the sensible answer; for the patients, the separate tables did.

This article is about how to decide. The short version is the one Judea Pearl, Madelyn Glymour and Nicholas Jewell give in the first chapter of their primer on causal inference: "there is no statistical method that can determine the causal story from the data alone" (Pearl, Glymour and Jewell, 2016). The longer version is more useful, because it says exactly how much the data can contribute, where the causal story has to come from, and what goes wrong in each direction when the story is wrong. It is worked through below on a single table, the kidney-stone comparison of the first article, read under every causal structure it admits, and then on three problems where the choice has consequences: collider bias, the Berkeley admissions as a question about direct discrimination, and Lord's paradox about weight gain in a university dining hall. Every number computed below is printed by a script in the series repository, linked at the end.

## One table, read twice

Pearl and his co-authors open their primer with a table that readers of the first article will recognise. A new drug is given to some patients and not to others. Among men, 81 of 87 patients who took it recovered and 234 of 270 who did not; among women, 192 of 263 and 55 of 80. Combined, 273 of 350 recovered with the drug and 289 of 350 without. These are the kidney-stone counts of Charig and colleagues, relabelled: the drug is better for men and better for women, and worse overall. The primer's answer is unambiguous. Gender is "a common cause of both drug taking and failure to recover," so "we should consult the segregated data, which shows us unequivocally that the drug is helpful."

Two pages later the same counts reappear with a different label on the strata, and with the drug and no-drug columns swapped, so that the drug now looks better overall and worse in each stratum. Instead of gender, the table now splits patients by their blood pressure measured after treatment, and the drug is supposed to work partly by lowering it. The table to read reverses: "it makes no sense to separate the results based on blood pressure," and the aggregate table is the right one, which again favours the drug. The authors draw the conclusion explicitly: "Remarkably, though the numbers are the same in the gender and blood pressure examples, the correct result lies in the segregated data for the former and the aggregate data for the latter."

That is Simpson's paradox stated as a problem in causal inference, and it removes most of the mystery. The paradox is not that the numbers behave strangely. They behave as weighted averages always behave. The paradox is that a reader expects the numbers to say which comparison to make, and they cannot. The rest of this article shows why they cannot, how far they can go, and what has to be added.

## Twenty-five graphs and one table

The question can be put precisely. A table of three binary variables, treatment X, stratum Z and outcome Y, is a joint distribution over eight cells. A causal structure among three variables can be drawn as a directed acyclic graph, in which an arrow from one variable to another means that the first is a direct cause of the second. There are 25 such graphs on three labelled variables, from the empty graph to the six complete ones, in which every pair is joined. Each graph implies some conditional independences: in the graph with arrows from Z to X and from Z to Y and no arrow between X and Y, for instance, X and Y must be independent given Z. If the data contradict an implied independence, the graph is ruled out. If they do not, the graph remains possible.

For the kidney-stone table, the script runs the six relevant tests. The data reject, with p-values below 10⁻⁶, every graph that makes treatment independent of stone group, with or without conditioning on the outcome, and every graph that makes stone group independent of the outcome. They do not reject the hypothesis that treatment and outcome are independent (a G statistic of 2.315 on one degree of freedom, p = 0.128), nor that they are independent given stone group (3.450 on two degrees of freedom, p = 0.178). Ten of the 25 graphs survive at the 5 per cent level. Six of them are complete and fit the table exactly, as any complete graph fits any distribution over its variables. The other four impose one independence each and fit it to within 0.0105 in every cell, in units of joint probability, about seven patients.

For each surviving graph, the script computes what it implies about the effect of the treatment, the quantity Pearl writes as P(Y | do(X)): the probability of success if every patient were given treatment A, against the probability if every patient were given B. The answers are not close to each other. Six of the ten graphs contain no directed path from treatment to success, so under them the effect is exactly zero. One, in which treatment affects stone group and stone group affects success but treatment has no direct arrow to success, gives a difference of −0.0848, favouring B. Two give −0.0457, the aggregate difference. One gives +0.0537, favouring A. A table that fits all ten structures within sampling error is consistent with a treatment that helps, one that harms and one that does nothing.

Some of that ambiguity is a matter of sample size and would shrink with more patients. Two tests keep the sparse graphs alive. Independence of treatment and outcome within stone groups would be rejected with 80 per cent power at the 5 per cent level if the study had been about 2.80 times as large, some 1,960 patients, and the observed table were the truth; marginal independence of treatment and outcome, which keeps the collider graph without a direct arrow alive, would need about 3.40 times as many, some 2,380. The rest of the ambiguity would not shrink at all, and it is the part that matters for the paradox.

The three complete graphs that respect the obvious time order, with treatment before success, are the three stories of the textbook, and Figure 1 draws them. If stone group affects both the choice of treatment and the chance of success, it is a confounder, and the comparison should be made within stone groups. If treatment affects stone group, which affects success, it is a mediator, and the comparison should be made across the totals. If both treatment and success affect stone group, it is a collider, and again the totals are right. The three graphs fit the 700 patients exactly, with no residual at all, and two of them give one answer and the third the opposite.

![Figure 1](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/simpsons-paradox/part2/figures/fig1_three_structures.png)

*Figure 1. Three causal structures that reproduce the kidney-stone counts exactly. When stone group is a confounder, the effect of treatment is estimated within stone groups (adjusted rates 0.833 and 0.779); when it is a mediator or a collider, it is estimated from the totals (0.780 and 0.826).*

This is the formal content of Pearl and co-authors' remark that no statistical method can determine the causal story from the data. Graphs with the same links and the same colliders between unlinked variables imply exactly the same conditional independences, a result due to Thomas Verma and Pearl (Verma and Pearl, 1990), and the six complete graphs on three variables have the same links and no unlinked pairs at all. No test of independence can separate them, however large the sample. What separates them is information that is not in the table: which variable came first, which could physically influence which, how the data were collected.

For the kidney stones, that information is available and decisive. Stone size was a property of the patient at presentation, before any treatment was chosen; neither the treatment nor its success could have changed it. That rules out every graph with an arrow into stone group from treatment or outcome, leaving only the confounder story among the complete graphs. Under that story, the comparison is the stratified one, and open surgery is better by 5.4 points, with the caveats about period and ureteric stones that the first article raised. The data did not decide this; the calendar did.

Simpson's own two stories fit the same scheme. In his pack of cards, colour and rank are fixed when the pack is printed, and the dirt comes later, from a baby who, in his table, happened to handle more red cards and more court cards. Colour and rank both point into dirt, which makes dirt a collider, the reading Pearl gives in a footnote to his 2014 commentary, and conditioning on a collider manufactures an association between its causes: within the dirty cards and within the clean ones, red and plain appear associated, while in the whole pack, which is the population nobody conditioned on, they are not. The combined table is right because it is the only one that does not condition on the collider. In his clinical story, sex is fixed before treatment and affects both who is treated and who survives, which makes it a confounder, and the separate tables are right because they block the back-door path through sex. Simpson did not have the graphs, but his judgement about which table was "sensible" is exactly what the graphs deliver.

The collider reading of the kidney-stone table is less natural, and that is informative too. It would require the stone group to be recorded after treatment and to depend on both the treatment and its success: a classification made from post-operative imaging, for instance, in which a failed treatment leaves more residual fragments to be counted as multiple stones. Under that story, stratifying by the recorded group would compare treatments among patients selected partly by their outcome, and the totals would be the right comparison. The point is not that the story is plausible for Charig and colleagues' data, where it is not, but that the same counts would carry it without complaint. Whether a stratifying variable was measured before or after the outcome could have touched it is the first thing to establish about any reversal.

## The adjustment formula

When the stratum variable is a confounder, Pearl's back-door criterion says which variables to adjust for and how. A set of variables is a valid adjustment set if it blocks every path from treatment to outcome that starts with an arrow into the treatment, and contains no descendant of the treatment. For three variables with Z a confounder, Z itself is such a set, and the effect is given by the adjustment formula:

```
P(Y = 1 | do(X = x))  =  Σ_z  P(Y = 1 | X = x, Z = z) · P(Z = z)
```

The formula averages the stratum-specific success rates of a treatment over the distribution of the strata in the whole population, not over the distribution among the patients who happened to get that treatment. That is exactly the standardisation of the first article. For the kidney stones, 49.0 per cent of all 700 patients had large or multiple stones. Weighting by that common mix gives 0.8325 for open surgery and 0.7789 for PCNL, a difference of +0.0537, against the unadjusted 0.7800 and 0.8257. (The primer, applying the formula to rounded rates, reports a difference of 0.0502; with the exact counts it is 0.0537.)

The same answer can be reached by weighting the patients instead of the strata. Among small-stone patients, the probability of receiving open surgery was 0.2437; among large-stone patients, 0.7668. Weighting each patient by the inverse of the probability of the treatment actually received, given stone group, builds a pseudo-population in which treatment is independent of stone group, and the weighted success rates are again 0.8325 and 0.7789. The two estimators coincide here because the adjustment set is a single discrete variable and the propensity is estimated within its cells; with continuous covariates and models they differ, and that difference is the subject of a large literature. What they share is the causal assumption: Z blocks every back-door path and is not itself affected by the treatment.

Pearl's resolution of the paradox, in a 2014 commentary, rests on a result he calls a "sure-thing" theorem, proved in his book *Causality* (Pearl, 2009a): an action that increases the probability of an event B in every subpopulation "must also increase the probability of B in the population as a whole, provided that the action does not change the distribution of the subpopulations" (Pearl, 2014). The proof is the law of total probability applied to the interventional distribution. Under intervention, the success rate of each treatment is a weighted average of its stratum-specific rates, and the weights, the population distribution of the strata, are the same for both treatments. If one treatment's rate is higher in every stratum, its weighted average with the same weights is higher too. A reversal in observed data needs the weights to differ between the treatments, which is exactly what the allocation component of the first article measured; an intervention that does not alter the strata removes that difference by construction. Under intervention, reversals cannot happen. If giving open surgery to every patient raises the success rate among small stones and among large stones, and does not change anyone's stone size, it raises the success rate overall. The reversal in the observed table is an artefact of observation: patients were not given treatments at random, and the group that got open surgery was not the group that got PCNL. The theorem explains why the reversal surprises; the back-door criterion above says which table to consult; and together they let Pearl write that, "armed with these criteria, we can safely proclaim Simpson's paradox 'resolved'." The resolution consists of reading the question as a question about interventions. It does not consist of a rule that the stratified table is always right, and the remaining sections are about the cases in which it is not.

## When the structure is right but incomplete

Choosing the confounder story for the kidney stones settles which table to read. It does not settle whether stone group is the only confounder, and the first article already named a second candidate: the period of treatment. Open surgery was used from 1972 to 1980 and PCNL from 1980 to 1985, so anything that changed between those periods, in patients, imaging, anaesthesia or follow-up, is mixed into the comparison and cannot be separated from it, because no patient in either period received the other treatment.

A sensitivity analysis asks how strong such an unmeasured confounder would have to be to change the conclusion. Tyler VanderWeele and Peng Ding's E-value gives a one-number answer: the minimum strength of association, on the risk-ratio scale, that an unmeasured confounder would need with both the treatment and the outcome to explain away an observed risk ratio (VanderWeele and Ding, 2017). The adjusted risk ratio of open surgery against PCNL is 1.0689, with a 95 per cent bootstrap interval from 0.9863 to 1.1631, and its E-value is 1.340. A confounder associated with treatment and with success by risk ratios of 1.34 each could account for the whole adjusted advantage; since the interval already includes one, no confounding at all is needed to make it compatible with no effect.

For the period of treatment the arithmetic is starker. Period determines treatment completely in this study, so no patient can be compared with another treated in the same period, and the formal bounding factor of Ding and VanderWeele (2016) reaches its limit, in which only the confounder's association with the outcome matters. Put directly: if success rates were about 7 per cent higher, in relative terms, in the early 1980s than in the 1970s, for reasons unrelated to the procedure, that period effect alone would equal the adjusted risk ratio of 1.0689 and account for the whole adjusted advantage of open surgery. The causal structure says which table to read; it does not say that the table read is free of the variables nobody recorded.

## When the stratum is a mediator

Suppose the stratum variable is affected by the treatment. In the primer's second story, the drug lowers blood pressure and lower blood pressure aids recovery. Then the stratified comparison holds blood pressure fixed and asks what the drug does by routes other than blood pressure, while the aggregate comparison lets blood pressure respond to the drug as it does in reality and asks what the drug does in total. Both are legitimate questions; they are different questions, and only the second is the one a patient deciding whether to take the drug is asking.

Mediation analysis makes the distinction quantitative. Read the kidney-stone table as if treatment determined stone group, so that treatment A puts 75.1 per cent of patients in the large-stone group and treatment B 22.9 per cent. The total effect of A against B is the aggregate difference, −0.0457. The controlled direct effect, the difference made by treatment with stone group held fixed at a given value, is +0.0644 among small stones and +0.0425 among large ones. The natural direct effect, defined by Pearl in 2001 as the effect of switching treatment while holding the mediator at the value it would have taken under the reference treatment, is +0.0594. It is computable from the table by his mediation formula only under strong assumptions: no unmeasured confounding of treatment and mediator, of treatment and outcome, or of mediator and outcome (Pearl, 2001; see also Robins and Greenland, 1992). The effect of letting the mediator move from its distribution under B to its distribution under A, with the treatment itself held at A, is −0.1051, and the two sum to the total, −0.0457, as Pearl's decomposition of a total effect requires. Pearl's own natural indirect effect holds the treatment at the reference level B instead and is −0.0937; direct and indirect effects defined at different reference levels need not add up, and which pair is reported is a choice that should be stated.

Under this reading the treatment does good by its direct route and harm by its indirect route, and the harm is larger. Stratifying would report only the good. That is not a hypothetical worry. Paul Rosenbaum's paper on "the consequences of adjustment for a concomitant variable that has been affected by the treatment" set out the problem for observational studies in 1984 (Rosenbaum, 1984), and Joshua Angrist and Jörn-Steffen Pischke's textbook of applied econometrics calls such variables "bad controls": "variables that are themselves outcome variables in the notional experiment at hand" (Angrist and Pischke, 2009). In a linear model with a treatment X, a mediator M with coefficients 0.5 from X to M and from M to Y, and a direct coefficient of 0.30 from X to Y, all variables standardised, the script finds a total effect of 0.5500 and a coefficient of 0.3000 on X once M is included in the regression. Both numbers are correct. The second is not the effect of the treatment.

## When the stratum is a collider

A collider is a variable caused by two others. Conditioning on it, by stratifying, restricting the sample or including it as a regressor, makes its causes associated even when they are independent. Joseph Berkson described the most consequential version in 1946, in a paper on the limitations of fourfold tables built from hospital data (Berkson, 1946). Two diseases may be independent in the population, but if having either one makes hospital admission more likely, then among hospitalised patients they will typically appear negatively associated: a patient admitted without the first disease is more likely to have the second, because something brought that patient in.

The script computes a simple case exactly. Two diseases are independent in the population, with prevalences of 10 and 20 per cent. A person with neither is admitted with probability 0.05, with either one alone with probability 0.40, and with both with probability 0.70. In the population the odds ratio between the diseases is 1.000. Among the admitted it is 0.219. An admitted patient with the first disease has the second with probability 0.304; an admitted patient without the first disease has the second with probability 0.667. A study of hospital records would find a strong protective association that does not exist. Admission is the collider, and the hospital is the stratum.

Colliders do not need to be as visible as hospital admission. Sander Greenland, Judea Pearl and James Robins showed that adjusting for a variable measured before the treatment, which the usual advice treats as safe, can itself introduce bias if that variable is a collider of two unobserved causes, one of the treatment and one of the outcome (Greenland, Pearl and Robins, 1999), and Greenland later quantified how large that bias can be (Greenland, 2003). The structure is called an M, after its shape when drawn: an unobserved U1 causes both the treatment X and the covariate Z, an unobserved U2 causes both Z and the outcome Y. Without adjustment, the path X ← U1 → Z ← U2 → Y is blocked at Z, and the unadjusted estimate is unbiased. Adjusting for Z opens it.

How much does that matter? In a linear Gaussian version with a true effect of 0.30 and every variable standardised, the script computes the bias exactly. With each of the four paths through U1 and U2 at 0.3, adjusting for Z biases the estimate by −0.0082. At 0.5, by −0.0667. At 0.7, close to the maximum of 1/√2 that the standardisation allows, the adjusted coefficient is −0.0160, wrong in sign, a bias of −0.3160. The bias grows roughly with the fourth power of the path strength, so it is small unless all four paths are strong, which is the conclusion Greenland and later Peng Ding and Luke Miratrix reached: M-bias "tends to be relatively small compared to confounding bias" (Ding and Miratrix, 2015). The left panel of Figure 2 draws the curve.

![Figure 2](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/simpsons-paradox/part2/figures/fig2_colliders.png)

*Figure 2. Bias in the estimated effect of X on Y (true value 0.30) in linear Gaussian models with standardised variables. Left: the M-structure, as the four paths through the unobserved U1 and U2 strengthen. Right: the size of the bias in the butterfly structure, in which Z is also a direct cause of X and Y, with the U-paths fixed at 0.5; the unadjusted bias is positive and the adjusted bias negative.*

The harder case is the one Ding and Miratrix called the butterfly, in which Z is both a collider of U1 and U2 and a direct cause of X and Y. Now not adjusting leaves the confounding path X ← Z → Y open, and adjusting opens the collider path. With the U-paths at 0.5 and Z's own paths at 0.1, the unadjusted bias is +0.0600 and the adjusted bias −0.0712; at 0.2, +0.1400 and −0.0784; at 0.3, +0.2400 and −0.0896; at 0.4, +0.3600 and −0.1082. Neither choice is unbiased, and which is less biased depends on the relative strength of the two kinds of path, which the data cannot reveal because U1 and U2 are unobserved. In this parameterisation, once Z's own paths exceed about 0.12 (0.117 to three decimals), adjusting is the smaller error in absolute size, which agrees with Ding and Miratrix's general conclusion that "for linear systems, except in some extreme cases, adjusting for all the pretreatment covariates is in fact a reasonable choice." The right panel of Figure 2 plots the size of the two biases and shows where they cross.

Carlos Cinelli, Andrew Forney and Pearl collected these cases and others into an atlas of eighteen small graphs, each labelled as a good, bad or neutral control, and warned specifically against the habit of "conditioning on all pre-treatment predictors of the treatment assignment" without looking at the structure (Cinelli, Forney and Pearl, 2024). The practical lesson is not that adjustment is dangerous but that it is a causal claim. Putting a variable in a regression, stratifying a table by it or restricting a sample to one of its values asserts that the variable sits on a back-door path and not on a forward path or at a collision. The analyst who adds controls because more controls feel safer is making that assertion for every one of them.

## Berkeley as a question about direct effects

The Berkeley admissions of the first article look different through this lens. The question raised by the allegations in 1973 was whether admissions committees discriminated against women: whether, as Bickel, Hammel and O'Connell put it, the decision was "influenced by the sex of the applicant when that is immaterial to the qualifications for entry" (Bickel, Hammel and O'Connell, 1975). In causal terms, sex affects admission through two routes. One runs through the choice of department: men and women applied to different departments, and departments differed in how many applicants they admitted. The other is direct: the effect of sex on admission for an applicant to a given department. The aggregate gap measures the total effect, both routes together; the committees are responsible only for the direct route. Department is a mediator, and for this question, unlike the medical question about the drug, the right comparison is the stratified one.

For the six departments, the script computes the decomposition. The total effect of being a man rather than a woman is +0.1416, the aggregate gap. The controlled direct effects, department by department, are −0.2035 in A, −0.0496 in B, +0.0286 in C, −0.0184 in D, +0.0383 in E and −0.0114 in F. The natural direct effect, averaging the department-level differences over the departments women actually chose, is −0.0011: essentially zero. The remaining part of the gap, due to where the two sexes applied and evaluated at men's department-level rates, is +0.1427, essentially all of it. Both figures assume, as the mediation formula does, that nothing unmeasured affects both the choice of department and admission, and the next paragraphs are about what happens when that fails. With the departments men chose as the reference instead, the natural direct effect of being a woman is +0.0710, because men applied heavily to department A, where women did much better. The left panel of Figure 3 shows the department-level effects against the totals.

![Figure 3](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/simpsons-paradox/part2/figures/fig3_berkeley_direct.png)

*Figure 3. Left: men's admission rate minus women's in each of the six Berkeley departments (circle area proportional to applicants), with the total effect and the natural direct effect computed with each sex's choice of departments as the reference. Right: a stylised model in which sex has no direct effect on admission, but application to the harder of two departments depends on an unmeasured qualification; the apparent within-department advantage of men in each department, along a path on which one sex's slope is zero and the other's increases.*

This is the reading Bickel and his co-authors gave, and it is a reading that requires an assumption they stated themselves. Stratifying by department estimates the direct effect only if nothing else affects both the choice of department and the chance of admission, or, in their words, if men and women applying to a department are equally qualified, an assumption they called "clearly false in its most extensive sense" and chose not to test with measures such as test scores. In causal terms, department is a mediator, and conditioning on a mediator estimates a direct effect only if the mediator and the outcome share no unmeasured cause. If they do, the department becomes a collider between sex and that cause, and stratifying by it creates an association between sex and admission even when the committees ignore sex entirely. William Kruskal raised exactly this objection in an exchange with Bickel published in Fairley and Mosteller's 1977 collection, as later commentators have reported, with state of residence as his example of a factor that could affect both department choice and admission (Fairley and Mosteller, 1977, pp. 128–129). Pearl states the general principle in his overview of causal inference: "by conditioning on the mediator Z, we may create spurious associations between X and Y" (Pearl, 2009b).

The script builds a stylised model to show how large the effect can be. There are two departments, a hard one and an easy one, calibrated so that an applicant drawn from the whole qualification distribution would be admitted with probability 25 and 65 per cent respectively. Sixty per cent of women and 25 per cent of men apply to the hard one. Every applicant has a qualification U, standard normal, that raises the log-odds of admission with a slope of one in either department. Admission depends on department and qualification, never on sex: by construction there is no direct effect of sex. The only freedom is in how qualification affects the choice of department, through a logistic slope that may differ between men and women.

If qualification does not affect the choice for either sex, the within-department rates are equal, 0.250 and 0.650 for both, and the stratified comparison correctly finds no direct effect. If better-qualified men are more likely to try the hard department (slope one for men, zero for women), men in the hard department are better qualified than women there, and the stratified comparison finds men admitted at 0.360 against women's 0.250, an apparent bias against women of 11.0 points in the hard department, alongside 3.9 points in women's favour in the easy one. Reverse the slopes and the apparent bias reverses: women ahead by 5.2 points in the hard department, men ahead by 9.7 in the easy one. With the slopes at +1.5 for men and −0.5 for women, the apparent bias against women in the hard department is 17.7 points. With the same slope of one for both sexes, men are ahead by 5.9 points in both departments, because the men in each department are drawn from a different part of the qualification distribution from the women. The right panel of Figure 3 traces the apparent effects along a path on which one sex's slope is zero and the other's runs from 0 to 1.5: in the hard department from −6.6 to +14.7 points, in the easy one from +12.7 to −5.0, with no discrimination anywhere.

The real departments' direct effects are a few points, apart from department A's twenty. The stylised model shows that sorting on an unmeasured qualification can produce differences of comparable size, in either direction. It does not show that Berkeley's departments did so; nothing in the six-department table can say whether they did. What it shows is that the stratified analysis, which is the right analysis for the question about the committees, answers that question only under an assumption about applicants that the table cannot check. The aggregate analysis answers a different question correctly, and the stratified analysis answers the right question under an assumption. That is the general situation, and Berkeley is a clean instance of it.

## Lord's paradox

In 1967 Frederic Lord described a problem that has been argued about ever since (Lord, 1967). A university wants to know the effect of "the diet provided in the university dining halls and any sex difference in these effects." Each student is weighed on arrival in September and again the following June. Two statisticians analyse the data. The first finds that the mean weight of the girls is the same in June as in September, and so is the mean weight of the boys, and concludes that "there is no evidence of any differential effect on the two sexes." The second, "working independently, decides to do an analysis of covariance," regressing June weight on September weight and sex, and finds that "the boys showed significantly more gain in weight than the girls" of the same initial weight. Lord's conclusion was pessimistic: such a study "is attempting to answer a question that simply cannot be answered in any rigorous way on the basis of available data" (as quoted in Pearl, 2016).

The script simulates the situation in its simplest form. Boys weigh 70 kg on average and girls 56 kg, each with a standard deviation of 8 kg in both September and June, and a correlation of 0.7 between a student's two weighings. Nothing changes between the two dates: each group's June distribution is identical to its September distribution. In the population, the mean gain is exactly zero in both groups, so the first statistician's answer is zero. The covariance-adjusted difference is (1 − 0.7) × 14 = 4.200 kg in favour of boys. With 1,000 simulated students of each sex, the difference in mean gain is −0.045 kg (standard error 0.277) and the covariance-adjusted difference is +4.197 kg (standard error 0.332), with a slope on September weight of 0.693. Both statisticians compute correctly, and their answers differ by four kilograms. Figure 4 shows why.

![Figure 4](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/simpsons-paradox/part2/figures/fig4_lord.png)

*Figure 4. Simulated September and June weights of boys and girls whose distributions do not change over the year. Left: both group means lie on the line of no gain. Right: within each group June weight regresses towards the group mean, so at any given September weight boys are heavier in June than girls; at 63 kg the difference is 4.2 kg.*

The algebra is short. Within each group, June weight equals the group mean plus 0.7 times the student's September deviation from that mean, plus noise. A regression of June weight on September weight and sex therefore finds a common slope of 0.7 and a sex coefficient equal to the difference between the group means multiplied by one minus the slope, (1 − 0.7) × 14 kg. The coefficient is not an estimate of any change over the year; it is regression to the mean, computed separately for two groups whose means differ. Because the two regression lines are parallel, the coefficient is the same at every September weight, and at every September weight it compares a boy and a girl who are atypical of their groups in opposite directions.

A boy and a girl who both weigh 63 kg in September are not comparable students. The boy is light for a boy and the girl heavy for a girl, and each will regress towards the mean of their own group: the boy's expected June weight is 65.10 kg and the girl's 60.90 kg. The second statistician's comparison holds September weight fixed and attributes the 4.2 kg difference to sex. The first statistician's comparison lets September weight be whatever it is for each sex and finds no change.

Pearl's analysis, published in 2016, places the two answers in the framework of this article (Pearl, 2016). Sex affects September weight, which affects June weight, and sex may also affect June weight directly. September weight is a mediator. The first statistician estimated the total effect of sex on the change in weight, which is zero. The second estimated the direct effect of sex on June weight with September weight held fixed, which is not. In Pearl's words, "both statisticians were in fact correct, though each estimated a different effect," and "Lord's pessimistic conclusions were rather premature." For a different version of the problem, introduced by Howard Wainer and Lisa Brown, in which the groups are two dining rooms rather than two sexes and the question is the effect of the dining room, Pearl concluded that "the right answer, therefore, lies with statistician-2," the one who adjusted, disagreeing on that point with Wainer and Brown (Wainer and Brown, 2004; Pearl, 2016). Paul Holland and Donald Rubin had argued in 1983 that neither statistician was correct or incorrect, because each conclusion rests on an untestable assumption about how students' weight would have changed under some other diet (Holland and Rubin, 1983).

Lord's paradox is Simpson's paradox with a continuous stratum. The adjusted and unadjusted comparisons differ because the groups differ in the distribution of the covariate, and which comparison is right depends on whether the covariate is a confounder of the effect in question or a mediator of it. For sex, which is fixed at birth and precedes September weight, the covariate cannot be a confounder; whether to adjust depends on whether the question is about the total or the direct effect. For dining room, chosen by students who already had a September weight, the covariate precedes the treatment and can confound it, and adjustment is called for.

## What the data can and cannot decide

The examples point to a division of labour that applies to any reversal, and to any adjustment decision whether or not a reversal occurs.

The data can rule structures out. In the kidney-stone table, they rejected every graph in which stone group was independent of treatment or of outcome. With more variables, they can do more: a variable that is independent of the treatment but associated with the outcome, for instance, cannot be a confounder. Conditional independence tests, and the causal discovery algorithms built on them, can narrow a large set of possible structures to a smaller one, the equivalence class that Verma and Pearl characterised.

The data cannot choose within the equivalence class, and for a reversal the class in the usual case contains structures that give opposite answers. In the kidney-stone table, all six complete graphs fit exactly, and the ten graphs that survive at the 5 per cent level imply effects ranging from −0.0848 through zero to +0.0537. A larger sample would shrink the set of surviving sparse graphs but would never separate the complete ones.

What chooses within the class is knowledge from outside the table. Time order is the most powerful: a variable measured before the treatment cannot be a mediator or a collider of the treatment and outcome, although, as the M and butterfly structures show, it can still be a collider of unobserved causes. Knowledge of the mechanism is next: whether a drug could plausibly change blood pressure, whether qualifications could plausibly influence the choice of department. Knowledge of how the data were collected is the third: whether the sample was restricted to people who were admitted, hired, hospitalised or followed up, any of which makes the restriction a collider whenever both the treatment and the outcome, or their causes, affect who is included. Miguel Hernán, David Clayton and Niels Keiding put the general point in one line: "analytical errors may occur when the problem is stripped of its causal context and analyzed merely in statistical terms" (Hernán, Clayton and Keiding, 2011).

When outside knowledge does not settle the structure, the honest output is the set of answers the plausible structures imply, as with the identification regions of the missing-data literature. In the kidney-stone case, that set includes the stratified answer, the aggregate answer and zero. In the Berkeley case, it includes a direct effect near zero, if application decisions were unrelated to unmeasured qualifications, and direct effects of several points in either direction if they were not. A report that gives one number without saying which structure it assumes has made a choice the data did not make.

## Questions to ask before adjusting

The examples translate into questions an analyst can put to any variable before stratifying by it, adding it to a regression or restricting a sample on it. They are the same questions for a reversal and for an ordinary adjustment, because a reversal is only an adjustment large enough to change the sign.

Was the variable determined before the treatment, or could the treatment have changed it? If the treatment could have changed it, it is at least partly a mediator, and adjusting for it estimates something other than the total effect. That may be the intended estimand, as with the committees at Berkeley, or a mistake, as with a drug whose benefit runs through the variable.

Could the outcome, or something caused by the outcome, have changed it? If so, it is a collider of treatment and outcome, and adjusting for it, or selecting on it, biases the estimate in a direction that has nothing to do with the effect.

Does it share unmeasured causes with the outcome? If it is a mediator that shares causes with the outcome, adjusting for it to estimate a direct effect opens a collider path, as in the stylised admissions model. If it is a pre-treatment variable that shares unmeasured causes with both treatment and outcome, it may be an M or butterfly covariate; the linear calculations suggest that in most realistic cases adjusting for it remains the smaller error, but that is a judgement about path strengths, not a fact the data supply.

Which question is being asked: the effect of the treatment as it would operate in practice, including its consequences for the variable, or the effect with the variable held fixed? The aggregate and stratified tables answer these two questions, and only the analyst can say which one matters.

## Drawing the graph in practice

None of this requires an analyst to be a philosopher of causation. It requires writing down, before looking at the comparison of interest, what each variable is and when it came into existence, and drawing the arrows that the subject matter makes plausible. In a working data-science setting, that usually means three passes over the variables available.

The first pass is about time. Every variable gets a timestamp relative to the treatment or decision being evaluated: fixed characteristics, measurements taken before the decision, measurements taken after it, and the outcome. Variables recorded after the decision are candidates for mediators and colliders and should not be adjusted for by default, however predictive they are. Variables recorded before it are candidates for confounders, with the M and butterfly caveats.

The second pass is about selection. Any filter that defines the data set, such as customers who logged in, patients who were admitted, applicants who were interviewed or transactions that were flagged for review, is a variable that has been conditioned on. If the filter could have been affected by both the treatment and the outcome, the whole data set is stratified on a collider, and no adjustment within it can undo that. This is the Berkson structure, and in operational data it is common, because operational data are often defined by events that the treatment and the outcome both influence.

The third pass is about the question. For each comparison, the analyst says whether the target is the total effect of the treatment as it operates in practice or an effect with some intermediate variable held fixed, and chooses the adjustment set accordingly. Tools such as the back-door criterion, implemented in software for causal graphs, then turn the drawn graph into an adjustment set, or report that no adjustment set exists among the measured variables, which is itself a finding. What the software cannot do is draw the graph, and the examples in this article show that the graph is where the answer comes from.

## The resolution, and what it leaves

Pearl's claim that the paradox is resolved is correct in the sense he intended. Once the causal structure is stated, the choice between the aggregate and the stratified table is determined, and under intervention a treatment that helps in every stratum, without changing the strata, helps overall. The paradox arises only when a causal question is answered with associational data and no statement of the structure.

What the resolution does not do is supply the structure. In each example in this article, the structure came from somewhere other than the numbers: the timing of a kidney-stone measurement, the biology of blood pressure, the institutional meaning of a department, the birth sex of a student weighed in September. When that knowledge is firm, the paradox disappears. When it is not, the paradox becomes an honest range of answers, and the most useful thing an analyst can do is to say which structure each answer assumes. The next article in this series goes to real data in which that range is wide and the stakes are real.
