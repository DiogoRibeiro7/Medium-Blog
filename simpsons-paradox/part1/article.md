# What the Classic Examples of Simpson's Paradox Actually Show: Yule, Simpson, Berkeley and the Kidney Stones, Recomputed With Their Uncertainty

## Part 1 of 4. The famous tables, from Pearson's skulls in 1899 to the kidney-stone treatments of 1986, recomputed one by one: what each table creates, erases or reverses, and how much evidence each actually carries

Simpson's paradox is usually introduced with a table and a shrug. A treatment is better for men and better for women, and worse for everyone. A university admits women at a higher rate than men in most of its departments and at a much lower rate overall. A baseball player hits for a higher average than another in each of two seasons and a lower average over both. The arithmetic is checked, the reader is told that this is what happens when the groups differ in size, and the lesson is drawn that one should always look inside the aggregate.

That lesson is half right, and the other half is the subject of this series. The arithmetic of a reversal is easy. The question it leaves open, which table answers the question one actually has, is not an arithmetic question at all, and the data cannot settle it by themselves. Before getting to that, though, it is worth looking closely at the examples on which the standard lesson rests, because they are less uniform than the retelling suggests. Some of them do not reverse anything. Some were invented. One of the most cited was not in the paper it is attributed to. And several of the real ones carry much less statistical evidence for the reversal than their fame suggests.

This first article goes through the classic tables in order and recomputes each one: the rates within each group, the rates overall, the formal tests that a modern analyst would run, and a measure that the standard retellings never report, which is how probable it is, given the data, that the reversal holds in the population and not only in the sample. The second article asks which table is right and shows that the answer depends on the causal structure behind the numbers. The third goes to real data sets in which the choice matters. The fourth brings the problem into the daily work of data science, where it appears in A/B tests, metric dashboards and fairness audits. Every number computed below is printed by a script in the series repository, linked at the end, and every number taken from a historical source is attributed to it.

## Mixtures that create an association

The phenomenon is older than its name by more than half a century, and it began not with a reversal but with something that appears from nothing. In 1899 Karl Pearson, Alice Lee and Leslie Bramley-Moore, in a long memoir on the inheritance of fertility, included a proposition "on the spurious correlation produced by forming a mixture of heterogeneous but uncorrelated materials" (Pearson, Lee and Bramley-Moore, 1899). The argument is short. If two measurements are uncorrelated within each of several groups, but the group means differ on both measurements, then pooling the groups produces a correlation that belongs to none of them. Their illustration was Cicely Fawcett's measurements of skulls from the Paris Catacombs. Among 806 male skulls, the correlation between length and breadth was 0.0869; among 340 female skulls it was −0.0424; in the mixed series it was 0.1968, "a value which cannot possibly be considered zero." The authors drew the general warning that "as it is almost impossible to guarantee the absolute homogeneity of any community, our results for correlation are always liable to an error, the amount of which cannot be foretold," and noted that "whether any given mixture increases or reduces the correlation will depend entirely on the signs of the differences of the means of the sub-groups."

George Udny Yule carried the same point from correlations to tables of attributes in 1903, in a paper on the theory of association that discussed "the fallacies that may be caused by the mixing of distinct records" (Yule, 1903). The example he gave there, and repeated in his textbook, is worth recomputing because it is so clean. Suppose an attribute is present in 50 per cent of fathers and 50 per cent of sons, and in 10 per cent of mothers and 10 per cent of daughters, and suppose it is inherited not at all: within each line, whether the child has it is independent of whether the parent has it. Per hundred father–son pairs, the four cells (parent with and child with, parent with and child without, parent without and child with, both without) are 25, 25, 25 and 25. Per hundred mother–daughter pairs they are 1, 9, 9 and 81. Within each line, the probability that the child has the attribute is the same whether or not the parent has it: 0.500 and 0.500 for fathers and sons, 0.100 and 0.100 for mothers and daughters. The cross-product ratio is exactly one in both.

Now mix the two lines, as a careless record-keeper might, into a single table of parents and offspring. The cells become 26, 34, 34 and 106 per two hundred pairs. Among the offspring of parents with the attribute, 43.3 per cent have it; among the offspring of parents without it, 24.3 per cent. The cross-product ratio is 2.384. In Yule's words, "13/30 = 43 per cent. of the offspring of parents with the attribute possess the attribute themselves, but only 17/70 = 24 per cent. of the offspring of parents without the attribute," and the association "is, however, due solely to the association of both with male sex" (Yule, 1927). Mixing the other two lines, fathers with daughters and mothers with sons, produces an association of the opposite sign: 16.7 per cent against 35.7 per cent, a cross-product ratio of 0.360. Mixing all four lines produces no association at all, 30.0 per cent against 30.0 per cent. Yule stated both results: "if records for male-female and female-male lines were mixed, the illusory association would be negative," and "if all four lines were combined there would be no illusory association at all."

Yule's textbook adds a second example, with a hospital, that comes closer to the modern form. One hundred men and one hundred women are admitted; 30 per cent of the men and 60 per cent of the women die, whether treated or not, so the treatment does nothing. But the treatment is given to 80 per cent of the men and 40 per cent of the women. Among men, 24 of the 80 treated and 6 of the 20 untreated die; among women, 24 of the 40 treated and 36 of the 60 untreated. In the combined table, 48 of the 120 treated patients die, 40.0 per cent, and 42 of the 80 untreated, 52.5 per cent. A useless treatment appears to reduce mortality by twelve and a half points, because it was given mostly to the sex that dies less. Yule's phrase for this, "an illusory negative association," is the same structure that would later be called confounding by indication in clinical epidemiology: the people who receive a treatment differ, before treatment, from the people who do not.

Neither of Yule's examples is a reversal. In both, an association that is absent within every group appears in the combined table. That is the most common form of the phenomenon in practice, and it is worth keeping separate from the dramatic form, because the remedies are the same while the alarm is not.

## Simpson's two stories

Edward Hugh Simpson came to statistics by an unusual route. From 1942 to 1945 he worked at Bletchley Park, where from 1943 he led a team in Block B working on the Japanese naval cipher JN-25 (Simpson, 2010; Royal Statistical Society, 2019). After the war he studied statistics at Cambridge, and in 1947 he joined the civil service, where he spent the rest of his career, rising to senior posts in the Department of Education and Science and being appointed CB in 1976. The biographical note to his own 2010 account of Bletchley says that he "ceased being an active statistician in 1947." His one paper on contingency tables, published in 1951, is four pages long and, by his own acknowledgement, grew from a question suggested to him by Maurice Bartlett some years earlier (Simpson, 1951).

The paper's subject is the interpretation of interaction in three-way tables, and its famous example is not a reversal either. Simpson presents a pack of cards in which a baby has been playing, so that some cards are dirty. An investigator classifies the cards as red or black, as court cards or plain, and, "in case it was relevant," as dirty or clean. The table, printed as fractions of 52, has the dirty cards split as 4 red court, 8 red plain, 3 black court and 5 black plain, and the clean cards as 2, 12, 3 and 15. Among the dirty cards, the share of red cards that are plain is 8/12 and the share of black cards 5/8; among the clean cards, 12/14 and 15/18. The cross-product ratio is 1.200 in both. "The investigator deduced a positive association between redness and plainness both among the dirty cards and among the clean." In the combined table, 6 red court and 20 red plain against 6 black court and 20 black plain, the ratio is 1.000. Simpson's verdict is that it is the combined table "which provides what we would call the sensible answer, namely, that there is no such association." Red and plain cannot be associated in a pack of cards, whatever a baby has done to it.

Then he relabels the same numbers. The rows become alive and dead, the columns untreated and treated, and the two layers male and female. Among males, 8 of 13 treated patients survive against 4 of 7 untreated, 0.615 against 0.571; among females, 12 of 27 against 2 of 5, 0.444 against 0.400. The cross-product ratio is again 1.200 in each sex and 1.000 combined, where 20 of 40 treated and 6 of 12 untreated survive, both 0.500. "This time we say that there is a positive association between treatment and survival both among males and among females; but if we combine the tables we again find that there is no association between treatment and survival." And now the separate tables are the sensible ones: "The treatment can hardly be rejected as valueless to the race when it is beneficial when applied to males and to females."

That is the whole point of the paper, and it is the point of this series. The same eight numbers support opposite conclusions about which table to read, depending on the story that produced them. In the cards, the third variable, dirt, was produced by the baby and has nothing to do with the colour or the rank of a card; conditioning on it manufactures an association. In the clinic, the third variable, sex, is a characteristic of patients that affects survival and differs between those treated and those not; conditioning on it removes a distortion. Simpson asked the right question, "What is the 'sensible' interpretation here?", and answered it by appeal to what the variables mean, not to any feature of the numbers. The second article in this series is about how to make that appeal precise.

Simpson's numbers are proportions of a pack, not samples, so the question of statistical evidence does not arise for them in the usual way. If one did treat the eight cells as counts from 52 observations, the Fisher exact test within each sex would give p = 1.000. The association Simpson discusses is a property of the hypothetical population he wrote down, and the paper uses it to make a logical point, not an empirical one.

## A name, and a reversal

The phenomenon got its name in 1972, when Colin Blyth published "On Simpson's paradox and the sure-thing principle" in the *Journal of the American Statistical Association* (Blyth, 1972). Blyth's interest was in decision theory. Leonard Savage's sure-thing principle says, roughly, that if one would prefer action A to action B whatever the value of some unknown condition, one should prefer A to B when the condition is unknown. A reversal of the kind that now carries Simpson's name looks like a counterexample: a treatment better in each group and worse overall. Blyth's resolution, and the discussion it provoked in the same issue among Dennis Lindley, Jack Good, Robert Winkler and John Pratt, turns on what is being chosen and what is held fixed, which is again a question about the structure of the problem and not about the numbers.

Blyth's example, as it is usually reproduced in later expositions, is extreme by design. In one group, a new treatment saves 10 per cent of patients against 5 per cent for the standard one; in another, 95 per cent against 50 per cent. Combined, the new treatment saves 1,095 of 10,100 patients, 10.8 per cent, and the standard one 5,050 of 11,000, 45.9 per cent. The new treatment is better in both groups and worse by 35 points overall, because almost all of its patients are in the group where nothing works well. The script splits the aggregate difference of −0.3507 into an allocation component of −0.5845, the effect of the two treatments being given to different mixtures of patients, and a within-group component of +0.2338, the effect of the treatments themselves at a common mixture. The decomposition is a standard device from demography, due to Evelyn Kitagawa (Kitagawa, 1955), and it will appear again in every example below: whenever the allocation component has the opposite sign to the within-group component and is larger, the aggregate reverses.

## The algebra of a reversal

The decomposition deserves to be written out once, because everything in this series rests on it. Take two groups, indexed by g, compared on a rate across K strata. Let w_gk be the share of group g's cases that fall in stratum k, and r_gk the rate in group g and stratum k. Each group's aggregate rate is the weighted average of its stratum rates, with its own weights:

```
R_g = Σ_k  w_gk · r_gk
```

The difference between the two aggregate rates can then be split exactly into two parts, using the averages of the two groups' weights and of their rates in each stratum:

```
R_1 − R_2  =  Σ_k (w_1k − w_2k) · (r_1k + r_2k)/2   +   Σ_k (w_1k + w_2k)/2 · (r_1k − r_2k)
              └──────────── allocation ──────────┘     └────────── within strata ─────────┘
```

The first term is non-zero whenever the two groups are distributed differently across strata that have different rates; it is the part of the aggregate gap that would remain if the groups had identical rates in every stratum. The second term is a weighted average of the within-stratum differences. If every within-stratum difference has the same sign, the second term has that sign too, and a reversal happens exactly when the first term has the opposite sign and a larger magnitude.

With two strata, the condition has a geometric reading that Figure 2, further on, draws for the kidney stones. Each group's aggregate rate lies on the straight line joining its two stratum rates, at a position fixed by its share of cases in the second stratum. If one group's line lies above the other's everywhere, the aggregate can still favour the lower line, provided the lower group's cases sit far enough towards the stratum with the higher rates and the upper group's towards the stratum with the lower ones. How far is far enough can be computed for any table, and the examples below report it. Nothing in the condition requires small samples, unusual numbers or carelessness. It requires only that the groups compared differ in their mix and that the strata differ in their rates, which is the ordinary state of observational data.

The name stuck. Three years later, Peter Bickel, Eugene Hammel and William O'Connell, analysing the graduate admissions of the University of California, Berkeley, wrote that they had "stumbled onto a paradox, sometimes referred to as Simpson's in this context," citing Blyth (Bickel, Hammel and O'Connell, 1975). Their paper made the phenomenon famous outside statistics.

## Berkeley, 1973

The question Bickel and his co-authors were asked to answer was whether the university's graduate admissions in the autumn of 1973 discriminated against women. The aggregate numbers, in their Table 1, looked damning. Of 8,442 men who applied, 3,738 were admitted, 44.3 per cent; of 4,321 women, 1,494, 34.6 per cent. The script reproduces their chi-square statistic of 110.8 with Yates's correction, 111.2 without, a p-value of about 10⁻²⁵. If both sexes had been admitted at the overall rate, 1,771.3 women would have been admitted rather than 1,494, a shortfall of 277.

Their analysis went department by department. Of 101 departments, 16 had no women applicants or rejected no applicant of either sex, and the authors based most of their computations on the remaining 85. Testing each for a bias large enough to occur by chance less than five times in a hundred, they found "four such departments" biased against women, accounting for a deficit of 26 women, and "six departments biased in the opposite direction," accounting for a deficit of 64 men. They then combined the department-level tests in two ways. Fisher's method of combining probabilities gave a two-sided result that would occur by chance "about 29 times in 1000", but when the question was put one-sidedly, as a test of bias against women, the combined result would occur "about 85 times in 100": there was evidence of departments that departed from equal treatment, and none that the departures ran against women. Pooling the departments with the Mantel–Haenszel procedure, they found 1,493 women admitted against 1,432.9 expected under no bias within departments, a chi-square of 8.55 with a two-sided p of .003: a small but significant bias in favour of women once department was taken into account. The direction of the analysis matters as much as its result. The aggregate table answered a question about the university as a whole, and the stratified analysis a question about the committees that made the decisions, and only the second was the question the university had been asked. Their explanation was in the pattern of applications. "The proportion of women applicants tends to be high in departments that are hard to get into," and the bias in the aggregate "stems not from any pattern of discrimination on the part of admissions committees, which seem quite fair on the whole, but apparently from prior screening at earlier levels of the educational system" (Bickel, Hammel and O'Connell, 1975).

The table of six departments, labelled A to F, that appears in most textbooks and in the R dataset `UCBAdmissions` is not in the Science paper. Its usual source is the introductory text of David Freedman, Robert Pisani and Roger Purves, and it covers the six largest departments, 4,526 applicants in all. It is a convenient table for recomputation because it shows the whole mechanism in miniature, and the script works from it.

In the six departments, 1,198 of 2,691 men were admitted, 44.5 per cent, and 557 of 1,835 women, 30.4 per cent: a gap of 14.2 points, an odds ratio of 1.841 with a 95 per cent interval of 1.621 to 2.091, and a Fisher exact p of about 5 × 10⁻²². Department by department, the picture changes. Department A admitted 62.1 per cent of men and 82.4 per cent of women, a difference of 20.3 points in favour of women with a Fisher p of 0.00002. Department B admitted 63.0 and 68.0 per cent, D 33.1 and 34.9, and F 5.9 and 7.0, all slightly in favour of women and none significant. Departments C and E went the other way, 36.9 against 34.1 and 27.7 against 23.9 per cent, neither significant. Women's rate is higher in four of the six departments, and the only large difference among the six favours women. The left panel of Figure 1 shows them side by side.

The right panel shows why. Departments A and B, which admitted around 63 per cent of their applicants, received 30.7 and 20.8 per cent of the men's applications and 5.9 and 1.4 per cent of the women's. Departments C and E, which admitted 35.1 and 25.2 per cent, received 12.1 and 7.1 per cent of the men's applications and 32.3 and 21.4 per cent of the women's. Across the six departments, the correlation between the excess of women's share over men's share and the department's admission rate is −0.763. Women applied, disproportionately, where admission was hard.

The decomposition puts numbers on this. The aggregate difference of +0.1416 in favour of men splits into an allocation component of +0.1777, which comes entirely from where the two sexes applied, and a within-department component of −0.0360, which favours women. Standardising the comparison to the combined pattern of applications, as if men and women had applied to the six departments in the same proportions, gives a difference of −0.0426 in favour of women, with a 95 per cent bootstrap interval of −0.0777 to −0.0063.

The formal tests tell a more nuanced story than the standard retelling. The Mantel–Haenszel common odds ratio, men against women, is 0.905 with an interval of 0.772 to 1.060, and the Cochran–Mantel–Haenszel test gives p = 0.217: on the odds-ratio scale, the six departments show no significant bias in either direction once department is taken into account. A logistic regression with department effects gives the same odds ratio, 0.905. But the Breslow–Day test of whether the odds ratio is the same in all six departments rejects it decisively, with a chi-square of 18.826 on 5 degrees of freedom and p = 0.002. The departments are not alike, and the reason is department A. Without it, the Mantel–Haenszel odds ratio is 1.031 (0.870 to 1.222), with p = 0.724, and the Breslow–Day test gives p = 0.634. Five of the six departments treated men and women about equally; the sixth admitted women at a much higher rate.

The difference between the standardised difference, which excludes zero, and the Mantel–Haenszel odds ratio, which does not, is a matter of weighting, and it is a small preview of a theme that runs through the series. The standardised difference weights each department by its number of applicants on the scale of percentage points, so department A, large and with a 20-point gap, counts heavily. The Mantel–Haenszel estimator weights departments by a function of their cell counts on the scale of odds, and gives department A, where few women applied, less weight. Both are legitimate summaries of six departments that do not agree with each other. Neither is "the" bias. When the strata are heterogeneous, a single adjusted number answers a question that depends on the weights chosen, and the weights are a decision, not a fact.

Bickel and his co-authors also built a hypothetical table to show the mechanism, with two departments they called "Machismatics" and "Social warfare". The first admits 200 of 400 men and 100 of 200 women, 50 per cent each; the second admits 50 of 150 men and 150 of 450 women, a third each. Neither department discriminates. Combined, men are admitted at 45.5 per cent and women at 38.5 per cent, and the script reproduces the chi-square of 5.71 printed in their paper, with a two-sided p of 0.017, the "less than 2 percent" of their text. A perfectly fair pair of departments can produce a statistically significant aggregate gap, provided the sexes apply to them in different proportions.

Berkeley is often described as a reversal. In the six departments it is not quite one: the aggregate favours men by 14 points, and within departments the average difference favours women by about four, but two of the six departments favour men. The posterior probability, computed below for every example, that in the population every department favours women while the aggregate favours men is 0.010. What the Berkeley data do show, with overwhelming evidence, is that the aggregate gap is not a measure of how the admissions committees treated applicants. The posterior probability that the aggregate favours men is 1.000 to four decimals, and the posterior probability that the standardised difference favours women is 0.984. The aggregate and the standardised comparison point in opposite directions, and the data are decisive about both.

## Stones, 1986

The second example every textbook uses comes from urology. In 1986 Clive Charig, David Webb, Stephen Payne and John Wickham compared three treatments for kidney stones in patients treated consecutively by one team over 14 years: open surgery between 1972 and 1980, percutaneous nephrolithotomy (PCNL), a keyhole procedure, between 1980 and 1985, and extracorporeal shockwave lithotripsy (ESWL) from 1985 (Charig et al., 1986). A treatment counted as successful if the patient was free of stones, or the stones were reduced to fragments under 2 mm, three months later. The paper's Table II gives success by stone size, in two groups: stones of mean diameter under 2 cm, and stones of 2 cm or more or multiple stones. It was this table, restricted to open surgery and PCNL, that Steven Julious and Mark Mullee used in 1994 in a short *BMJ* note on confounding (Julious and Mullee, 1994), and that has been reproduced in statistics teaching ever since.

The numbers, recomputed from Charig and co-authors' Tables I and II: for small stones, open surgery succeeded in 81 of 87 patients, 93.1 per cent, and PCNL in 234 of 270, 86.7 per cent. For large or multiple stones, open surgery succeeded in 192 of 263, 73.0 per cent, and PCNL in 55 of 80, 68.8 per cent. Open surgery is better in both groups. Overall, open surgery succeeded in 273 of 350, 78.0 per cent, and PCNL in 289 of 350, 82.6 per cent. PCNL is better overall. (The online text of Julious and Mullee's note prints the small-stone rate for PCNL as 83 per cent; 234 of 270 is 86.7 per cent, as Charig and co-authors' table has it.)

The mechanism is the same as at Berkeley, and Figure 2 draws it. Of the open-surgery patients, 75.1 per cent had large or multiple stones; of the PCNL patients, 22.9 per cent. Each treatment's overall success rate is a weighted average of its two size-specific rates, so it lies on the straight line joining them, at the position given by its own mix of patients. Open surgery's line lies above PCNL's everywhere, but open surgery's overall rate sits far down its line, near the large-stone end, and PCNL's near the small-stone end. The aggregate difference of −0.0457 splits into an allocation component of −0.0994 and a within-group component of +0.0537; the difference standardised to the combined mix of patients is +0.0537 in favour of open surgery. The geometry also says how robust the reversal is to the mix. Holding every stratum rate fixed, and open surgery's mix as it was, the two overall rates would be equal if 48.4 per cent of PCNL's patients had large or multiple stones; any share below that makes PCNL look better overall. The actual share was 22.9 per cent, well below the threshold, so the aggregate reversal is not a knife-edge accident of the mix.

What the textbook versions do not say is how weak the evidence is. Within small stones, the difference of 6.4 points has a Fisher exact p of 0.127 and an odds ratio of 2.073 with a 95 per cent interval from 0.824 to 6.244. Within large or multiple stones, the difference of 4.3 points has p = 0.479. The Mantel–Haenszel odds ratio combining the two groups is 1.447 (0.916 to 2.286), with p = 0.119. The aggregate difference in favour of PCNL, the one that is supposed to be misleading, has p = 0.154. Neither leg of the famous reversal is statistically significant at the conventional level. The standardised difference has a bootstrap interval of −0.0112 to +0.1197, which includes zero.

A sample-size calculation shows how far the data are from settling the question. If the observed rates were the true ones, a trial with 80 per cent power to detect the small-stone difference at a two-sided 5 per cent level would need 343 patients per arm; for the large-stone difference, 1,790; for the aggregate difference, 1,188. The comparison has 87 and 270 patients in the small-stone group and 263 and 80 in the large one.

There are also two reasons, unrelated to sample size, to be careful about reading either table as a comparison of treatments. The first is time. The two treatments were used in different periods, open surgery in the 1970s and PCNL in the early 1980s, so any change in patients, imaging, anaesthesia or follow-up between those periods is confounded with the treatment. Julious and Mullee's note says as much: "Randomised trials are therefore necessary to demonstrate any treatment effect." The second is the grouping. The large-stone group also contains patients with multiple stones, a heterogeneous category within which the two treatments could still have been given to patients of different severity. Stone size is the variable the data happen to record; it is not guaranteed to be the only one that differed between the patients chosen for each treatment.

The third treatment in the paper is usually left out of the retelling, and it outperforms both. ESWL succeeded in 200 of 204 patients with small stones, 98.0 per cent, in 101 of 124 with large or multiple stones, 81.5 per cent, and in 301 of 328 overall, 91.8 per cent. The paper's own conclusion was about lithotripsy, not about the comparison that made it famous: "Stones greater than 2 cm in diameter lead to a high failure rate with all forms of treatment, but much less so with ESWL."

## Batting averages

The most popular example outside medicine and admissions is from baseball, and it was publicised by Kenneth Ross in a book on mathematics and the sport (Ross, 2004). In 1995 Derek Jeter of the New York Yankees had 12 hits in 48 at-bats, a batting average of .250, and David Justice of the Atlanta Braves had 104 in 411, .253. In 1996 Jeter had 183 in 582, .314, and Justice 45 in 140, .321. Justice had the higher average in each season; over the two seasons combined, Jeter had 195 in 630, .310, against Justice's 149 in 551, .270. The script reads these figures from the Lahman baseball database and adds 1997, in which Justice again had the higher average, 163 in 495 (.329) against Jeter's 190 in 654 (.291). Over all three seasons Jeter is still ahead, .2998 against .2983.

The mechanism is, once more, allocation. Of Jeter's at-bats over 1995 and 1996, 7.6 per cent came in 1995, the season in which both players hit worse; of Justice's, 74.6 per cent. The aggregate difference of +0.0391 in Jeter's favour is an allocation component of +0.0445 and a within-season component of −0.0054. Keeping both players' seasonal averages and Jeter's at-bats fixed, the two-season averages would be equal if 17.4 per cent of Justice's at-bats had come in 1995; above that share, Jeter leads. With 74.6 per cent, Justice was far past it. The reason has nothing to do with either player's hitting: Jeter played 15 games in 1995, and Justice 40 in 1996.

The evidence for the within-season advantage is essentially nil. In 1995 the difference was three thousandths of a batting average, with a Fisher p of 1.000; in 1996, seven thousandths, with p = 0.919. The Mantel–Haenszel odds ratio over the two seasons is 0.972 (0.690 to 1.370), p = 0.872. The aggregate difference of 39 points of batting average, which looks large to a baseball fan, has p = 0.158. A reversal requires two comparisons to point in opposite directions, and in this example neither comparison is distinguishable from no difference at all. The example is a correct illustration of the arithmetic and says nothing about which player was the better hitter.

## How much evidence each table carries

A reversal is a claim about two comparisons at once: the aggregate points one way and the stratified comparison points the other. In a sample, both can be true by chance. The question an analyst should ask of any reported reversal, before asking which table to believe, is how probable it is that both hold in the population from which the sample came.

The script answers that question with a simple Bayesian model, the same for every example. Each stratum-specific rate gets a uniform prior and a binomial likelihood, so its posterior is a Beta distribution. Each group's distribution across strata gets a uniform Dirichlet prior and a multinomial likelihood, so the population mix is uncertain too. From 200,000 draws of all of these, it computes the population aggregate rate for each group, the population difference standardised to a common mix, and the population rate within each stratum. Four probabilities follow: that the aggregate favours the group the sample aggregate favours; that the standardised difference favours the other group; that every stratum favours the other group; and that both the first and the third hold together, which is a full reversal in the population.

For Berkeley's six departments, the probabilities are 1.000 that the aggregate favours men, 0.984 that the standardised difference favours women, 0.010 that every department favours women, and 0.010 for a full reversal. The aggregate gap is certain, the adjusted comparison very probably points the other way, and the claim that women were favoured in every department is almost certainly false, because departments C and E point the other way and department A dominates the rest.

For the kidney stones, the probabilities are 0.937 that the aggregate favours PCNL, 0.938 that the standardised difference favours open surgery, 0.733 that open surgery is better in both stone groups, and 0.670 that the full reversal holds. The reversal is more likely than not, about two to one, but far from established, and that is before any account is taken of the confounding by period.

For Jeter and Justice over 1995 and 1996, the probabilities are 0.928 that the aggregate favours Jeter, 0.538 that the standardised difference favours Justice, 0.279 that Justice was better in both seasons, and 0.227 for the full reversal. Over all three seasons, the aggregate probability falls to 0.530, the standardised one rises to 0.816, and the full reversal falls to 0.066. The more data are added, the less there is to explain: over three seasons, the two players hit at almost exactly the same average, and Justice was very slightly better in each.

The three examples differ in a way the usual retelling hides. At Berkeley, the aggregate and the adjusted comparisons are both well determined and point in opposite directions; the substantive question is which one answers the question being asked, and the data are good enough for that question to matter. In the kidney stones, both comparisons are uncertain and the reversal is a modest probability, confounded by period; the example illustrates the arithmetic correctly and the clinical conclusion weakly. In the batting averages, neither comparison is distinguishable from zero, and the reversal is an accident of how many at-bats each player had in a bad season.

None of this makes the paradox less real. A reversal does not need statistical significance to mislead: an analyst who looks only at the aggregate of the kidney-stone data would recommend PCNL, and one who looks only within stone sizes would recommend open surgery, and the data would support neither with any confidence. But it does mean that the first question to ask of a reported reversal is the same question one asks of any reported difference: how large is it compared with its uncertainty?

## The examples that were not reversals

The history also shows that the word "paradox" has been applied to three different phenomena, and that most of the founding examples belong to the first two.

The first is an association that appears in the aggregate when there is none within groups. Pearson's skulls, Yule's mixed lines of inheritance, Yule's hospital and Bickel's hypothetical departments are all of this kind. It is by far the most common in practice, because it needs only that the groups differ in their mix and in their rates, which is true of almost any two groups of people.

The second is an association within groups that disappears in the aggregate. Simpson's cards and Simpson's patients are of this kind. It is the form Simpson actually wrote about, and his paper is about when the vanishing is the sensible answer and when it is the misleading one.

The third is a reversal, an association within every group whose sign flips in the aggregate. Blyth's constructed example is of this kind, and so, approximately, are the kidney stones and the batting averages. So is an often-cited case from official statistics collected by Clifford Wagner in a short paper titled "Simpson's paradox in real life", in which, as it is usually summarised, the average federal income tax rate in the United States fell in every income bracket between 1974 and 1978 while the overall rate rose, which can only happen if a larger share of income had moved into the higher, more heavily taxed brackets (Wagner, 1982). In that example the strata are not groups of people being compared but the same population at two dates, and the allocation term is the movement of income between brackets. It requires more: the allocation must not only differ between the groups compared but must differ in a direction correlated with the stratum-specific rates, strongly enough to outweigh the within-group difference. Berkeley's six departments come close, with the adjusted comparison reversing the aggregate but two departments dissenting.

All three are the same phenomenon in the sense that matters. In each case, the aggregate comparison mixes two things, the difference between the groups compared within strata and the difference in how they are distributed across strata, and the decomposition of the aggregate into an allocation component and a within-stratum component makes the mixture explicit. Whether the aggregate or the stratified comparison is the right one is not a question that the decomposition answers. It is the question Simpson posed with his cards and his patients, and the one the next article takes up.

## What the history separates

The classic examples of Simpson's paradox are usually presented as interchangeable illustrations of one lesson, and they are not. Pearson's skulls and Yule's records were warnings about pooling heterogeneous data, written by people building the theory of correlation and association. Simpson's paper was a careful logical point about the interpretation of three-way tables, and its best-known example does not reverse anything. Blyth gave the reversal its name and its connection to decision theory. Berkeley was a real investigation with large samples, in which the reversal of the adjusted comparison is well established and the policy question it raises is genuine. The kidney stones are a real comparison with small samples and a confounded design, in which the reversal is a probability of about two in three. And Jeter and Justice are a correct piece of arithmetic on differences too small to mean anything.

What they have in common is the decomposition. In every example, the aggregate difference between two groups is the sum of a difference in how the groups are distributed across strata and a difference in how they fare within strata. A reversal happens when the first is large and of the opposite sign. That much is arithmetic, and it is the part every introduction explains.

What they do not have in common is the answer to the question that matters for a decision: should the comparison be made within strata or across them? For Simpson's cards, across. For Simpson's patients, within. For Berkeley, it depends on whether the question is about the admissions committees or about the whole pipeline of education that brought applicants to them, and Bickel and his co-authors were explicit that the second question had an answer of its own. For the kidney stones, within, if stone size is a cause of both the choice of treatment and the outcome, and neither table is reliable if the period of treatment matters too.

Those answers are not in the tables. The same numbers can come from different causal structures, and the structure decides which table is right. Judea Pearl, who more than anyone made that statement precise, has gone as far as to write that with the tools of causal inference "we can safely proclaim Simpson's paradox 'resolved'" (Pearl, 2014). Whether the resolution is as complete as that claims is a fair question, and the second article in this series takes it up: it sets out the tools, applies them to the examples above, and shows what can and cannot be learned about the structure from the data themselves.
