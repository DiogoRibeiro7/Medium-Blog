# Reversals in Real Data: COVID-19 Fatality by Age, SAT Scores and Test Participation, Penguin Bills by Species, and How Often Chance Produces Them

## Part 3 of 4. Four data sets in which the aggregate and the stratified comparison disagree, each analysed with the question it actually answers, followed by a measurement of how often reversals appear when nothing structural is causing them

The first two articles in this series stayed close to the textbook. The first recomputed the classic tables and found that several of them carried far less evidence than their fame suggests. The second argued that the choice between the aggregate and the stratified table is a causal question, settled by the structure behind the data and not by the data themselves. Both worked with small, familiar tables. This article goes to data in which a reversal occurs at scale and the choice of table changes a conclusion someone might act on.

There are four cases. The first is the comparison of COVID-19 case fatality in China and Italy in early 2020, which Julius von Kügelgen, Luigi Gresele and Bernhard Schölkopf analysed as a problem in mediation and which shows a reversal that is real, large and changing over time. The second is the relation between teacher pay and average SAT scores across US states, where a negative correlation turns positive once the share of students taking the test is accounted for, and where the variable that reverses the sign is a selection mechanism rather than a classic confounder. The third is a set of penguins whose bill measurements are negatively related across species and positively related within each of them, a reversal that repeats itself when a further variable is added. The fourth is not a single case but a count: how often reversals appear in random tables, in samples from populations that have none, and in thirty years of batting averages, where the observed number of reversals can be compared with the number that noise alone would produce.

Each case is analysed with the same three questions in mind. What does each comparison estimate? Which of those estimands is the question someone actually wants answered? And how strong is the evidence for the reversal, as opposed to the arithmetic of it? Every number computed below is printed by a script in the series repository, linked at the end; the data are downloaded from pinned versions of public repositories and checked against digests.

## COVID-19 in China and Italy, early 2020

In the first weeks of the pandemic, the case fatality rate, the share of confirmed cases who died, was widely compared across countries. The Chinese Center for Disease Control and Prevention reported, for cases confirmed by 11 February 2020, 1,023 deaths among 44,672 cases, a rate of 2.3 per cent (China CDC Weekly, 2020; Wu and McGoogan, 2020). The Istituto Superiore di Sanità reported, for Italy on 9 March 2020, 357 deaths among 8,026 cases of known age, a rate of 4.4 per cent (Istituto Superiore di Sanità, 2020). Italy's rate was almost twice China's.

Within age bands, the order reverses. In every band from 10 to 19 years upwards, Italy's case fatality rate was lower than China's: 0.0 against 0.18 per cent in the band from 10 to 19, 0.0 against 0.19 in the twenties, 0.0 against 0.24 in the thirties, 0.11 against 0.44 in the forties, 0.21 against 1.30 in the fifties, 2.52 against 3.60 in the sixties, 6.39 against 7.96 in the seventies and 13.19 against 14.77 per cent among cases aged 80 or more. In the youngest band both rates were zero. Italy was lower in eight of nine bands and equal in the ninth. Figure 1 shows both halves of the picture.

![Figure 1](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/simpsons-paradox/part3/figures/fig1_covid_age.png)

*Figure 1. Left: case fatality rate by age in China (cases confirmed by 11 February 2020, reported 17 February) and Italy (9 March 2020), with each country's overall rate as a dashed line. Right: the age distribution of confirmed cases in each country.*

The reason is the age of the cases. Of China's confirmed cases, 11.9 per cent were aged 70 or over; of Italy's, 41.3 per cent. Since case fatality rises steeply with age, a country whose confirmed cases are older will have a higher overall rate even if it does better at every age.

Von Kügelgen and his co-authors framed this as a mediation problem (von Kügelgen, Gresele and Schölkopf, 2021). "The country C causally influences fatality F along two different paths": a direct path from country to fatality, through everything about how a country's cases were treated, and "an indirect path C → A → F mediated by A," the age of the cases. Each of the three effects answers a different question. The total effect answers "What would be the effect on fatality of changing country from China to Italy?" The direct effect answers "For the Chinese case demographic, would the Italian approach have been better?" The indirect effect answers "How would the overall CFR in China change if the case demographic had instead been that from Italy, while keeping all else (i.e., CFR's of each age group) the same?"

The script reproduces their numbers exactly from the counts. The total effect of Italy against China is +2.158 percentage points, the difference in crude rates. The direct effect, Italy's age-specific rates against China's averaged over China's age distribution, is −0.763 points: with the Chinese case mix, the Italian rates would have produced fewer deaths. The indirect effect, China's age-specific rates averaged over Italy's age distribution against China's own, is +3.268 points: the Italian case mix alone, with Chinese care, would have produced many more. The direct effect averaged over Italy's age distribution instead is −1.110 points, and that version together with the indirect effect adds exactly to the total; the version with China's mix does not, adding to +2.505, a reminder from the second article that direct and indirect effects defined at different reference levels need not sum to the total.

Which of these numbers is the right one depends on who is asking. A planner estimating how many deaths Italy's epidemic would produce needs the total effect, or rather the crude rate itself, since the age of Italy's cases was a fact of the Italian epidemic and not a distortion of it. A clinician or health-system analyst asking whether Italian hospitals were treating cases of a given age worse than Chinese ones needs the direct effect, which in early March said they were not. A demographer asking how much of the gap the age structure of the epidemic accounts for needs the indirect effect. The headlines of spring 2020 mostly reported the first number and read it as if it were the second, which is the standard misreading of a Simpson reversal: an aggregate comparison, correct as a description, taken as an answer to a question about the strata.

The evidence for the direct effect is strong. Under uniform priors on every age-specific rate and on each country's age distribution, the posterior probability that Italy's overall rate was higher is 1.0000 to four decimals, and the posterior 95 per cent interval for the direct effect at China's age mix runs from −0.890 to −0.350 points, with a posterior probability of 1.0000 that it is negative. (The interval is pulled towards zero relative to the point estimate because the uniform priors give Italy's empty young bands a positive rate.) The claim that Italy was lower in every single band is much weaker: the posterior probability that Italy's rate was lower in all eight bands with at least one death is 0.0641, because the young bands contain very few deaths in either country and almost no information. The probability that Italy was lower in each of the four bands from 50 upwards, where the deaths were, is 0.8611. The reversal that matters, between the crude comparison and the age-adjusted one, is decisive; the textbook form, every stratum one way and the total the other, is not established, and does not need to be.

## The same comparison, three months long

The pinned repository that accompanies von Kügelgen and co-authors' paper also contains Italian data at thirteen later dates, from 12 March to 26 May 2020. Comparing each with the Chinese data shows the structure changing. Figure 2 plots the three effects.

![Figure 2](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/simpsons-paradox/part3/figures/fig2_covid_time.png)

*Figure 2. Effects of Italy against China (cases confirmed by 11 February) on the case fatality rate, with Italian data at successive dates in 2020: the total effect (difference in crude rates), the indirect effect through the age distribution of cases, and the direct effect evaluated at China's age distribution.*

The indirect effect barely moves: it is +3.268 points on 9 March, +3.194 on 12 March, between about 2.95 and 3.50 for the rest of the period, and +3.444 on 26 May. The age of Italy's confirmed cases stayed much older than China's throughout. The direct effect, by contrast, changes sign. It is −0.763 points on 9 March and −0.236 on 12 March, then +1.185 on 19 March, +1.640 on 26 March, +2.864 on 2 April and +3.827 by 26 May. By then Italy's crude rate was 13.73 per cent among 230,760 cases, a total effect of +11.437 points. The authors describe the turn in their conclusions: "The direct effect initially was negative, meaning that age-specific fatality in Italy was lower; however, it changes sign around mid-March when an overloaded health system in northern Italy was reported."

The time series matters for reading the reversal. On 9 March, the aggregate comparison said Italy was doing worse and the stratified comparison said Italy was doing better, and the stratified comparison was the right answer to the question about the quality of care. By the end of March, both comparisons said Italy was doing worse. The reversal was a feature of a particular moment, and a single snapshot would have suggested a stable conclusion that did not last.

Two caveats travel with every number in this section, and the authors state the first themselves: "we only consider confirmed cases, i.e., patients who tested positively for Covid-19," which creates "a problem of selection bias." Case fatality depends on who is tested, and in early 2020 testing in Italy was concentrated on people with symptoms, who were older and sicker. In the language of the second article, being a confirmed case is a variable affected both by age and by severity, and conditioning on it is conditioning on something close to a collider. The age distribution of confirmed cases is partly an artefact of testing, and so are the age-specific rates. The mediation analysis decomposes the crude difference correctly given the data, but the data are a selected sample of infections, and an analysis of infection fatality would need estimates of infections, not confirmed cases.

How much can testing alone move the decomposition? A stylised calculation gives a sense of scale. Take a hypothetical country B with exactly China's infections and deaths in every age band, so that nothing about care or the virus differs, and suppose B confirms every death but only a share of the surviving infections under 60, the age at which mild cases were least likely to be tested. If B confirms all of them, it is China, and every effect is zero. If it confirms three-quarters, its crude case fatality is 2.76 per cent, the share of its confirmed cases aged 70 or over is 14.4 per cent instead of 11.9, and the decomposition attributes +0.143 points to a direct effect and +0.343 to an indirect one. If it confirms half, the crude rate is 3.48 per cent and the direct effect +0.426 points; if a quarter, 4.70 per cent, a share aged 70 or over of 24.5 per cent and a direct effect of +1.253 points. None of these direct effects says anything about care, because by construction care is identical. Differences in testing feed both the age mix of confirmed cases and the age-specific rates among them, and the decomposition, which takes confirmed cases as given, cannot separate them from differences in treatment. The numbers are illustrative, not an estimate of what happened in either country.

The second caveat is about the comparison itself. The Chinese data were collected by 11 February and the Italian data from March onwards, at different stages of different epidemics. The direct effect compares the treatment of cases in two health systems at two different times; it is not a measure of the quality of either system in general.

## Every pair of countries

The same repository contains age-stratified counts for twelve countries at dates between February and May 2020. Leaving out the Diamond Princess cruise ship, which is not a country and has few cases, eleven remain, and the script compares every pair of them, 55 in all. For each pair it computes the crude difference in case fatality rates and the difference standardised to the average of the two countries' age distributions.

For 16 of the 55 pairs, the crude and the age-standardised comparisons point in opposite directions. For three, the reversal is complete in the textbook sense: every age band with at least one death favours one country while the crude rate favours the other. The largest disagreements involve Spain. Against Colombia, Spain's crude rate was 4.71 points higher and its age-standardised rate 2.36 points lower; against Argentina, 4.50 points higher and 2.35 lower; against South Africa, 5.84 points higher and 0.30 lower. Switzerland against Colombia shows the same pattern, 2.13 points higher crude and 2.00 points lower standardised. The pattern is driven by the same variable as before. The share of confirmed cases aged 70 or over ranged from 4.3 per cent in South Africa and 7.1 per cent in Colombia to 36.8 per cent in Spain and 40.0 per cent in Italy, with Portugal, at 22.2 per cent and a crude rate of 4.33 per cent on 28 May, in between.

A reversal in more than a quarter of the pairs is not chance. It is what happens when a quantity that varies by a factor of a hundred across strata, case fatality from the twenties to the eighties, is averaged over distributions that differ by a factor of ten in their oldest band. Von Kügelgen and co-authors, analysing all 132 ordered pairs of their twelve countries, found that "direct and indirect effects across 132 pairs of countries are only weakly correlated": knowing that a country's case mix was old says little about how well it treated cases of a given age. Ranking countries by crude case fatality in spring 2020 ranked them largely by the age of their confirmed cases, which in turn depended on whom each country tested.

The effect on a league table can be measured directly. Standardising each country's age-specific rates to a common age distribution, the average of the eleven countries' distributions of confirmed cases, removes the differences in case mix and leaves the differences in age-specific fatality. The two rankings agree only loosely: the Spearman correlation between the crude and the standardised ranks is +0.618, and the largest move is five places. Italy stays first, at 13.73 per cent crude and 8.08 per cent standardised, and Sweden and the Netherlands stay near the top. South Africa moves from last to sixth, from 2.12 to 5.29 per cent; Colombia from eighth to fourth, from 3.24 to 7.30; Argentina from seventh to fifth. Spain falls from fourth to eighth, from 7.96 to 4.91 per cent, and Portugal from sixth to tenth, from 4.33 to 4.11, on data for 28 May 2020. China is last on the standardised scale, at 3.34 per cent, and tenth on the crude one.

The standardised table has its own assumption, which is the choice of the standard population. Directly standardised rates are weighted averages of age-specific rates, and a different standard gives different weights; with case fatality rising a hundredfold across age bands, a standard weighted towards the old magnifies differences in the oldest bands and one weighted towards the young magnifies differences that rest on a handful of deaths. The ranking above uses the average case mix of the eleven countries because it is neutral among them, not because it is correct. The standardised ranking answers the question "which country did worse with a given case of a given age", and it inherits every problem of comparing confirmed cases across testing regimes. The crude ranking answers the question "which country recorded more deaths per confirmed case", which is a real quantity and, for most purposes, the wrong one.

## SAT scores and teacher pay

In 1999 Deborah Lynn Guber published a teaching paper on what she called "the spending/performance paradox" (Guber, 1999). Across the fifty US states in 1994–95, average total SAT scores were lower where schools spent more per pupil: the slope was −20.89 points per thousand dollars of spending and the correlation −0.381. The resolution, in her words, "hinges on students recognizing the importance of an omitted variable": the share of eligible students who take the SAT. In some states nearly all college-bound students took it; in others, where the ACT was the usual test, only a small and self-selected group did, and they were the strongest students. Controlling for the share taking the test, the partial correlation "reverses to +.391," and the spending coefficient becomes +12.29.

The script reproduces Guber's spending regressions exactly from the mosaicData version of her data and adds the same analysis for teacher salary, which her data set includes but her paper does not analyse. Average SAT score falls by 5.540 points per thousand dollars of average teacher salary (95 per cent interval −8.822 to −2.257). Adding the share of students taking the test turns the slope positive: +2.180 points per thousand dollars (+0.110 to +4.251), with the share taking the test at −2.779 points per percentage point. The correlations explain the reversal. The share taking the test is strongly negatively correlated with the average score (−0.887) and positively correlated with salary (+0.617): states that paid teachers more were, for reasons of geography and history, states where most students took the SAT. Figure 3 shows the pattern.

![Figure 3](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/simpsons-paradox/part3/figures/fig3_sat.png)

*Figure 3. Average total SAT score against average teacher salary in the fifty US states, 1994–95, coloured by the share of eligible students taking the SAT (thirds of the states). Solid line: regression across all states. Dashed lines: the regression controlling for the share taking the test, drawn at each third's mean share.*

The extremes of the distribution show the mechanism in individual states. The three states with the lowest participation, Mississippi and Utah at 4 per cent and North Dakota at 5, had average scores of 1,036, 1,076 and 1,107 and paid teachers 26.8, 29.1 and 26.3 thousand dollars. The three with the highest, Connecticut at 81 per cent, Massachusetts at 80 and New York at 74, had averages of 908, 907 and 892 and paid 50.0, 40.8 and 47.6 thousand dollars. A comparison of these six states alone would suggest that teacher pay lowers scores by a large amount. What it actually compares is the best few per cent of students in three states with most of the students in three others.

Stratifying more coarsely tells a more cautious story. Splitting the states into thirds by the share taking the test, at 11 and 55 per cent, gives groups whose mean shares are 7.3, 28.1 and 67.4 per cent and whose mean scores are 1,052.1, 965.2 and 890.6. Within the thirds, the slopes of score on salary are +1.85 (standard error 2.47), −0.81 (2.47) and +1.45 (0.85): small, mixed in sign and individually indistinguishable from zero. A regression with a separate intercept for each third gives a salary slope of +0.673, with an interval from −1.519 to +2.865. The reversal from negative to positive is clear when the share taking the test is controlled as a continuous variable, and becomes an absence of any relation when it is controlled in three coarse groups, which leave considerable variation in participation within each.

The form of the continuous control matters less than the coarseness of the grouping. With the logarithm of the share taking the test, which lets the effect of moving from 4 to 8 per cent be larger than the effect of moving from 60 to 64, the salary slope is +2.576 points per thousand dollars (+0.984 to +4.168). With a quadratic in the share, it is +1.803 (+0.193 to +3.413). Every smooth control gives a positive slope with an interval that excludes zero; the three-group control gives an interval that straddles it. When a stratifying variable is continuous and its effect on the outcome is strong, cutting it into a few groups leaves residual confounding within them, and the stratified estimate drifts back towards the crude one. The same thing happens, less visibly, whenever an analyst bins a continuous covariate to build a table.

The structure behind this reversal differs from the one behind the COVID-19 comparison, and the difference matters for interpretation. The share of students taking the test is not a cause of the state average that operates through students' learning. It is a selection mechanism: it determines which students' scores enter the average. A state where 4 per cent of students take the SAT reports the average of its strongest 4 per cent; a state where 81 per cent take it reports the average of most of its students. The negative crude relation between pay and scores is a comparison of averages over different populations, and controlling for participation is an attempt to put them on a common footing. A linear control for participation does that only approximately, since the effect of selecting the top 4 per cent rather than the top 30 per cent is unlikely to be linear in the share.

Whether the adjusted relation means that paying teachers more raises scores is a different question, and the data cannot answer it. The comparison is between states, which differ in income, demography and many other things that affect both salaries and scores, and a state-level relation, adjusted or not, says nothing directly about the effect of a raise on the students of any one teacher. What the reversal does establish is narrower and still useful: the crude negative relation, which was used in public argument as evidence that spending does not help, is an artefact of who takes the test.

## Penguin bills, by species and by sex

The Palmer penguins data set, published by Kristen Gorman, Tony Williams and William Fraser in 2014 and packaged for teaching by Allison Horst, Alison Hill and Gorman, contains bill measurements for three species of penguin from three islands in the Palmer Archipelago (Gorman, Williams and Fraser, 2014; Horst, Hill and Gorman, 2020). Its authors use it explicitly to illustrate the paradox: "When penguin species is omitted, bill length and depth appear negatively correlated overall," and "the trend is reversed when species is included, revealing an obviously positive correlation between bill length and bill depth within species" (Horst, Hill and Gorman, 2022).

For the 342 penguins with both measurements, 151 Adélie, 68 Chinstrap and 123 Gentoo, the pooled slope of bill depth on bill length is −0.0850 millimetres per millimetre, with a 95 per cent interval of −0.1225 to −0.0475, and a correlation of −0.235. Within species the slopes are +0.1788 for Adélie, +0.2222 for Chinstrap and +0.2048 for Gentoo, all with intervals well above zero, and correlations of +0.391, +0.654 and +0.643. A regression with a common slope and a separate intercept for each species gives +0.1999 (+0.1655 to +0.2343). Figure 4 shows why: Adélie penguins have short, deep bills, Gentoo penguins long, shallow ones, and Chinstraps long, deep ones, so that the species cloud as a whole tilts the other way from each species within it.

![Figure 4](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/simpsons-paradox/part3/figures/fig4_penguins.png)

*Figure 4. Bill depth against bill length for 342 Palmer penguins, with the regression line within each species and across all penguins (dashed).*

The continuous version of the decomposition of the first article makes the mechanism exact. The covariance between bill length and bill depth over all 342 penguins, −2.527 square millimetres, is the sum of a within-species part, the average of the covariances inside each species weighted by species size, and a between-species part, the covariance of the species means weighted the same way. The within-species part is +1.736 and the between-species part −4.263. The pooled relation is negative because the species means are arranged along a line that runs against the relation inside each species, and that arrangement contributes more than twice as much to the total covariance as the relation inside the species does. This is the law of total covariance, and it is to continuous variables what the allocation and within-stratum decomposition is to rates: a reversal happens when the between-group term has the opposite sign to the within-group term and is larger.

The example is usually stopped there, and it is worth taking one step further, because the next step shows that "the" stratified answer is itself a choice. Male penguins are larger than females within each species, in both bill length and bill depth. Sex therefore plays within species the role that species plays across them: it adds a component of joint variation between the two measurements that comes from differences between groups, not from the relation within a group. Adding a separate intercept for each combination of species and sex, for the 333 penguins of known sex, reduces the common slope from +0.1999 to +0.0674, with an interval of +0.0288 to +0.1059. The slope stays positive, but most of the within-species relation turns out to be a between-sex relation.

The same sequence appears whenever a model is fitted to grouped data. A regression with no group terms estimates a mixture of within-group and between-group relations, weighted by how much each contributes to the variation in the predictor. A regression with group fixed effects estimates the within-group relation only. A mixed model with group random effects estimates something in between, shrinking towards one or the other depending on how much the group means vary relative to the noise. The authors of the penguin package note that the reversal by species is only one of several in their data, and the sex-by-species step above is another. Every one of these estimators is a legitimate summary, and their disagreement is information about the structure, not a problem to be resolved by choosing the one with the best fit.

Which slope is right depends on the question, and here the question is biological. If the interest is in how bill depth scales with bill length across species, as a question about the evolution of feeding apparatus, the pooled relation across species means are the object of interest, and the within-species slopes are a nuisance. If the interest is in how the two dimensions covary among individuals of a species, the within-species slope is the answer, and whether sex should be held fixed depends on whether the difference between the sexes is part of the covariation being studied or a separate phenomenon. Each successive stratification answers a narrower question. None of them is wrong, and none of them is the answer to a question that has not been stated.

## How often reversals happen without a cause

The three cases so far had a structural explanation: age, selection into the test, species and sex. A reversal can also appear with no structure behind it, as the Jeter and Justice example in the first article showed. How often does that happen?

The theoretical benchmark comes from Marios Pavlides and Michael Perlman, who asked what proportion of all 2 × 2 × 2 tables exhibit the paradox (Pavlides and Perlman, 2009). If a table is drawn uniformly from the set of all probability tables with eight cells, the probability that both sub-tables show an association of one sign and the combined table the opposite sign is exactly 1/60, a result for which they presented a proof due to Peter Hadjicostas. The script checks it with two million random tables and finds reversals in 0.01673 of them, with a Monte Carlo standard error of 0.00009, against 1/60 = 0.01667. Rogier Kievit and co-authors, citing the same figure, observed that it "suggests SP might occur in nearly 2% of comparable datasets, but reports of SP in empirical data are far less common" (Kievit et al., 2013).

The 1/60 figure is about populations. Samples add a second source of reversals: a population without a reversal can produce a sample with one. The script draws 20,000 population tables uniformly from the simplex, keeps only those without a reversal, and draws a sample of size n from each. With 50 observations, 1.58 per cent of the samples show a reversal the population does not have; with 100, 1.38 per cent; with 500, 0.78 per cent; with 1,000, 0.53 per cent; and with 5,000, 0.31 per cent. Spurious reversals become rare only in large samples, and in small ones they are about as common as the real reversals that a random population would contain. The left panel of Figure 5 draws the curve from a separate run of the same simulation.

![Figure 5](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/simpsons-paradox/part3/figures/fig5_chance.png)

*Figure 5. Left: share of samples showing a reversal when drawn from random population tables that have none, by sample size (a separate run of the simulation reported in the text). Right: reversals per thousand pairs of regular baseball players over consecutive seasons, 1990–2018, observed and in one simulation in which each player's true average is the same in both seasons.*

Random tables are a weak model of real data, so the script turns to a large real data set in which every reversal can be counted: thirty years of major league batting. For every pair of consecutive seasons from 1990–91 to 2018–19, it takes every player with at least 100 at-bats in both, and for every pair of such players checks whether one had the higher batting average in each season and the lower average over the two combined, exactly the Jeter and Justice pattern. Over 29 season pairs there are 1,573,467 pairs of players, and 4,055 of them show a reversal: 0.00258 of pairs, about one in 388. The rate varies between about 1.9 per thousand pairs in 1994–95, the strike-shortened seasons, with 77 reversals among 41,328 pairs, and about 3.4 per thousand in 1998–99, with 195 among 57,291.

How many of these would appear by chance? The script builds a null model in which nothing about a player changes between the two seasons: each player's true average is his observed average over the two seasons combined, his at-bats in each season are fixed at their observed values, and his hits in each season are drawn from a binomial distribution. Twenty simulations of this model produce a mean of 3,240 reversals, with a range of 3,076 to 3,411. The observed count of 4,055 is 1.251 times the null mean and above every simulated value. Most baseball reversals are noise, in the sense that a model with no change in any player's ability produces four-fifths of them. But a quarter more appear than noise would produce, and the right panel of Figure 5 shows the excess in most of the 29 season pairs.

None of the individual reversals is evidence of anything about the players involved. For each of the 4,055 reversing pairs, the script computes the difference in batting average in each season and over the two combined, divided by its binomial standard error. In no pair are both season differences larger than 1.96 standard errors, as a conventional test would require; in only two pairs is even one of them; and in no pair is the combined difference. The median absolute z statistic of the season differences is 0.136 and that of the combined difference 0.100. Reversals happen between players who hit almost identically, because only near-identical averages can be reordered by a difference in playing time. The population-level excess and the individual-level noise are not in conflict. A mechanism that shifts the balance of at-bats slightly towards good seasons will produce a few more reversals across a million and a half pairs, while leaving every single reversal indistinguishable from chance.

The excess has a structural explanation that the null model leaves out. Playing time responds to performance: a player who hits well gets more at-bats, and a player who slumps or is injured gets fewer. Across 9,555 player season pairs, the rank correlation between the change in a player's batting average and the change in his at-bats from one season to the next is +0.244. That correlation is exactly the ingredient a reversal needs. A player whose good season is his long season and whose bad season is his short one has a combined average weighted towards his good season; a rival with the opposite pattern has a combined average weighted towards his bad one. The Jeter and Justice reversal of 1995–96 was an instance of it, with Jeter's 15 games in 1995 and Justice's 40 in 1996, although, as the first article showed, the differences in that case were too small to say anything about either player. It would not even be counted here: Jeter had 48 at-bats in 1995, below the threshold of 100 that defines a regular player for this count. The most famous reversal in baseball depends on a season in which one of the two players barely played.

The empirical literature on how often reversals occur in real data is thin. Nazanin Alipourfard, Peter Fennell and Kristina Lerman, searching user-activity data from Stack Exchange, found reversals in 7 of 110 pairs of variables they examined (Alipourfard, Fennell and Lerman, 2018); other data-mining studies report counts without the number of comparisons from which they came. The baseball count is useful precisely because the denominator is known and a credible null model exists. It suggests a rule of thumb that the other cases in this article confirm: in data with many comparisons, reversals will appear, most of them will be noise when the strata are small, and the ones worth taking seriously are those with a mechanism that can be named and a sample large enough to make the stratum-level differences visible.

## Searching for reversals

The baseball count was a search: every pair of players, every pair of seasons, looking for one pattern. Searches like it are increasingly automated. Data-mining tools scan a table for every combination of an outcome, a comparison and a stratifying variable in which the sign flips, and report the hits as findings. The cases in this article suggest what such a search needs in order to mean anything.

It needs a denominator. A list of reversals found is uninterpretable without the number of comparisons examined. With 1,573,467 pairs of players, 4,055 reversals is a rate of one in 388; with no denominator, it would read as a striking number of paradoxes.

It needs a null model that matches the data. Random tables give 1/60, a rate far above what most real data show and irrelevant to any particular data set. A null that keeps the structure of the data and removes only the mechanism being tested, as the constant-ability model of batting does, gives a baseline against which the observed count means something. Here it showed that four-fifths of the reversals are what noise produces.

It needs a filter on evidence. A reversal whose parts are each indistinguishable from zero is an arithmetic event, not a finding. In the baseball data every one of the reversals fails that filter. In the COVID-19 data the reversal between crude and age-adjusted comparisons passes it decisively. In the SAT data it passes with a smooth control and fails with a coarse one.

And it needs a mechanism. In each case where a reversal survived scrutiny, there was a variable whose role could be named and argued for: age, test participation, species. Where no mechanism can be named, a reversal found by search is better treated as a candidate for the next data set than as a result.

## Reading a reversal in real data

The four cases share a sequence of steps that turns a reversal from a curiosity into an analysis.

The first step is to name the variable that drives the reversal and to say what kind of variable it is. In the COVID-19 comparison it is age, a mediator of the effect of country on fatality, and a variable through which testing practice also operates. In the SAT data it is the share taking the test, a selection mechanism that determines whose scores are averaged. In the penguins it is species, and then sex, groups whose differences in means generate a covariation unrelated to the relation within groups. In baseball it is playing time, a variable that responds to the outcome being measured. Each kind calls for a different reading, as the second article set out.

The second step is to say which comparison answers which question. For COVID-19, the total effect answers a question about the outcome of the epidemic as it unfolded, the direct effect a question about how cases of a given age fared, and the indirect effect a question about how much of the difference the age of the cases explains; all three are legitimate and they differ in sign. For SAT scores, the crude relation answers a question about published state averages and the adjusted one a question about states with comparable participation; neither answers a question about the effect of pay. For the penguins, each level of stratification answers a narrower biological question. For batting averages, the season-by-season comparison answers a question about hitting and the combined one a question about a mixture of hitting and playing time.

The third step is to measure the evidence. The COVID-19 reversal between crude and adjusted comparisons is decisive, while the stronger claim that every age band favoured Italy is not. The SAT reversal is clear with a continuous control and disappears into noise with a coarse one. The penguin reversal is decisive at every level. Most batting-average reversals are noise, and a minority reflect a real mechanism. In each case the reversal came with a different strength of evidence for each of its parts, and the summary "the aggregate is misleading" would have hidden all of it.

The fourth step is to check the stability of the conclusion. The COVID-19 direct effect changed sign within ten days. The penguin within-species slope fell by two-thirds when sex was added. The SAT reversal depends on how participation is controlled. A reversal is a statement about a particular partition of a particular data set at a particular time, and its interest lies in what it reveals about the structure, not in its sign.

## The same arithmetic in institutional comparisons

The COVID-19 league table is one instance of a problem that institutions face constantly: comparing hospitals, schools, branches or regions whose populations differ. A hospital that treats older and sicker patients will have higher crude mortality than one that treats younger ones, whatever the quality of its care; a school whose pupils arrive with lower attainment will have lower average results at the end. The standard response in health-services research is risk adjustment or case-mix adjustment, which is standardisation under another name: each institution's outcomes are compared with what would be expected given the characteristics of the people it serves.

The three cases in this article show the two ways that response can go wrong. If the adjustment variable is a confounder of the comparison, as age is for the effect of a hospital on mortality, adjusting for it is necessary, and the adjusted comparison is the right one for questions about quality. If the variable is affected by the institution's own practice, as the age distribution of confirmed cases was affected by testing policy, adjusting for it removes part of the effect being measured, or adds a distortion, and the adjusted comparison answers a question nobody asked. And if the variable defines who enters the comparison at all, as participation defined whose SAT scores were averaged, the comparison is between different populations, and no adjustment within the data can make them the same. Which of these holds is, again, a question about how the data came to exist.

## What the real cases add

The textbook examples of the first article were small and mostly static, and the causal analysis of the second worked with stylised structures. The real cases add three things that neither showed.

They show that reversals are common where a strong stratifying variable differs in distribution between the groups compared: more than a quarter of country pairs in the COVID-19 data, every pair of species in the penguins. In such data, the aggregate comparison is a statement about the mixture as much as about the groups, and analysts who compare countries, schools, hospitals or customer segments on crude rates are comparing their mixtures.

They show that the stratifying variable is often not a confounder in the textbook sense. Age mediates the effect of country, participation selects which scores are observed, playing time responds to the outcome. The second article's warning, that the role of the variable decides the table, is not a theoretical nicety; in three of the four real cases, the variable that produces the reversal is not a common cause of the comparison and the outcome.

And they show that chance produces reversals too, at a rate that depends on sample size and on how many comparisons are made, so that a reversal found by searching many partitions of a data set needs the same scrutiny as any other result found by searching. The final article in this series takes that point into the daily work of data science, where reversals turn up in A/B tests whose traffic mix shifts, in dashboards that aggregate across segments, and in fairness audits that compare groups whose composition differs, and where they can be searched for systematically.
