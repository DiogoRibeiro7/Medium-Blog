# Repeated Play of the Monty Hall Game: Learning Curves, Probability Matching and Models of Why People Stay

## Part 4 of 4. Every game ends by showing whether switching would have won, so experience should settle the question within a few dozen games. The published experiments show people learning far more slowly and levelling off near two-thirds. Simple learning models show which ingredients reproduce that pattern and what each one costs

The first three articles in this series treated the Monty Hall problem as a problem in probability, decision theory and missing data, and in each of them the people who answered one half appeared only as the authors of a mistake. This one is about them. The question is empirical and has two parts. How do people actually decide when they meet the game for the first time? And what happens when they play it again and again, with the car revealed at the end of every game, so that the answer is in front of them as data?

The second part is where the problem becomes interesting for anyone who builds or studies learning systems. The game is an unusually generous teacher. Whatever the player chooses, the last door opened shows where the car was, and therefore whether switching would have won. Each game is a coin toss with a known structure and a hidden bias of two-thirds, and the player sees every toss. A learner that did nothing but count would settle on switching within a few dozen games. The laboratory record shows people doing something quite different: starting far below one half, rising slowly, and in the longest experiments settling close to switching two times in three, a pattern psychologists call probability matching. Pigeons given the same task end up switching almost every time.

What follows sets out the evidence on the first decision, then the evidence on repeated play, then a sequence of learners, each built to test one explanation against the published learning curves. The learners are simple on purpose: a counter, a Bayesian who doubts the game is anything but fifty-fifty, a matching learner whose prior pulls towards staying, reinforcement learners of the kind used in psychology and machine learning, and learners whose memory or regret weighs a loss after switching more heavily than a loss after staying. Each reproduces some features of the human data and fails on others, and the failures say as much as the fits. Every number computed below is printed by a script in the series repository, linked at the end, and every number taken from an experiment is attributed to the paper that reports it.

## The first decision

The one-shot result is among the most replicated findings in the psychology of reasoning, and it has hardly moved in thirty years. Donald Granberg and Thad Brown reported in 1995 that "in the first encounter, only 12% switch" (Granberg and Brown, 1995). Bruce Burns and Mareike Wieth, surveying the experiments available a few years later, counted seven studies with versions of the problem equivalent to the standard one, with switch rates "ranging from 9% to 17%" (Burns and Wieth, 2003). Wim De Neys found that 5.2 per cent of 230 students gave the switching answer (De Neys, 2004). In a study of humans and monkeys published in 2018, Watzek and co-authors gave a paper version of the problem to students who did not already know it, and 32 of 162, 19.8 per cent, chose to switch (Watzek et al., 2018). Andrea Morone and co-authors took the problem out of the university and into a shopping centre in Bari in October 2019, and among the 80 shoppers in their control condition 10 per cent made the switching choice (Morone et al., 2021). Wilcox gave it to 50 online participants without prior familiarity with the problem, and 4 of them, 8 per cent, switched (Wilcox, 2024). Granberg compared students in Brazil, China, Sweden and the United States and found that the four did not differ significantly (Granberg, 1999); a systematic review by Lore Saenen and co-authors summarises that study as showing sticking rates between 79 and 87 per cent across the four cultures (Saenen et al., 2018).

The stability of the number is part of what makes it interesting. Students, shoppers, online workers and four national samples, in experiments spread over three decades, put the share who switch on first meeting somewhere between about one in twenty and one in five. A behaviour that robust is unlikely to be an accident of wording, and the literature has proposed several mechanisms for it, which are not mutually exclusive.

The most direct is the belief that two closed doors must be equally likely, which the earlier articles traced through the letters to *Parade*. Granberg and Brown's participants, and most participants since, say so when asked. The belief by itself does not explain why people stay rather than choose at random between the two doors. If the two doors are equal, staying and switching are equally good, and a person indifferent between them should switch about half the time. The observed rate is far below one half, so something else breaks the tie.

Thomas Gilovich, Victoria Medvec and Serena Chen supplied one candidate in the same year. In their version of the game every participant received a modest prize whatever they chose, and those who had switched assigned it a higher monetary value than those who had stayed (Gilovich, Medvec and Chen, 1995). Their reading is that an error of commission, giving up the door one had and losing, is more painful than an error of omission, keeping the door and losing, and that people who switched and did not win compensated by inflating the value of what they received. If a loss after switching is expected to hurt more than a loss after staying, a person who believes the doors are equal has a reason to stay. The same asymmetry will return later in this article, as a parameter in a learning model.

Burns and Wieth proposed a cognitive account. The host's choice of which door to open depends on two things, the contestant's pick and the location of the car, and in the language of causal graphs it is a collider of the two. Conditioning on a collider makes its causes dependent even when they were independent before, and their argument is that people do not represent this structure, so they do not see that the opened door carries information about the car (Burns and Wieth, 2004). A version of the problem framed as a competition, in which the host's choice is visibly the result of a contest, raised switching in their experiments to 42 per cent, against 14 per cent for the standard doors, among 124 participants (Burns and Wieth, 2003).

De Neys added a capacity argument. With a secondary task occupying working memory, the switching rate among 41 participants fell to zero (De Neys, 2004). Whatever the correct analysis requires, it requires effort, and people whose attention is taken elsewhere fall back on the default.

The default is the important word. For a decision made once, under a belief that the options are equal, sticking with the first choice is what a status-quo preference, an aversion to the regret of commission and a lack of spare cognitive capacity all predict. None of these mechanisms depends on experience. The question for the rest of the article is what happens to them when the player gets data.

## What one game teaches

When the game ends, the doors are opened. Whatever the player chose, the player now knows whether switching would have won. This is the feature that separates the Monty Hall game from most learning tasks studied in psychology and machine learning. In a slot machine, or a multi-armed bandit, the player observes only the payoff of the arm that was pulled. Here the player sees the counterfactual too, because the outcome of the other option is determined by the outcome of the chosen one: if staying lost, switching would have won, and the reverse. Each game is therefore a Bernoulli trial for the event "switching wins", with probability two-thirds, observed in full regardless of what the player did.

Consider a learner that ignores the reasoning entirely and learns from outcomes alone. Its rule is to switch in the next game if switching has won more of the past games than staying, and to toss a coin when they are tied, including in the first game. After one game it switches with probability 0.667, because switching won that game with probability two-thirds. After 5 games it switches with probability 0.790, after 10 with probability 0.855, after 20 with probability 0.935 and after 50 with probability 0.992; after 100 games it is certain, to three decimal places. The learner makes mistakes early, because short runs can mislead: the probability that switching wins at most 5 of the first 10 games is 0.213, and at most 10 of 20 is 0.092. But the probability of being misled falls fast, to 0.011 for at most 25 of 50.

The cost of learning this way is small. Over 10 games the counting learner expects to win 5.8 cars, against 6.7 for a player who always switches and 3.3 for a player who always stays. Over 50 games the figures are 31.9, 33.3 and 16.7, and over 200 games 131.8, 133.3 and 66.7. A player who knew nothing about probability and simply kept score would, within a few dozen games, be playing almost as well as one who had read the solution. ## Belief and decision are learned at different speeds

A natural objection is that people do not start from a blank slate. They start from a firm belief that the two doors are equally likely, and evidence against a firm belief takes time to accumulate. That is true of belief, and the right panel of Figure 1 shows how much time. But it turns out not to be true of the decision, and the difference is worth working through, because it rules out one of the most intuitive explanations for slow learning.

Model a sceptical learner who assigns probability π to the hypothesis that the game is exactly fifty-fifty and spreads the rest of its belief uniformly over all other values of the probability that switching wins. After n games in which switching would have won k times, the evidence for the fifty-fifty hypothesis against the alternative is a Bayes factor that grows slowly with n. The expected log-likelihood ratio of the true value, two-thirds, against one half is 0.0566 nats per game, so an expected Bayes factor of 10 in favour of the truth over fifty-fifty takes about 41 games, a factor of 20 about 53 games and a factor of 100 about 81. Against the opposite hypothesis, that staying is the better choice with probability two-thirds, the evidence accumulates four times faster, at 0.2310 nats per game, and the same three factors take 10, 13 and 20 games. The game is far better at telling people that staying is not better than at telling them that it is not a tie.

For a learner with π = 0.5, the share whose posterior probability that the game is not fifty-fifty exceeds 0.95 is 0.02 after 10 games, 0.06 after 20, 0.26 after 50 and 0.60 after 100. Half of such learners are convinced after 85 games, and nine in ten after 175. A stronger prior delays conviction further. With π = 0.9, half are convinced after 127 games; with π = 0.99, after 172, and nine in ten only after 291. A person who walks in sure that the game is a tie can play it for an afternoon and remain unsure.

The decision is a different matter. The sceptical learner's predictive probability that switching wins the next game is a weighted average of one half, from the fifty-fifty hypothesis, and (k + 1)/(n + 2), from the alternative. That average exceeds one half exactly when (k + 1)/(n + 2) exceeds one half, which is exactly when k > n/2, whatever the weight π. The script checks this on every number of games from 1 to 59 and every value of π used here. A Bayesian who acts on its predictive probability switches as soon as switching has won more often than not, which is the counting rule. Its prior affects how confident it is in the decision, not which decision it makes, because a hypothesis that predicts a tie cannot tip a decision either way.

The consequence for the human data is direct. A strong belief in equal probabilities, however firmly held, cannot by itself explain people who keep staying after switching has won most of the games they have seen. A decision rule that is consistent with any such belief would have them switching. To produce the slow learning observed in the laboratory, something has to break the symmetry between the two choices: a pull towards staying that is not a belief about the probabilities, a way of learning from outcomes that is not the Bayesian one, or a way of choosing that does not simply pick the better option. The experiments with repeated play allow each of these to be examined.

## The published learning curves

The first study of repeated play was the one Granberg and Brown reported in 1995. In the version with 50 computer-simulated games, switching rose, in the words of their abstract, to 55 per cent "across trials"; Petrocelli and Harris, describing the same experiment, report that switching reached approximately 50 per cent in the final block of 10 games (Granberg and Brown, 1995; Petrocelli and Harris, 2011). In a control version based on roulette, switching began low and did not decrease across trials. Granberg and Brown also tried changing the payoffs, and Petrocelli and Harris report the results: with 1 point for a win after staying and 2 points for a win after switching, participants switched in 63 per cent of the final 10 games, and with 1 point and 4 points in 85 per cent (Petrocelli and Harris, 2011).

Petrocelli and Harris ran the most informative studies for the present purpose, because they report enough statistics to fit a model to. In their first study, 47 students played 60 games with win or lose feedback after each one and a chance in a prize draw that depended on their performance. Switching rose from a mean of 0.21 in the first block of 10 games (standard deviation across participants 0.22) to 0.38 in the last block of 10 (0.26). Over all 60 games participants switched 19.96 times on average, a third of the time. The authors' summary is blunt: "participants generally failed to learn that the switch decision is twice as successful as the stick decision after 60 trials." Their second study, with 65 participants and the same number of games, found 2.57 switches in the first 10 games and 4.88 in the last 10, and 25.08 switches out of 60 overall. It also asked participants afterwards how often each kind of decision had lost, and found that their "memory for switch losses was significantly overestimated" (Petrocelli and Harris, 2011).

Herbranson and Schroeder ran a comparison that removes the cover story altogether (Herbranson and Schroeder, 2010). Their human participants, six in each condition, played 200 games in four blocks of 50 on a computer that never mentioned doors, hosts or cars and avoided the words "stay" and "switch"; the only feedback was "win" or "lose". In the condition in which switching paid off two-thirds of the time, participants switched on 56.67 per cent of games in the first block and 65.67 per cent in the last, with 95 per cent intervals of 34.51 to 78.83 and 45.04 to 86.29. In the mirror condition, in which staying paid off two-thirds of the time, they switched on 30.00 per cent and 27.67 per cent. The same paper reports six pigeons trained on an analogous task for 30 days, with up to 100 trials a day from the fifth day on. They switched on 36.33 per cent of trials on day 1 and 96.33 per cent on day 30 (interval 93.97 to 98.70), and the share of trials on which they were rewarded rose from 47.00 to 63.83 per cent. Pigeons in the mirror condition fell from 30.17 per cent switching on day 1 to 4.33 per cent by day 15. The authors' summary of the human result is that "the probability of switching quickly approaches and hovers near the matching value", and William Herbranson's later review put it as a contrast: pigeons "maximize their wins by switching on virtually all trials", whereas humans "utilize a suboptimal strategy involving probability matching" (Herbranson, 2012).

Other studies fill in the picture without changing it. Klein and co-authors tested 15 students and 7 rhesus monkeys over up to 500 games; on the first game 10 of the 15 students stayed, after 100 games 5 of the 15 students and 3 of the 7 monkeys showed a statistically significant preference for switching, and by the end of testing about half of each species did (Klein et al., 2013). Mazur and Kahlbaugh compared pigeons, college students and preschool children and found that "in all three groups, some individuals approximated the optimal strategy of switching on every trial, but most did not", although when switching won 90 per cent of the time, "all college students and all but one pigeon learned to switch on nearly every trial" (Mazur and Kahlbaugh, 2012). In Watzek and co-authors' study, 100 students played up to 500 games against three or eight doors, and humans and both species of monkey "quickly and consistently switched doors, especially in the eight-door condition" (Watzek et al., 2018). Pigeons do not always maximise either: in a study by Thomas Zentall and co-authors, 14 pigeons switched on 46.2 per cent of trials over their first five sessions and 73.6 per cent over their last five, after 60 sessions of 96 trials (Zentall, Case and Collins, 2015).

Experiments run by economists, with money paid for every car, tend to show higher rates. Wei James Chen and Joseph Tao-yi Wang paid 40 students NT$10 for each win over 30 rounds; in their control group, which played the standard three-door game throughout, switching started at 60 per cent and converged to around 80 per cent, and over the second half of the session it was 77 per cent (Chen and Wang, 2020). Morone and Fiore, with 20 participants per treatment and 10 paid rounds, report an overall switch rate of 41.5 per cent in the standard version (Morone and Fiore, 2008).

The systematic review by Saenen and co-authors, which covered 26 articles, summarises the whole literature in one sentence: "despite repeated experience, participants failed to learn the absolute and unqualified advantage of switching" (Saenen et al., 2018). Two further findings sharpen that conclusion. Tubau and Alonso found that accumulated experience increased switching but did not change the erroneous intuition (Tubau and Alonso, 2003), and Ana Franco-Watkins, Peter Derks and Michael Dougherty found that experience with a card version of the game affected choices but not participants' understanding of the probabilities (Franco-Watkins, Derks and Dougherty, 2003). People who learn to switch more often do not necessarily learn why.

Set against the counting learner, the gap is large. Given the same 50 to 60 games as the participants of Granberg and Brown or Petrocelli and Harris, the counting learner switches with probability 0.989 in games 41 to 50 and 0.994 in games 51 to 60. Given the 200 games of Herbranson and Schroeder, it switches with probability 1.000 in the final block. The human figures in those blocks are about 0.50, 0.38 and 0.66. Figure 2 puts the published curves and the counting learner on the same axes, with the pigeons and humans of Herbranson and Schroeder side by side on the right.

The information was there. People had it and did not act on it the way a counter would. The rest of the article looks for the learner that does what they did.

## Matching

Probability matching is one of the oldest findings in the experimental study of choice. When people are asked to predict which of two events will occur, and one occurs with probability p, they tend, after some experience, to predict it on about a share p of trials rather than on every trial, which would maximise the number of correct predictions (Vulkan, 2000). Herbranson and Schroeder's human data look exactly like this. When switching paid off two-thirds of the time, their participants switched on about two-thirds of the games; when it paid off one-third of the time, they switched on about one-third.

The cost of matching is easy to compute. A player who switches with probability s wins with probability 1/3 + s/3. Always staying wins 0.3333 of the time, switching half the time 0.5000, matching at two-thirds 0.5556, switching nine times in ten 0.6333 and always switching 0.6667. Over 200 games, matching wins 111.1 cars on average against 133.3 for always switching and 66.7 for always staying. Matching recovers two-thirds of the gain available from switching and leaves a third on the table.

A matching learner can be written down as precisely as the counting learner. It keeps an estimate of the probability that switching wins, starting from a prior with mean p₀ worth m games of experience, updates it with the outcome of every game, and switches with probability equal to its current estimate. After t games in which switching would have won k times, it switches with probability (p₀m + k)/(m + t). Its expected switch probability in a game preceded by t games is (p₀m + 2t/3)/(m + t), which starts at p₀ and approaches two-thirds. With a flat prior worth two games, starting at one half, the matching learner's switch rate is 0.637 by game 10, 0.660 by game 50 and 0.665 by game 200, and it never goes higher. The right-hand curve in the left panel of Figure 1 is this learner.

Fitted to Herbranson and Schroeder's four block means by least squares, a single matching learner reproduces both conditions. It needs a prior worth only 3.3 games, so its starting point hardly matters, and it predicts switch rates of 0.565 and 0.657 for the first and last blocks of the condition in which switching pays, against the observed 0.567 and 0.657, and 0.297 and 0.330 for the condition in which staying pays, against 0.300 and 0.277. All four predictions lie well inside the reported intervals. The counting learner, by contrast, predicts final-block switch rates of 1.000 and 0.000, outside both intervals. The same intervals exclude matching for the pigeons: matching predicts 0.667, and the pigeons' day-30 interval runs from 0.940 to 0.987.

Six participants per condition is a small sample, and the intervals are wide. What the data can do is distinguish between the two extremes, and they do: the humans' final switch rate is consistent with matching and inconsistent with maximising, and the pigeons' is the reverse. The same split appears, less cleanly, across the other studies. The final-block rates of Granberg and Brown's incentive conditions, 63 and 85 per cent, and the 77 to 80 per cent of Chen and Wang's paid participants, show that matching is not a law. When the payoff for a win after switching was quadrupled, people switched more than two-thirds of the time, which a learner that matches frequencies alone would not do. Matching is a good description of what people converge to when every win is worth the same, and a poor description of how sensitive they are to the size of the payoff.

## A prior worth dozens of games

Herbranson and Schroeder's participants switched on 57 per cent of their first 50 games, close to indifference, and their task had no story: they did not know they were choosing between keeping a door and abandoning it. The participants of Granberg and Brown and of Petrocelli and Harris played the game with doors, a host and a car, and they started near the one-shot rates, between 10 and 25 per cent. The matching learner can absorb that difference in one place, its prior. A learner that starts with a low estimate of the probability that switching wins, and holds it as firmly as if it had been earned from many games of experience, will rise towards two-thirds slowly.

For Petrocelli and Harris's two studies, the two parameters of the matching learner can be solved exactly from the first and last blocks of 10 games. For the first study, a starting switch probability p₀ = 0.185 and a prior worth m = 80 games reproduce 0.21 and 0.38. For the second, p₀ = 0.206 and m = 34 games reproduce 0.257 and 0.488. The block means are averages over a few dozen people, and the script propagates their uncertainty by drawing them from normal distributions with the reported standard deviations divided by the square root of the sample size. The draws are independent, because the correlation between a person's first and last blocks is not reported, which probably makes the intervals somewhat wide. The 95 per cent interval for the prior's weight runs from 37 to 228 games in the first study and from 14 to 78 in the second.

The fit uses two numbers and has two parameters, so it is not a test. The overall switch rate across all 60 games, which the papers also report and which was not used in fitting, is a test. The matching learner calibrated to the first study predicts an overall rate of 0.305, with a 95 per cent interval of 0.254 to 0.361; the observed rate is 0.333, with a standard error of 0.027. For the second study the prediction is 0.397 (0.345 to 0.457) and the observation 0.418 (0.026). Both observations fall inside the predicted intervals. The matching learner gets the shape of the curve between the first and last blocks right, which is more than a straight line between the two blocks would guarantee, since the overall rate depends on how quickly the rise happens.

For Granberg and Brown the inputs are rougher. The abstract gives the first-game rate of 12 per cent, and the final block of 10 is about 50 per cent according to Petrocelli and Harris; the sample size is not available, so no interval can be computed. A matching learner with p₀ = 0.12 needs a prior worth 19 games to reach 50 per cent in games 41 to 50, or 12 games to reach 55 per cent.

Read as a single number, the weight of the prior is the size of the pull towards staying. In these terms, the cover story of doors, a host and a car is worth between about a dozen and about eighty games of contrary experience, against about three games for Herbranson and Schroeder's participants, who had no story to begin with. The interpretation is the model's, not the participants', and it should be stated as a conjecture: the prior's weight is a summary of everything that pulls people towards staying, including the belief in equal probabilities, the regret of commission and the plain inertia of a first choice, and the matching learner does not separate these. What it does is put them on a common scale, in units of experience, and that scale makes the slow learning look less mysterious. A person who comes to the game with the equivalent of eighty games of belief that staying is better will not be persuaded by sixty.

Figure 3 draws the three calibrated learners beyond the end of the experiments. The extrapolation is the model's prediction, not data, and it assumes that nothing changes after the last game observed, which is the kind of assumption that longer experiments tend to break. Taken at face value, the learner calibrated to Petrocelli and Harris's first study would switch with probability 0.451 in game 100, 0.529 in game 200, 0.600 in game 500 and 0.631 in game 1,000. It would cross one half after 151 games of experience and reach 0.6 only after 498. The learner calibrated to the second study would cross one half after 61 games and reach 0.6 after 204. All of them approach two-thirds and none goes beyond it.

The cost of the pull towards staying can be counted in cars. At the overall switch rate observed in Petrocelli and Harris's first study, a player expects 26.7 cars in 60 games, against 40 for always switching and 20 for always staying; in the second study, 28.4. Two-thirds of the possible gain from switching is lost while the evidence accumulates, and even at its plateau the matching learner would still give up a third of it.

## Learners built from reinforcement

The matching learner describes the data compactly but says nothing about mechanism. A learner built from reinforcement does, and it is the kind of model psychologists have fitted to choice data for decades and that machine learning uses in a more elaborate form. It keeps a value for each option, updates the value after each outcome by a fraction α of the prediction error (the delta rule), and chooses between the options with a logistic, or softmax, rule whose sharpness is set by an inverse temperature β (Sutton and Barto, 2018). Here the probability of switching is the logistic function of β times the difference between the two values, minus a constant bias b towards staying. Both values start at one half. A bias of ln 9 gives a first-game switch rate of 10 per cent, close to the one-shot evidence.

Two versions of the learner differ in what they learn from. One learns only from its own outcome: if it stayed, it updates the value of staying with what staying earned, and leaves the value of switching untouched. The other also learns from the door not chosen, updating both values after every game with what each would have earned, as the revealed car allows. In machine-learning terms, the first faces a bandit problem and the second a problem with full information.

With α = 0.1, β = 10 and b = ln 9, the learner that uses the door not chosen crosses one half in game 11 and reaches 0.628 by game 25 and 0.664 by game 50, where it stays. The learner that uses only its own outcome is slower: it crosses one half in game 23 and reaches 0.517 by game 25 and 0.647 by game 50. But it keeps rising, to 0.708 by game 100 and 0.730 by game 200, and ends higher than the full-information learner. If both values settled at their expected payoffs, two-thirds for switching and one-third for staying, the switch rate would be 0.757. Neither learner reaches it. The full-information learner, because both values move on every game, in opposite directions, so their difference is noisy, and a logistic choice rule evaluated at a noisy difference produces a lower average switch rate than the same rule evaluated at the settled difference. The own-outcome learner updates the value of staying only when it stays, which becomes rare, so that value moves less and the difference is steadier. The left panel of Figure 4 draws both curves.

The learning rate matters in a way that resembles the human data. A learner that weighs recent games heavily chases runs of outcomes: three games in a row in which switching would have lost, which happens once in 27 runs of three games on average, push the value of switching down far enough to send it back to staying for a while. With α = 0.3, the full-information learner settles at 0.615 within about 10 games, and the own-outcome learner at 0.623. With α = 0.03, the full-information learner rises slowly, crossing one half in game 36 and reaching 0.710 by game 200, while the own-outcome learner takes until game 79 to cross one half and reaches 0.704 by game 200. A learner that remembers more learns more slowly but levels off higher, and a learner that reacts to every run levels off well below the settled value. The right panel of Figure 4 shows the effect for the full-information learner.

Learning only from one's own outcome has a second consequence that averages hide. Jerker Denrell and James March described the hot-stove effect: a learner that has had a bad experience with an option tends to avoid it, and by avoiding it never collects the evidence that would correct the bad impression (Denrell and March, 2001). In the Monty Hall game, the effect is weaker than in many settings, because the option people default to, staying, is the bad one, and every game spent staying teaches that staying loses. It is not absent. With α = 0.3, 17.2 per cent of own-outcome learners switch less than a third of the time in games 151 to 200, against 1.2 per cent of learners that also learn from the door not chosen. A player who discounts what the revealed car says about the choice not made is much more likely to end up stuck.

Two features of the reinforcement learner's behaviour bear on the human data. The first is that a plateau near two-thirds is easy to produce. With b = 0, a settled learner switches two-thirds of the time when β = 3 ln 2 ≈ 2.079; with the bias of ln 9, when β ≈ 8.671; with β = 10 and α = 0.1, noise brings the plateau to 0.664. The second is that nothing in the learner makes two-thirds special. The plateau depends on β, b and α, and the same learner with β = 15 settles at 0.776 with full information and is still rising, at 0.888, after 200 games with own outcomes only. The matching learner ends at two-thirds because that is the frequency it matches; the reinforcement learner ends there only if its parameters happen to put it there. Herbranson and Schroeder's mirror condition, in which humans also matched at one-third, is evidence for the first kind of learner over the second, at least for those participants. It is also consistent with the pigeons being better described by a reinforcement learner with a sharp choice rule, which maximises once its values have settled.

## Regret and memory

Gilovich and his co-authors' asymmetry between commission and omission, and Petrocelli and Harris's finding that people overestimate how often switching lost, can both be added to the learners as a single extra parameter, and both give a precise answer to the question of how strong the asymmetry would have to be to explain what is observed.

Start with regret. Suppose a loss after switching costs the player κ, measured in cars, while a loss after staying costs nothing beyond the car not won. The expected payoff of switching is then 2/3 − κ/3 and that of staying 1/3, and staying becomes the better choice when κ > 1: a loss after switching would have to hurt as much as winning a car pleases. For a reinforcement learner with no bias and β = 10, the settled switch rate is 0.966 with no regret, 0.841 with κ = 0.5, 0.500 with κ = 1, 0.159 with κ = 1.5 and 0.034 with κ = 2. With the bias of ln 9, the same values of κ give 0.757, 0.370, 0.100, 0.021 and 0.004. The right panel of Figure 5 shows how the answer depends on the sharpness of choice: a sharp chooser flips from switching to staying around κ = 1, and a noisy one drifts more gradually.

![Figure 5](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/monty-hall/part4/figures/fig5_asymmetry.png)

*Figure 5. Left: the long-run switch rate of a matching learner that remembers each loss after switching as w losses. Right: the settled switch rate of a softmax learner with no bias, for three values of choice sharpness, when a loss after switching costs κ cars of regret.*

Then memory. A matching learner that remembers every loss after switching as w losses, and every other outcome correctly, settles at a switch rate s that solves a quadratic, (w − 1)s² + 3s − 2 = 0. With accurate memory, w = 1, s is two-thirds. With w = 1.5 it is 0.606, with w = 2 it is 0.562, with w = 3 exactly one half and with w = 4 it is 0.457, and the corresponding probabilities of winning are 0.535, 0.521, 0.500 and 0.486. A memory that doubles the losses after switching costs the matching learner another 3.5 percentage points of wins on top of what matching already costs; one that quadruples them leaves it worse off than a player who tosses a coin.

These calculations do not show that regret or memory explains the human data, because the experiments do not measure κ or w. Petrocelli and Harris measured memory and found the distortion in the direction the model needs, but their reported means do not give the actual number of switch losses to compare with the recalled one. What the calculations show is the scale. A regret large enough to make staying optimal requires a loss after switching to feel as bad as a car feels good, which is large. A memory distortion that holds a matching learner down near one half requires losses after switching to be remembered about three times over. Smaller asymmetries, which seem plausible, lower the plateau by a few points without changing its character. A conjecture consistent with these numbers is that regret and memory explain part of the gap between matching and the lower plateaus seen in some studies, such as the 38 per cent final block of Petrocelli and Harris's first study, but not the gap between matching and maximising.

## What raises switching

The studies that tried to help people learn are, in model terms, studies that change one ingredient at a time, and they can be sorted by which ingredient they touch.

Some change the prior, that is, the pull towards staying. Framing the problem as a competition raised first-decision switching from 14 to 42 per cent in Burns and Wieth's study (Burns and Wieth, 2003). Wilcox's training in mental simulation, in which participants were taught to imagine the outcomes of the game case by case, raised switching from 8 to 52 per cent in a one-shot test (Wilcox, 2024). In the field experiment of Morone and co-authors, a group of experts chose the switching option 67 per cent of the time (Morone et al., 2021). Chen and Wang's participants who first played a version with 100 doors, where the advantage of switching is obvious, switched 86 per cent of the time when they returned to three doors, against 77 per cent for those who had played three doors throughout (Chen and Wang, 2020). James, Friedman and co-authors, in a study of 77 participants making 30 decisions each, found that merging the unchosen doors automatically, so that switching visibly meant taking two doors instead of one, raised the share of rational choices "from around 40% to over 80%" (James et al., 2018).

Some change the feedback. Saenen and co-authors found, in an experiment with cards, that people learned to switch most when they received feedback in the form of conditional frequencies, but that their understanding of the problem did not improve (Saenen, Van Dooren and Onghena, 2015). Petrocelli and Harris manipulated how salient the counterfactual outcome was, and found that in the final 10 games participants in the low-salience condition switched 5.59 times on average and those in the high-salience condition 4.10 times (Petrocelli and Harris, 2011). The direction is the opposite of what the reinforcement learner would suggest, since making the outcome of the door not chosen more salient should help a learner that uses it. In Petrocelli and Harris's account, the counterfactual thoughts that follow a loss after switching ("if only I had stayed") are the ones that inhibit learning, which is the memory asymmetry of the previous section in another form.

Some change the choice rule, by changing who chooses or what the choice is worth. Granberg and Brown's increased payoffs for wins after switching raised the final-block switch rate to 63 and 85 per cent (as reported by Petrocelli and Harris, 2011). Tilman Slembeck and Jean-Robert Tyran found that communication within small groups combined with competition between them could eliminate the anomaly entirely (Slembeck and Tyran, 2004), and Boris Maciejovsky and co-authors found that people who had played in teams learned the solution faster and carried the improvement into their individual decisions up to five weeks later (Maciejovsky et al., 2013).

The pattern across these interventions is that each moves the switch rate, and that the ones that move it most either make the right answer visible at a glance (100 doors, merged doors) or make the consequences of the choice large and explicit (quadrupled payoffs, competition between teams). That is what the learners predict. A change in the prior shortens the time to the plateau without moving the plateau; a change in payoffs or in the sharpness of choice moves the plateau itself. A conjecture consistent with the review's conclusion is that most interventions in the literature affect the first and few affect the second, which would explain why so many studies report learning that stops short of the optimum.

## How much experience?

The question this article set out to answer has several answers, depending on what is meant by learning.

To learn the decision from outcomes alone, a player who keeps score needs very little: after 10 games such a player switches with probability 0.855, after 20 with 0.935 and after 50 with 0.992. A Bayesian who doubts the game is anything but a tie makes exactly the same decisions, whatever the strength of the doubt.

To be convinced, the same Bayesian needs much more: 85 games for half of such learners to be 95 per cent sure the game is not a tie when they start at even odds, 127 games when they start 90 per cent sure it is a tie, and 172 games when they start 99 per cent sure. The gap between the decision and the conviction is one reason the experimental literature finds people switching more often without understanding why: the evidence to act on arrives long before the evidence to believe.

To learn as people actually learn, the published curves and the matching learner calibrated to them suggest something like this: a switch rate that starts between 10 and 25 per cent when the game is told as a story, crosses one half somewhere between about 60 and 150 games of experience, and levels off near two-thirds, with the long tail an extrapolation that the experiments did not reach. Without the story, as in Herbranson and Schroeder's task, the start is near one half and the plateau is reached within the first block of 50 games. With large and explicit payoffs, or with the problem reframed so that the answer is visible, the plateau can be higher, around 80 per cent.

To learn as Herbranson and Schroeder's pigeons learned, finally, takes about a month of daily sessions, and ends at 96 per cent; in Zentall and co-authors' procedure, 60 sessions brought pigeons to 74 per cent.

The contrast between the last two is the most thought-provoking result in this literature, and the learners suggest a reason for it, which is a conjecture rather than a finding. Pigeons behave like reinforcement learners with a sharp choice rule and no story: their values settle, and their choices follow the larger value. Humans in these experiments behave like learners who are estimating a probability and choosing in proportion to it, a strategy that is optimal in some environments, such as ones that change over time or reward exploration, and costly in a stationary game like this one. A human who matches is hedging against the possibility that the game is not what it seems. In a game whose rules, as the earlier articles in this series showed, really can turn the advice around, that hedge is not foolish; it is expensive only because in the laboratory the rules never change.

## What the series adds up to

Across four articles, the Monty Hall problem has been taken apart in four directions. The history showed how much the famous experiments and arguments actually established, and how much evidence they carried. The protocol showed that the answer is a property of the host's rules, and that only one of those rules, the policy that decides when the switch is offered, can reverse the decision. The missing-data reading showed that the one-half answer is the ignorable analysis of a mechanism that is not ignorable, and that the same mistake changes which model an offline evaluation picks. This article has shown that people given the data in the most generous possible form, the full outcome of every game, learn the decision slowly and incompletely, and that the slowness is better explained by how they choose and what they bring to the game than by any shortage of evidence.

The thread through all four is that the answer depends on the process that produced the data, and that the process is easy to leave out. The readers of *Parade* left out the host's rule. The offline evaluation leaves out the user's choice of what to rate. And the players in the laboratory, if the matching account is right, leave out the possibility that the game is exactly what it is described to be, and hedge against a host who never comes.
