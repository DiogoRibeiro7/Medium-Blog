# The Monty Hall Problem, 1889–2026: A History With the Calculations Redone

## Part 1 of 4. What was actually published, what was actually computed, and how much evidence the famous experiments carried

Steve Selvin died in Oakland on 30 September 2026, at the age of 85. He was an emeritus professor of biostatistics at Berkeley, the author of more than two hundred and fifty papers on cancer incidence, birth defects, twins and disease clustering, and, by his school's account, a teacher whose eight o'clock introductory course was legendary among students (UC Berkeley School of Public Health, 2026). Outside his field he is known for two short letters to the editor of *The American Statistician* written in 1975, the second of which gave a probability puzzle the name it still carries. Monty Hall, the game-show host after whom Selvin named it, died in Beverly Hills on 30 September 2017. The man who named the problem and the man it was named after died on the same day of the year, nine years apart.

The problem is usually told as a single episode. A reader of *Parade* magazine asked Marilyn vos Savant about three doors, a car and two goats in September 1990, she said the contestant should switch, about ten thousand people wrote in, most of them to tell her she was wrong, and the mathematics proved her right. Every element of that version is true, and the version is still a poor guide to what happened, because it compresses three different things into one number. There is the number itself, the two-thirds probability that switching wins. There is the model that produces the number, a set of rules about what the host knows and does, without which no number can be computed at all. And there is the evidence, the classroom experiments, the dining-room trials and the computer simulations that people used to convince themselves, which varied enormously in how much they could actually prove. Different people in the story were right or wrong about different layers, and the long quarrel that followed the *Parade* columns was in large part a quarrel between people who were arguing about different layers without noticing.

This is the first of four articles that take the layers apart. This one is the history, told in order and with the calculations redone the way the participants would have done them, plus two questions the usual retelling rarely asks: how much evidence Monty Hall's own famous experiment actually contained, and how large a classroom experiment has to be before it can tell two-thirds from one-half reliably. The second part treats the host's rules as a family of statistical models and asks what each one implies. The third reads the host as a selection mechanism, in the sense of the missing-data literature, and follows the same structure into logged data and observational studies. The fourth looks at the laboratory evidence on why people refuse to switch, and at how much experience a learner needs before switching stops feeling wrong. Every number below is printed by a script in the series repository, linked at the end.

## The problem, stated so that it has an answer

A contestant faces three closed doors. One hides a car and two hide goats. The contestant points at a door, which we will call door 1, and it stays closed. The host then opens one of the two other doors and shows a goat behind it; call it door 3. The contestant may now keep door 1 or switch to door 2. Which choice wins more often?

That paragraph is the version most people remember, and as written it has no answer. The answer depends on why the host opened door 3, and the story only says that he did. The version under which switching wins with probability two-thirds needs four assumptions, and it is worth stating all of them at the start because the history that follows is largely a history of people discovering, one at a time, that each assumption was doing work. The car is placed behind each door with equal probability, independently of the contestant's choice. The host knows where the car is, and he always opens a door other than the contestant's that hides a goat. He always offers the switch, whatever the contestant chose. And when the contestant's door hides the car, so that the host has two goat doors to choose from, he picks one of them at random with equal probability.

Under those four rules the calculation is short. The contestant's door hides the car with probability 1/3, and nothing the host does can change what is behind it. If the car is behind door 1, switching loses. If it is behind either other door, the host is forced to open the only other goat door, and the remaining closed door is the car, so switching wins. Switching therefore wins exactly when the first choice was wrong, which happens with probability 2/3. Vos Savant's original answer in September 1990 made the same point with a larger number of doors. She asked readers to imagine a million doors, of which the contestant picks one and the host, who knows where the prize is and always avoids it, opens every other door except one, door number 777,777. Few people would keep their original door in that situation, and her claim was that the three-door game is the same game at a smaller scale. As the second half of this article shows, the million-door version is persuasive for a reason slightly different from the one usually given.

The first three assumptions decide whether switching wins two-thirds of the time on average across all games. The fourth, the host's coin toss when he has a choice, does not affect that average at all, but it does affect the probability that switching wins in the particular game where the contestant has just watched door 3 open. That distinction, between the probability that a strategy wins and the probability that a move wins given what has just been seen, is exactly where the professional statisticians took up the problem in 1991, and it is the subject of Part 2.

## Bertrand's boxes, 1889

The structure of the problem appears in print a century before *Parade*, in Joseph Bertrand's *Calcul des probabilités* of 1889. Bertrand described three boxes that look identical, each with two drawers and a coin in each drawer. One box holds two gold coins, one holds two silver coins and one holds a gold coin and a silver coin. A box is chosen at random, so the probability that it is the mixed one is 1/3. Now open one of its drawers. In the form in which the puzzle is usually quoted, a gold coin is found, and the question is the probability that the other drawer of the same box also holds gold.

The tempting argument runs as follows. The box cannot be the silver-silver one, so it is either gold-gold or gold-silver, and since the box was chosen at random the two remaining possibilities are equally likely, which makes the answer 1/2. Bertrand set out that argument himself, as a way in which opening a drawer would seem to change the probability of the mixed box from 1/3 to 1/2 whatever coin appeared, and showed that it is wrong with a large population, as vos Savant would with a large number of doors a century later. Take three hundred boxes, a hundred of each kind, and open one drawer in each. Fewer than two hundred gold coins can be expected to appear, so the first gold coin one meets is more likely than not to come from a box whose other coin is also gold, which is as far as Bertrand took the count (Bertrand, 1889; an English translation of the passage, with discussion, is in Bayesian Spectacles, n.d.). Finishing it gives the exact answer. About a hundred and fifty gold coins appear, and a hundred of them come from gold-gold boxes, because every drawer of a gold-gold box shows gold, while only half of the gold-silver boxes happen to show their gold coin. The probability that the other coin is gold is therefore 2/3.

The arithmetic is the same as the arithmetic of the doors, and seeing why makes the later history easier to follow. Before the drawer is opened, gold-gold and gold-silver are equally likely. The observation "a gold coin appeared" is twice as likely under gold-gold, where it happens every time, as under gold-silver, where it happens half the time. In the odds form of Bayes' theorem, the posterior odds are the prior odds multiplied by the ratio of those two likelihoods, so even odds become 2:1, a probability of 2/3. The silver-silver box, which the observation rules out completely, contributes nothing to the answer. The mistake in the tempting argument is not that it forgets to rule out the impossible box. It rules it out correctly, and then treats the two survivors as equally likely when the observation that eliminated the third also favoured one of the survivors over the other.

Bertrand's own purpose was a warning about probabilities assigned by symmetry alone, a theme that runs through his book, and his box problem is the oldest member of a family that keeps being rediscovered. In each member an observation rules out one of three equally likely possibilities, and the observation is twice as likely under one of the survivors as under the other.

## Three prisoners and a warden, 1959

Martin Gardner put the next member of the family in his *Mathematical Games* column in *Scientific American* in October 1959 (Gardner, 1959a), and saved the answer for the November issue (Gardner, 1959b). Three prisoners, A, B and C, are sentenced to death, and the governor has chosen one of them at random to be pardoned. The warden knows which one but will not say. Prisoner A asks the warden to tell him the name of one of the other two who will be executed, on the grounds that at least one of them will be and naming him gives nothing away. The warden says B. Prisoner A reasons that his own chance of being pardoned has risen from 1/3 to 1/2, since only he and C remain. He tells C, who reasons the same way about himself.

Prisoner A's reasoning is the tempting argument of Bertrand's boxes in a new costume. If A is the one pardoned, the warden could have named either B or C and, assuming he chooses at random, names B half the time. If C is pardoned, the warden must name B. The observation "the warden named B" is therefore twice as likely when C is pardoned as when A is, and A's probability stays at 1/3 while C's rises to 2/3. Exact enumeration in the repository gives exactly those two numbers. Gardner introduced the problem as a wonderfully confusing little one, and when John Tierney of *The New York Times* asked him about vos Savant's column in 1991, Tierney's article recalled Gardner's warning from 1959 that "in no other branch of mathematics is it so easy for experts to blunder" as in probability (Tierney, 1991).

Two later uses of the prisoners matter for this series. Frederick Mosteller included a version in his 1965 collection *Fifty Challenging Problems in Probability*, which put it in front of a generation of statistics students. And in 1988 Judea Pearl used a variant in *Probabilistic Reasoning in Intelligent Systems*, one of the founding books of probabilistic artificial intelligence, to show that a reasoning system cannot update its beliefs on a reported fact alone. It also has to represent the query or procedure that produced the report, because the same sentence from the warden supports different conclusions depending on how the warden chooses what to say (Pearl, 1988). That is the observation on which Part 3 of this series is built.

## Selvin's letters, 1975

The problem took its game-show form in February 1975, in a letter Steve Selvin, then an assistant professor of biostatistics at Berkeley, sent to *The American Statistician* under the title "A Problem in Probability" (Selvin, 1975a). It is written as a short comic dialogue set on *Let's Make a Deal*, with the host's name altered to Monte Hall. One of three boxes labelled A, B and C holds the keys to a new 1975 Lincoln Continental. The contestant takes box B. The host tries to buy it back, offering $100, then $200, then $500, and reminding him that his box holds the keys with probability 1/3. He refuses. The host then opens box A, which is empty, announces that the probability of the contestant's box has now risen to 1/2, and offers $1,000 for it. The contestant asks instead to trade box B for box C, the host calls it weird, and the dialogue ends with one line addressed to the reader: "HINT: The contestant knows what he is doing!"

Selvin's answer, that the trade wins with probability 2/3, drew objections from readers who thought the two remaining boxes must be equally likely. In August 1975 he published a second letter, "On the Monty Hall Problem", answering the critics, and it is there that the phrase "Monty Hall problem" appears in print for the first time (Selvin, 1975b). Selvin had also sent the problem to Monty Hall, who replied in a letter dated 12 May 1975 (Hall, 1975). Hall agreed with the arithmetic: the opened box did not turn the contestant's chance into a fifty-fifty bet, and in his words "It was always two to one against him." But he pointed to a hole of a different kind. On the real show, once a box had been opened, the contestant was not allowed to trade, so the problem as posed was not a game anyone could actually play with him.

Hall's letter is easy to read as a joke, and it is also the first appearance of the objection that would dominate the professional debate sixteen years later. He did not dispute the probability. He disputed the protocol, the claim that the host would offer the trade at all. Selvin's sketch itself contains the same point in a more sinister form, since its host is trying to buy the box back with escalating cash offers, which is what a host does when he wants the contestant to make a particular choice. The *New York Times* article of 1991 dated the name to an analysis in *The American Statistician* in 1976; the year was 1975, a small example of how quickly the problem's own history was compressed.

The problem then had a quiet decade among specialists. Barry Nalebuff, then an economist at Princeton and later at Yale, put it in the puzzle column he wrote for the *Journal of Economic Perspectives*, in the autumn 1987 issue (Nalebuff, 1987). He gave the Bayesian solution, framed the exchange between host and contestant as a game between two players with opposed interests, and asked economists whether the fact that most people choose to stay should make them look for alternatives to Bayes' rule as a model of behaviour (Galera, Carrera-Gómez and Coto-Millán, 2004, discuss that last question). By 1990 the problem had a name, a correct solution, a known dependence on the host's behaviour and a game-theoretic analysis, all in journals read by statisticians and economists. What it did not have was a large audience.

## *Parade*, 1990–1991

*Parade* was the magazine supplement distributed with Sunday newspapers across the United States, and "Ask Marilyn" was one of its regular features. Marilyn vos Savant had been listed in the *Guinness Book of World Records* for the highest recorded IQ, and her column answered readers' questions about logic, mathematics, language and everyday puzzles. On 9 September 1990 she printed a question from Craig F. Whitaker of Columbia, Maryland, describing the three doors, the goats and the host who knows what is behind them and opens a goat door, and asking whether it is to the contestant's advantage to switch. She answered that it is, that the contestant's door wins with probability 1/3 and the other with 2/3, and gave the million-door illustration (vos Savant, 1990a).

The reaction is the part of the story everyone knows. In follow-up columns on 2 December 1990 and 17 February 1991 she restated the answer, set out the six equally likely ways the game can go for a contestant who always stays or always switches, and printed letters from readers with doctorates who told her she was mistaken (vos Savant, 1990b, 1991a). By February 1991 she was reporting that she had received thousands of letters, nearly all saying she was wrong: 92 per cent of the letters from the general public were against her answer, and 65 per cent of those from universities. Overall, she wrote, nine out of ten readers disagreed with her. She added that "math answers aren't determined by votes" (vos Savant, 1991a), and then did something more useful than repeat the argument a third time. She asked teachers to run the game in their classrooms.

The protocol she proposed is worth describing carefully, because the second half of this article measures how much evidence it could produce. According to the description Michael Brandt gave in *The C Users Journal* in July 1992, two students play, one as contestant and one as host. Three paper cups are numbered 1, 2 and 3. With the contestant's eyes closed, the host throws a die until it shows a 1, 2 or 3 and hides a penny under that cup. The contestant throws the die the same way to choose a cup. The host lifts one of the two other cups that he knows to be empty. In the first stage the contestant always stays, and the game is played 200 times with the number of wins recorded; in the second stage it is played another 200 times with the contestant always switching (Brandt, 1992). Brandt, an assistant professor of psychiatry who wrote signal-analysis software in C, wrote a Monte Carlo simulation of the protocol in C and published it with his article.

The letters themselves were reported in detail by Tierney in his front-page article of 21 July 1991. Vos Savant estimated that she had received about ten thousand, the great majority disagreeing with her; close to a thousand carried the signatures of people with doctorates, many on the letterheads of mathematics and science departments. Tierney quoted several, including one from Robert Sachs, a mathematician at George Mason University, who had written to explain that once a door is shown to be a loser the two remaining choices must each have probability 1/2, and who asked her to confess her error. By the time of the article Sachs had written her a second letter, conceding the mistake, and had resolved as penance to answer everyone who had written to criticise him after his letter appeared in the column; he told Tierney it had been deeply embarrassing for him professionally, and Tierney noted that he was one of the few critics who retracted at all. The classroom experiments, the article reported, had by then been run in classes from second grade to graduate school at more than a thousand schools, and the problem had been argued over at the Central Intelligence Agency, at MIT and at Los Alamos (Tierney, 1991). Tens of thousands of students who ran the game at her suggestion, Tierney wrote, had reached the conclusion that switching pays.

It is easy to tell this part of the story as a morality tale about credentialed experts and one person who was right, and several retellings do. It is more useful to notice what the wrong answer actually was. The letters almost all made the same argument as Bertrand's tempting reasoner and Gardner's prisoner: an impossibility has been ruled out, two possibilities remain, and nothing distinguishes them, so they are equally likely. The argument is correct whenever the observation that ruled out the third possibility is equally likely under the two survivors, which is the case when the host opens a door at random and happens to reveal a goat. The writers were applying a principle that is usually right to a case where the host's knowledge breaks it, and most of them never asked how the door came to be opened. That question, rather than a calculation, is what separated the right answers from the wrong ones in 1990, and it is the same question the statisticians would put back on the table a few months later.

## Monty Hall's dining room, July 1991

Tierney's article is remembered for its headline and its count of letters, but its centre is an experiment. In July 1991 he visited Monty Hall, who had hosted roughly 4,500 episodes of *Let's Make a Deal* between 1963 and 1990, at his house in Beverly Hills. Hall set three small cardboard doors on his dining-room table. An ignition key stood for the car, and the goats were played by a package of raisins and a roll of Life Savers. The contestant was the reporter himself.

Hall first explained that he was not surprised by the experts. Contestants on the show, he said, made the same assumption once he had shown them an empty door: they believed the odds on their own door had improved to one in two, and they would refuse to give it up whatever cash he offered. Opening a door was a way of applying pressure, and he had a literary nickname for it, after Henry James's novella about a governess, *The Turn of the Screw*. Hall then ran ten rounds in which Tierney always stayed with his first door, and ten in which he always switched. Staying won four cars and six goats; switching won eight cars and two goats. Hall concluded that her answer was right and that the contestant should switch (Tierney, 1991).

Then he reread the original column, found what he considered a loophole, and suggested more rounds. In the first, Tierney picked door 1 and Hall simply opened it to show a goat. When Tierney protested that he had not been offered a switch, Hall replied that nothing obliged him to offer one, since he controlled the show. In the second round Tierney again picked door 1; Hall opened door 3 to show a goat, and as Tierney prepared to switch Hall started offering him money not to, raising the offer in steps to $5,000. Tierney switched and got a goat. Hall explained that the rising offers had been meant to persuade him that the car was behind door 2, because Hall knew it was behind door 1. He then won the next eight rounds by opening the contestant's own door at once whenever the first pick was wrong, and offering a switch only when the first pick was right. Against that host, the only way to win a car was to ignore vos Savant's advice and stay.

The article went on to record that Gardner and Persi Diaconis, then a probabilist at Harvard and formerly a professional magician, had noticed the same gap when they compared her wording with the versions they had analysed. Gardner told Tierney that the problem is not well formed unless it states that the host must always open an empty door and offer the switch, since a malevolent host who opens a door only when it suits him can push the probability of winning by switching as low as zero. Diaconis said that, strictly, the question cannot be answered without knowing the host's motivation. Vos Savant acknowledged the ambiguity in the original statement, called it a minor assumption that should have been made obvious by her subsequent analyses, and said she would not have minded the objection if her critics had raised it, because it would have shown they understood the problem; what dismayed her was that they never got past their first impression. Hall had the last word in the article. If the host is required to open a door every time and offer the switch, he said, the contestant should switch; if the host can choose whether to offer it, the contestant should beware, because it depends on the host's mood (Tierney, 1991).

Two things in that afternoon deserve more attention than they usually get. The first is that Hall's twenty fair rounds are often cited as a confirmation, and twenty rounds is a very small experiment; the second half of this article measures exactly how small. The second is that Hall's loophole is not a technicality. A host who offers the switch only to contestants who picked the car turns switching into a guaranteed loss, a host who offers it only to contestants who picked a goat turns it into a guaranteed win, and a real host on a real show offered switches according to whatever made good television. The two-thirds answer is a property of one protocol, and Hall, who had run the real protocol on and off between 1963 and 1990, knew that better than anyone writing letters to *Parade*.

## The statisticians' quarrel, 1991–2011

The professional literature took the problem up in the November 1991 issue of *The American Statistician*, in a paper by J. P. Morgan, N. R. Chaganty, R. C. Dahiya and M. J. Doviak titled "Let's Make a Deal: The Player's Dilemma" (Morgan et al., 1991a). Their complaint was not that vos Savant's number was wrong but that she had answered a different question from the one asked. Her arguments, and the classroom experiments, computed the probability that the strategy "always switch" wins, averaged over everything that can happen. The contestant in Whitaker's letter, they argued, is in a specific situation, having chosen door 1 and seen the host open door 3, and the relevant quantity is the conditional probability that switching wins given that situation.

Under the four rules stated at the start of this article the two quantities coincide at 2/3, and that coincidence is why the distinction looks pedantic. Morgan and his co-authors made it bite by relaxing the fourth rule. Suppose that when the contestant's door hides the car the host opens door 3 with probability q and door 2 with probability 1 − q, where q need not be one half. The car is behind door 2 with prior probability 1/3, and in that case the host opens door 3 with certainty. It is behind door 1 with prior probability 1/3, and in that case he opens door 3 with probability q. The posterior probability that switching wins after door 3 has been opened is therefore

```
P(switch wins | host opens door 3) = (1/3 · 1) / (1/3 · 1 + 1/3 · q) = 1 / (1 + q)
P(switch wins | host opens door 2) = 1 / (2 − q)
```

A host who always opens door 3 when he has the choice (q = 1) leaves switching at exactly one half after door 3 opens, and makes it a certain win after door 2 opens. A host who never opens door 3 by choice (q = 0) makes switching a certain win after door 3. The enumeration in the repository checks the formula at q = 0, 1/4, 1/2, 3/4 and 1 and gives 1, 4/5, 2/3, 4/7 and 1/2 after door 3. Two features of the result carry the whole later debate. The conditional probability never falls below one half, so switching never does worse than staying whatever the host's preference, and the contestant who knows nothing about q still loses nothing by switching. And the unconditional probability that "always switch" wins is 2/3 for every value of q, because the host's preference only redistributes the wins between the games in which he opens door 2 and those in which he opens door 3. Jeffrey Rosenthal later gave the extreme case a memorable name: in "Monty Crawl", a tired host who crawls to the lowest-numbered goat door available opens door 3 only when he must, so switching after door 3 wins with certainty and switching after door 2 wins half the time (Rosenthal, 2008).

Vos Savant answered Morgan et al. in a letter to the same journal, which the later literature describes as angry (vos Savant, 1991b); Richard Seymann published a comment and the authors a rejoinder (Seymann, 1991; Morgan et al., 1991b). The exchange did not end there. In 2010, answering a note by Hogbin and Nijdam, Morgan and his co-authors acknowledged an error in their original paper and printed part of Whitaker's original letter to vos Savant, which turned out to be more ambiguous than her paraphrase (Morgan et al., 2010; Gill, 2011a). The original letter, according to Gill's encyclopaedia entry, mentioned that its writer had worked out two different situations depending on whether the host knows what is behind the doors (Gill, 2011b), so the question that started the controversy already contained the distinction the controversy was about.

The most interesting contribution of that period is a dissent. Richard Gill, a mathematical statistician at Leiden, argued in *Statistica Neerlandica* in 2011 that the standard treatment of the problem, which supplies "obvious" assumptions and then computes a conditional probability, is an example of solution-driven science (Gill, 2011a). Whitaker asked for an action, whether to switch, not for a probability. A contestant who does not know the host's tie-breaking habit cannot compute the conditional probability at all, but can still answer the question, because switching is at least as good as staying for every possible habit and better on average. Gill's preferred formulation is the game-theoretic one, which the literature traces back to Nalebuff's column: if the host is free to place the car and choose doors to minimise the contestant's chance, and the contestant is free to randomise, the value of the game is 2/3, the host's minimax strategy is the uniform placement and the fair coin, and the contestant's is to pick a door at random and always switch. The uniform assumptions, on this view, are not something one assumes in order to compute; they are what a host who wants to give the contestant nothing exploitable would choose.

Leonard Gillman's paper "The Car and the Goats" in the *American Mathematical Monthly* of January 1992 gave a careful treatment of the conditional probabilities, including a host with a preference between the two doors, that many teachers later relied on (Gillman, 1992). Rosenthal's "Monty Hall, Monty Fall, Monty Crawl" in *Math Horizons* in 2008 gave the ignorant host its name, Monty Fall, after a host who trips against a door that happens to hide a goat; with that host the two remaining doors really are equally likely, as the enumeration in the repository confirms (Rosenthal, 2008). Jason Rosenhouse collected the whole literature in a book-length treatment the following year (Rosenhouse, 2009). Part 2 of this series takes these variants one at a time. For the history it is enough to note that by 2011 the professional literature had separated three questions that the 1990 letters had treated as one: what the probability of winning by switching is under a stated model, which model the question intended, and what a contestant should do when the model is not known.

## Erdős and a spreadsheet

The most quoted anecdote of the episode concerns Paul Erdős, among the most prolific mathematicians who ever lived. The fullest first-hand account is by his friend Andrew Vázsonyi, a Hungarian-American operations researcher who had known Erdős since they were teenagers in Budapest (Vázsonyi, 1999). On Erdős's last visit to Vázsonyi in Santa Rosa, California, Vázsonyi mentioned the car-and-goats problem and said the answer was to switch, expecting to move on to another subject. Erdős said it was impossible, and that switching should make no difference.

Vázsonyi showed him the decision-tree solution he used in his undergraduate course on quantitative techniques in management, and it did not work: Erdős wanted a direct explanation. Vázsonyi, by his own account, told Erdős he had seen many people convinced by verbal arguments, in both directions, but had never seen a single explanation that convinced many, and walked away. An hour later Erdős came back irritated, complaining that Vázsonyi had not told him why he should switch. Vázsonyi then showed him a Monte Carlo simulation of the game that he had written in Excel, and that convinced him. A few days later Erdős telephoned to say that Ron Graham had explained the problem to him and that he now understood it; Vázsonyi recorded that he could not follow Graham's explanation himself (Vázsonyi, 1999). The story is also told in Paul Hoffman's biography of Erdős (Hoffman, 1998).

The anecdote is usually told as a joke at the expense of a great mathematician, and that misses what is interesting about it. Erdős accepted the evidence of the simulation before he had an explanation he found satisfying. That is the right order for a problem in which intuition is known to fail, and it is the same order vos Savant chose when she stopped arguing and asked teachers to run the game. The question a data scientist would ask about both is how much a simulation or a classroom experiment of a given size can actually prove, and the rest of this article answers it.

## The calculation, once, without shortcuts

The cleanest way to see every version of the problem at once is to write down the joint distribution of the car's position and the host's action, and condition on what was seen. With the contestant holding door 1 and the four standard rules in force, there are four possible situations: the car behind door 1 with the host opening door 2, probability 1/6; the car behind door 1 with the host opening door 3, probability 1/6; the car behind door 2, in which case the host must open door 3, probability 1/3; and the car behind door 3, in which case he must open door 2, probability 1/3. Staying wins in the first two, total 1/3. Always switching wins in the last two, total 2/3. Given that door 3 was opened, the situations still possible are the second and third, with probabilities 1/6 and 1/3, so the car is behind door 2 with probability (1/3)/(1/6 + 1/3) = 2/3. The enumeration script does this with exact rational arithmetic:

```python
from fractions import Fraction as F

def standard_game(q=F(1, 2)):
    """Joint law of (car, opened door) when the player holds door 1."""
    joint = {}
    for car in (1, 2, 3):
        if car == 1:                       # host may choose; door 3 with prob q
            joint[(1, 3)] = F(1, 3) * q
            joint[(1, 2)] = F(1, 3) * (1 - q)
        else:                              # host is forced to open the other goat door
            joint[(car, 5 - car)] = F(1, 3)
    return joint
```

The odds form of Bayes' theorem turns the whole family of problems into one line. Write the posterior odds that the car is behind door 2 rather than door 1, after door 3 is opened, as the prior odds times the likelihood ratio of the host's action:

```
posterior odds (door 2 : door 1) = prior odds × P(host opens 3 | car behind 2) / P(host opens 3 | car behind 1)
                                 = 1 × 1 / q
```

With the fair coin, q = 1/2 and the likelihood ratio is 2, so the odds are 2:1 and the probability is 2/3. Bertrand's boxes have the same likelihood ratio, since a gold coin is certain from the gold-gold box and has probability one half from the mixed box, and so do Gardner's prisoners, since the warden must name B if C is pardoned and names B half the time if A is. The ignorant host who opens door 3 at random, conditional on its showing a goat, has a likelihood ratio of one, because he opens door 3 with probability one half whether the car is behind door 1 or door 2, and the posterior odds stay at even. Every disagreement in the history above is a disagreement about one number, the probability that the host would have done what he did under each hypothesis about where the car is. People who answered one half were, in effect, setting that ratio to one, which is correct for the ignorant host and wrong for the one in the question.

A literal simulation of a million games, placing the car and the contestant's choice at random and letting the host follow the rules, gives win rates of 0.3327 for staying and 0.6673 for switching.

## More doors, and what the large-number argument really changes

Vos Savant's million doors, and the hundred doors that many later explanations use, are the most effective intuition pump in the whole story, and they work for a reason worth making exact. Consider n doors, one car and n − 1 goats. The contestant picks one. The host, who knows where the car is, opens k of the other doors, all hiding goats, chosen at random among the goat doors he is allowed to open. The contestant then either stays or switches to one of the n − 1 − k other doors still closed, chosen at random. Staying wins with probability 1/n. Switching wins only if the first pick was wrong, which has probability (n − 1)/n, and in that case the car is behind one of the n − 1 − k other closed doors, uniformly, so

```
P(switch wins) = (n − 1) / (n · (n − k − 1)),     switch / stay = (n − 1) / (n − k − 1)
```

The repository checks the formula against brute-force enumeration of every placement of the car, every subset the host might open and every door the contestant might switch to, for all fifteen combinations with three to seven doors, and against literal simulation of a hundred thousand games for three cases.

The formula separates two things the hundred-door story changes at the same time. With three doors and one door opened, switching wins 2/3 and doubles the chance. With a hundred doors and ninety-eight opened, switching wins 0.99 and multiplies the chance by ninety-nine. But with a hundred doors and only one opened, switching wins 0.0101 against 0.0100 for staying, an advantage of one per cent: the ratio is 99/98 ≈ 1.0102. A simulation of a hundred thousand such games gives a switching win rate of 0.0105, within sampling error of 0.0101 (the standard error is about 0.0003), and a run of that size could not resolve a one per cent advantage. With a million doors and one opened, the ratio is 1.000001. Adding doors does nothing for the switcher by itself. What makes the large-number versions feel obvious is that the host does an enormous amount of work in them, opening every door except one, and each door he opens is a door he was forced to avoid if it hid the car.

The right-hand panel of Figure 2 shows how unevenly that work pays. With a hundred doors, opening one door improves switching by one per cent, opening half the doors only about doubles it (a factor of 2.02 at k = 50), and opening ninety leaves a factor of eleven. Almost all of the ninety-nine-fold advantage arrives with the last handful of doors, as the host's choices shrink the set of doors the car could be behind to one. The three-door game is the smallest case of "open everything except one", which is why the million-door story is a faithful illustration of it. It is a faithful illustration because it keeps the host's rule, open all but one, while changing the scale. A version that kept the number of opened doors at one while multiplying the doors would teach the opposite lesson.

## How much did the experiments prove?

The two experiments in this history were used in opposite ways. Monty Hall's twenty rounds were reported as a vindication, by Hall himself and by every retelling since. Vos Savant's classroom protocol was proposed as the way to end the argument for good. Both are usually cited as conclusions rather than read as what they are, data sets with sample sizes, and it is a short calculation to read them that way.

Start with the dining room. Staying won 4 of 10 games and switching won 8 of 10. The question at issue in 1991 was whether the stay and switch rates were 1/3 and 2/3, as vos Savant said, or 1/2 and 1/2, as her critics said. The likelihood of Hall's results under the first model, divided by their likelihood under the second, is a Bayes factor between two simple hypotheses, and it works out exactly to 2^34/3^20, about 4.9. Almost all of that comes from the switching games: eight cars in ten switches favour 2/3 over 1/2 by a factor of 4.4. The four cars in ten stays favour 1/3 over 1/2 by a factor of only 1.1, because four out of ten sits just below 0.415, the stay rate at which ten games favour neither model (ln(4/3)/ln 2, since a stay that wins counts against vos Savant by a factor of 3/2 and one that loses counts for her by 4/3). Someone who started at even odds between vos Savant and her critics would end Hall's experiment believing her with probability 0.83. A frequentist reading says the same thing differently: the one-sided Fisher exact test comparing the two arms gives p = 0.085, and the binomial test of eight switch wins in ten against a rate of one half gives p = 56/1024 ≈ 0.055. The exact 95 per cent interval for the switching rate runs from 0.44 to 0.98, which contains one half, and the interval for the staying rate runs from 0.12 to 0.74, which contains both one third and one half.

None of this makes Hall's demonstration wrong. Under vos Savant's model, ten games of each strategy show switching ahead 91 per cent of the time, a tie 5 per cent of the time and staying ahead 4 per cent of the time, so his result was the most likely kind of outcome. A gap of four or more cars in favour of switching, which he observed, happens with probability 0.48 under her model and 0.058 if both strategies win half the time. That is suggestive evidence, of the kind that should make a sceptic curious. It is not the kind of evidence that should end a scientific argument, and an analyst who reported "p = 0.085, Bayes factor 4.9" to a sceptical audience would be told, correctly, to collect more data. Hall understood that too, since his next move was to run more rounds with different rules.

The classroom protocol was a different matter. With 200 games staying and 200 switching, the expected base-10 logarithm of the Bayes factor between the two models is 9.8, a factor of the order of ten billion, and the probability that a class following the protocol honestly sees switching fail to come out ahead is 4.6 × 10⁻¹²; across a thousand classes, the expected number of failures is about 5 × 10⁻⁹. Whatever produced the few reports that did not favour switching, it was not sampling noise from the protocol she described. Had she asked for twenty games of each strategy instead, about twenty-one classes in a thousand would have seen switching tie or fall behind, which would have been enough to keep the letters coming.

The left panel of Figure 3 shows how the evidence grows with the number of games per strategy. Merely seeing switching come out ahead is easy: it happens 91 per cent of the time with ten games each and 98 per cent with twenty. A formal test is much harder to pass. The one-sided Fisher test at the five per cent level, comparing the two arms, first reaches 80 per cent power at 32 games per arm, 95 per cent at 53 and 99 per cent at 72. A test that uses only the switching games against a rate of one half, which is what someone who only wanted to check the switcher's success rate might do, first reaches those levels at 58, 98 and 139 games. The curves are jagged because exact tests on small counts can only reject at a few attainable significance levels, so power can dip after it first crosses a threshold (the binomial test stays above 80 per cent only from 60 games on, and above 99 per cent only from 143), which is a familiar nuisance in the analysis of small experiments and the reason the power of the binomial test is actually higher at five games than at ten. Vos Savant's two hundred per arm, chosen by someone who was not a statistician, was a well-powered design with a large margin. Hall's ten per arm gave the Fisher test about 30 per cent power, and the binomial test on his switching games about 10 per cent.

There is a second, quieter point about the protocol, and it connects the classrooms to the statisticians' quarrel. Brandt's description specifies a die for placing the penny and a die for the contestant's choice, and says that the host lifts one of the two other cups that he knows to be empty. It does not say what the host does when both other cups are empty, which is exactly the tie-breaking probability q in the Morgan et al. formula. The omission does not matter, and the reason is instructive. The protocol measures the win rate of the strategy "always switch", averaged over all games, and that rate is 2/3 for every value of q. A class with a host who always lifted the left-hand cup when he could would have produced the same totals as a class with a fair coin. The classroom experiments therefore answered vos Savant's question, about the strategy, with overwhelming force, and could not have answered Morgan's question, about the conditional probability after a particular door is opened, unless the classes had also recorded which cup the host lifted and broken their results down by it. The experiment and the dispute line up exactly: the experiment measured the one quantity that does not depend on the part of the protocol the dispute was about.

## How long a sceptic's simulation has to run

Erdős was convinced by a simulation, and Vázsonyi's essay does not say how many games it played. Excel could produce hundreds in a fraction of a second, so the length was limited only by patience. The question that matters for anyone who uses simulation to settle an argument is how long a run has to be before the evidence is strong, and for this problem it has a simple answer.

Consider a sceptic who watches switching games one at a time and keeps a running Bayes factor between "switching wins 2/3" and "switching wins 1/2". Each win multiplies the factor by (2/3)/(1/2) = 4/3 and each loss by (1/3)/(1/2) = 2/3. When switching truly wins two thirds of the time, the logarithm of the factor drifts upward by the Kullback–Leibler divergence between the two Bernoulli distributions, 0.0566 nats per game. Reaching a factor of 100, a conventional threshold for decisive evidence, therefore takes ln(100)/0.0566 ≈ 81 games on average. A simulation of twenty thousand such sceptics, each stopping when the factor reaches 100 or falls to 1/100, gives a median of 70 games, a mean of 83, a 90th percentile of 149 and a 99th percentile of 257, and 0.8 per cent of them stop at the wrong boundary. When switching truly wins half the time, the same rule stops after a median of 67 games, and 1.0 per cent of the runs wrongly reach a factor of 100 in favour of two-thirds.

The right panel of Figure 3 shows thirty such runs, with Hall's ten switching games marked. His factor of 4.4 is better than a typical run of ten: under the two-thirds model the median run has seven wins, which gives a factor of 2.2, and eight or more wins happens only 30 per cent of the time. It is still nowhere near decisive. A spreadsheet of a few hundred games, which is what any programmer would write in a few minutes, is enough to make the evidence overwhelming in expectation. Erdős's resistance, in other words, was not resistance to weak evidence. He wanted an explanation, and the simulation gave him a fact; the explanation came later, from Graham. That separation, between being convinced that something is true and understanding why, is common in statistics, where a simulation or an experiment often establishes a result long before anyone can say in one sentence why it holds, and it is worth being explicit about which of the two a given piece of evidence provides.

## What the history separates

Read in order, the history sorts its participants by the layer of the problem they were working on.

The number was settled early and repeatedly. Bertrand had the structure in 1889, and his count, carried to the end, gives two-thirds; Gardner in 1959, Selvin in 1975 and Nalebuff in 1987 had the same likelihood ratio of two and the same answer. The letters of 1990 were wrong at this layer: they ran the symmetry argument that Bertrand had warned against, and most of their authors did not ask how the door came to be opened. Vos Savant was right at this layer from the first column.

The model was the subject of everything that came after. Hall raised it in his letter to Selvin in 1975, when he pointed out that his show did not allow the trade, and again at his dining-room table in 1991, when he showed what a host who chooses when to offer the switch can do. Gardner and Diaconis raised it in Tierney's article. Morgan and his co-authors turned it into a formula, Rosenthal into a set of memorable variants, and Gill into an argument that the uniform assumptions are best understood as the host's minimax strategy rather than as facts about the world. Vos Savant's column had left part of the model implicit, and she acknowledged it; her critics in the letters had not noticed the model at all.

The evidence was the classrooms, Hall's twenty rounds and Vázsonyi's spreadsheet. The classroom protocol was strong enough to settle the strategy question beyond any reasonable doubt, and by construction it was blind to the conditional question. Hall's rounds were a demonstration, worth a Bayes factor of about five. The spreadsheet, at any length a programmer would bother to run, was decisive.

Selvin's first letter, read again with that sorting in mind, contains all three layers in a few hundred words. The hint at the end points to the answer. The host's escalating offers to buy back the box are a host strategy, which is what the model is about. And the second letter, written because readers objected, is where the assumptions started to be spelled out. The problem has been rediscovered every generation since because each generation tends to see only one of the layers. The next article in this series stays with the second, the model, and takes the host's possible rules one at a time: the biased host, the ignorant host, the host who offers the switch selectively, the host as a strategic opponent, and the contestant who has watched enough episodes to estimate how the host behaves.
