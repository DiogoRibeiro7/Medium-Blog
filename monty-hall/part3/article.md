# The Host Is a Missingness Mechanism: Monty Hall, Ignorability and the Self-Selected Ratings of the Coat Data

## Part 3 of 4. The one-half answer is what the Monty Hall problem looks like when the data are treated as missing at random, and the same mistake can be measured on real ratings, where it changes which model an offline evaluation picks

The second article in this series took the host's rules apart into four components and found that only one of them, the policy that decides when the switch is offered, can turn the advice around in a way no strategy protects against. It ended by calling that policy a selection mechanism: the host decides which games show the contestant an offer, and what the contestant should conclude depends on how that decision was made. This article takes the idea further, to the whole problem. The contents of the three doors are a data set with one value observed and two missing. The host's rule for opening a door is the mechanism that decides which value is observed. The answer of one half, which most of the roughly ten thousand readers who wrote to Marilyn vos Savant at *Parade* defended after her column of September 1990, is exactly the answer that standard missing-data theory gives when the mechanism is assumed to be ignorable, and the answer of two-thirds is the one it gives when the mechanism is modelled.

That reframing is not only a change of vocabulary. It connects the puzzle to the framework statisticians use every day to decide whether incomplete data can be analysed as if the missing part did not matter, and it carries the puzzle's lesson into data where the stakes are real. The second half of the article does that with a public data set that has an unusual and valuable property: the same people rated products twice, once choosing the products themselves and once rating products assigned to them at random. The first set of ratings plays the role of the doors the host chose to open; the second plays the role of a host who opens doors by coin toss. Comparing the two measures the selection mechanism directly, shows how much of it can be recovered from observable characteristics, and shows what it does to the offline evaluation of rating models, which is where most data scientists meet it. Every number computed from data in the article is printed by a script in the series repository, linked at the end.

## Rubin's question

In 1976 Donald Rubin asked when it is legitimate to analyse incomplete data by simply ignoring the process that made them incomplete (Rubin, 1976). Write Y for the complete data, the values one would have if nothing were missing, and R for the indicator of which values were observed. The missing-data mechanism is the conditional distribution of R given Y. Rubin's distinctions, in the three-way form that is now standard (Little and Rubin, 2019), are these. The data are missing completely at random if the probability of observing a value does not depend on the data at all. They are missing at random if it depends only on values that were observed. They are missing not at random if it depends on values that were not observed. When the data are missing at random, and the parameters of the mechanism are unrelated to the parameters of interest, the mechanism is ignorable: likelihood-based and Bayesian inference can proceed from the observed values alone, as if the pattern of missingness carried no information. When the data are missing not at random, the mechanism is not ignorable, and an analysis that ignores it answers a different question from the one asked.

The reason fits in one line of algebra. Split the complete data into the observed part and the missing part, and let θ govern the data and ψ the mechanism. What the analyst actually has is the observed values together with the pattern R, and their joint likelihood is the complete-data model times the mechanism, integrated over everything not seen:

```
L(θ, ψ | Y_obs, R) = ∫ f(Y_obs, Y_mis | θ) · f(R | Y_obs, Y_mis, ψ) dY_mis
```

If the mechanism does not depend on Y_mis, the second factor comes out of the integral, the likelihood splits into a part about θ and a part about ψ, and the part about θ is the likelihood one would write by ignoring R altogether. If the mechanism does depend on Y_mis, the integral cannot be split, and the pattern of what was observed carries information about θ that the ignorable likelihood throws away. Everything in this article is an instance of that second case.

Daniel Heitjan and Rubin later extended the idea from values that are either observed or missing to values that are observed only coarsely, as a set that contains the true value (Heitjan and Rubin, 1991). A rounded age, an interval-censored event time and a survey answer of "between 20,000 and 30,000" are coarse observations. The condition that makes the coarsening ignorable, together with the same requirement that the parameters of the coarsening process be distinct from those of the data, is called coarsening at random: the probability of observing a particular set must be the same for every true value inside that set. If a person whose income is 21,000 and one whose income is 29,000 are equally likely to answer "between 20,000 and 30,000", the coarse answer can be analysed by ordinary conditioning on the set. If the person with 29,000 is more likely to give that answer, ordinary conditioning is wrong.

## The game as a missing-data problem

Put the game in these terms. The complete data are the contents of the three doors: one car and two goats, with the car behind each door with probability one third. The contestant holds door 1. The host opens door 3 and shows a goat. What the contestant has observed is the content of one door, and through it a coarse observation of the car's location: the car is behind door 1 or door 2. The missing-data mechanism is the host's rule for choosing which door to open.

The analysis that ignores the mechanism conditions on the observed fact alone, "door 3 hides a goat". Of the three equally likely locations of the car, that fact rules out one and leaves two, equally likely, so switching wins with probability one half. This is the answer of the letters to *Parade*, and in Rubin's terms it is the ignorable analysis.

The analysis that models the mechanism uses the host's rule. A knowing host who must open a goat door opens door 3 with probability one half when the car is behind door 1 and with certainty when it is behind door 2. Including those probabilities gives posterior odds of 2 to 1 in favour of door 2 and the familiar two-thirds. The mechanism depends on the missing data, since which door the host opens depends on where the car is, and the car's location is exactly what the contestant does not observe. The data are missing not at random, the mechanism is not ignorable, and the ignorable analysis is wrong.

Coarsening at random gives the same verdict in one line. The coarse observation is the set {door 1, door 2}. For it to be coarsened at random, the probability that the host produces this observation must be the same whether the car is behind door 1 or door 2. For the knowing host the two probabilities are one half and one, a ratio of one half, and the condition fails. Peter Grünwald and Joseph Halpern used the Monty Hall problem as a central example in a 2003 study of when conditioning in a "naive" space that ignores the protocol gives the right answer, and, building on the statistical result that coarsening at random is exactly the condition under which naive conditioning is correct (Heitjan and Rubin, 1991; Gill, van der Laan and Robins, 1997), showed that the condition holds much less often than intuition suggests (Grünwald and Halpern, 2003). Judea Pearl had made the same point with Gardner's three prisoners in 1988: a reasoning system that conditions on the reported fact without representing the query that produced the report will draw the wrong conclusion (Pearl, 1988).

The ignorant host is the case where the mechanism really is ignorable. He opens door 2 or door 3 at random, without looking. Given that door 3 was opened and showed a goat, the probability of that observation is one half whether the car is behind door 1 or door 2, so the ratio is one and coarsening at random holds. The ignorable analysis gives one half, and so does the full one. The letters of 1990 were not using an absurd method. They were using the right method for a host who opens doors at random, and applying it to a host who does not.

## When the mechanism is unknown

A missing-data analyst who does not know the mechanism has three options: assume ignorability, model the mechanism with an assumption that cannot be checked from the data, or report what the data imply under every mechanism consistent with what is known. The third option is Charles Manski's partial identification (Manski, 2003), and the Monty Hall problem shows all three in miniature.

Write λ for the selection ratio, the probability that the host opens door 3 when the car is behind door 1 divided by the probability that he opens it when the car is behind door 2. The probability that switching wins after door 3 opens is 1/(1 + λ). The ignorable analysis sets λ = 1 and gets one half. The textbook host has λ = 1/2 and gives two-thirds. A host about whom the contestant knows only that he must open a goat door can have any λ between zero and one, depending on how he breaks ties, so the identification region for the probability that switching wins is the interval from one half to one. The left panel of Figure 1 draws it.

*Figure 1. Left: the probability that switching wins as a function of the host's selection ratio. The ignorable analysis sits at the boundary of the region a knowing host allows. Right: the mean rating of all coats in the Coat data implied by the self-selected ratings, as a function of an assumed selection slope, inside the bounds that hold under no assumption at all.*

Two features of that picture carry over to every non-ignorable problem. The selection ratio cannot be estimated from the observation itself: one opened door tells the contestant nothing about how the host would have behaved with the car somewhere else. It has to come from outside, from knowledge of the host's rules, from past episodes, or from an assumption. And the ignorable answer is not a neutral midpoint. Here it sits at the edge of the identification region, at its most pessimistic value for switching, because the knowing host's mechanism can only favour the door he leaves closed. Assuming ignorability is a substantive assumption about the mechanism, and in this case the most extreme one the setting allows.

A Bayesian contestant who does not know the tie-break has a fourth option, which is to put a prior on it, and the game shows a trap in doing so. With a uniform prior on q, it is tempting to average the conditional answer 1/(1 + q) over the prior, which gives ln 2 ≈ 0.693. That is not the posterior predictive probability, because it weights the values of q by the prior instead of by the posterior, which has already taken the opened door into account. The correct calculation averages the likelihood, not the answer: the probability of the car behind door 2 and door 3 opening is 1/3, the probability of the car behind door 1 and door 3 opening is E[q]/3 = 1/6, and the predictive probability that switching wins is (1/3)/(1/3 + 1/6) = 2/3. Because the host's mechanism enters the likelihood linearly, the Bayesian answer under uncertainty about q is the answer at the prior mean of q, and a contestant with any prior whose mean is one half, uniform or not, is facing the textbook host as far as this one decision is concerned. Averaging conclusions instead of likelihoods gives a different number, and it is worth checking for whenever the results of a sensitivity analysis are summarised by an average.

## Clips chosen for their ending

The most extreme form of non-ignorable selection is selection on the outcome itself, and the second article left a version of it open. Suppose a viewer estimates how often switching wins by watching clips of past games, and the clips were chosen by an editor. If every game is shown, or one game in ten chosen at random, the share of clips in which switching won is two-thirds, the true rate. If the editor always shows a game in which switching won and shows a game in which it lost only one time in five, the share of wins among the clips is 10/11, about 91 per cent. If the editor prefers the losses in the same proportion, it is 2/7, about 29 per cent. If only wins are shown, it is one.

The formula is p·s_w / (p·s_w + (1 − p)·s_l), where p is the true win rate and s_w and s_l are the probabilities that a winning and a losing game are shown, and it has the same structure as every formula in this series. The ratio s_w/s_l is a likelihood ratio controlled by someone who knew the outcome before deciding what to show, exactly as the host's offer ratio b/a was in the second article. Abraham Wald's analysis of the damage on bombers that returned from missions during the Second World War, in which the holes that mattered were the ones on the planes that did not return, is the classic statement of the same structure (Mangel and Samaniego, 1984). The returning planes were a sample selected on the outcome. The popular retelling, in which the armour was about to be put in the wrong places, goes beyond what Wald's memoranda record, but the structure it dramatises is the one in the formula: the damage that was observed was observed because it had not been fatal.

## A real data set with the same structure

Most data sets offer no way to measure their own selection mechanism, because the values that were not observed are, by definition, not in the data. The Coat data set, collected by Tobias Schnabel and co-authors for their 2016 paper on recommendations as treatments, is an exception built on purpose (Schnabel et al., 2016). They recruited 290 workers on Amazon Mechanical Turk and showed them a simple online shop of 300 coats, with facets and paging. Each worker was asked to find the coat he or she would most like to buy, and then rated, on a scale of one to five, the 24 coats explored along the way. Each worker also rated 16 coats chosen for him or her at random. The first set is the kind of data a recommender system collects in production, where people rate what they chose to look at. The second is a sample in which the mechanism is a coin toss and is therefore ignorable by design.

In the language of the game, the self-selected ratings are the doors the host chose to open, and the host is the user. The random ratings are the doors opened by an ignorant host. The question is what the first set says about the whole matrix of 290 × 300 = 87,000 user-coat pairs, of which 8 per cent were rated by choice, and the second set provides the answer to check against. The script downloads the files from a pinned copy in the repository of the AutoDebias project (Chen et al., 2021) and checks every file against a pinned SHA-256 digest; the original source is the authors' page at Cornell.

The two sets look very different. Among the self-selected ratings, 27.3 per cent are ones and 9.1 per cent are fives; among the random ones, 40.5 per cent are ones and 4.7 per cent are fives. The share of fours and fives is 27.4 per cent against 18.5 per cent, and the mean rating is 2.611 against 2.229, a difference of 0.383 points (computed from the unrounded means) with a 95 per cent interval of 0.333 to 0.435 when users are resampled. One data note matters for honesty about the comparison: 366 user-coat pairs appear in both files, always with the same rating, so they are a single response counted in both samples, presumably because the random draw sometimes picked a coat the user had already rated. Excluding them makes the difference slightly larger, 0.414.

## Measuring the host

Because the random ratings estimate the distribution of ratings over all user-coat pairs, the selection mechanism can be estimated directly. By Bayes' theorem, the probability that a pair is among the rated ones, given the rating the user would give it, is the share of that rating among self-selected ratings, times the overall share of pairs rated, divided by the share of that rating among random ratings. This is the naive-Bayes propensity estimator that Schnabel and co-authors used for the Yahoo! R3 data (Schnabel et al., 2016), and in the Coat data it gives a clean, increasing curve, drawn in the middle panel of Figure 2. A coat the user would rate one has a 5.4 per cent chance of being among those rated; at two, three and four the chance is 8.5, 9.1 and 10.6 per cent; at five it is 15.3 per cent. A coat the user loves is 2.84 times as likely to be observed as one the user dislikes. This is a host who opens the door more often when the prize is behind it, measured.

The direction of the selection is the opposite of the game's, and the comparison is instructive. Monty Hall's host never opens the door with the car: his selection hides the prize, so the doors he leaves closed are better than average, and the right inference from his choice is to move towards the unopened door. The users of the coat shop open, by rating them, the doors they like: their selection reveals the prizes, so the pairs they leave unrated are worse than average, and the right inference is to move the estimate of the unobserved pairs down. The mathematics is identical. In both cases the probability of observing a value depends on the value, the ignorable analysis treats the observed values as representative of the unobserved ones, and the error has the sign of the selection. A data scientist who has absorbed the Monty Hall problem as a rule about switching has learned the wrong lesson; the transferable lesson is that the sign and size of the correction come from the mechanism, and the mechanism has to be known or estimated before the observed values can be read.

With the mechanism estimated, the identification picture of the game can be drawn for the coats. With no assumption at all, the mean rating over all 87,000 pairs could lie anywhere between 1.13 and 4.81, because 92 per cent of the pairs are unrated and each of them could, as far as the self-selected data can say, be a one or a five. The ignorable analysis gives the mean of the observed ratings, 2.611. A one-parameter model of the mechanism, in which the probability of rating a coat grows exponentially with the rating at rate γ (a log-linear version of the exponential tilting model used in non-ignorable missing-data analysis by Kim and Yu, 2011, who tilt the distribution of the non-respondents relative to that of the respondents), turns the self-selected ratings into an implied mean for every value of γ: 2.445 at γ = 0.1, 2.288 at 0.2, 2.140 at 0.3. The right panel of Figure 1 draws the curve. The value that reproduces the random-sample mean is γ = 0.239, and a log-linear fit to the directly estimated selection probabilities gives a slope of 0.231 per rating point. Both numbers come from the same random sample, so their closeness is not an independent confirmation; what it shows is that the selection curve in Figure 2 is close to log-linear, which is what makes a one-parameter model a fair summary of it. The point is the same as in the game. Without the random sample, γ could not be estimated, and the self-selected ratings alone would be consistent with any mean in the bounds; the ignorable answer is one assumption among many, and here it is wrong by more than a third of a rating point.

The tilting model is a selection model, in the terminology of the missing-data literature: it says how the probability of being observed depends on the value. The other standard way to write a non-ignorable assumption is a pattern-mixture model, which says instead how the unobserved values differ from the observed ones (Little and Rubin, 2019). For the coats, the simplest pattern-mixture assumption shifts the mean of the unrated pairs by δ below the mean of the rated ones. To reproduce the random-sample mean, the 92 per cent of pairs that nobody chose to rate would have to average 0.416 points below the chosen ones, about 2.20 against 2.61. The two parametrisations describe the same gap in different units, one as a selection slope and one as a difference in means, and a sensitivity analysis can be reported in whichever unit its readers find easier to judge. In the game they are the same thing: the host's opening probabilities are the selection model, and the distribution of the car given which door was opened is the pattern mixture.

A technical note belongs here, because it explains a choice made later. With the naive-Bayes selection probabilities, which depend only on the rating, the reweighted mean of the self-selected ratings reproduces the random-sample mean exactly, 2.229, by construction, since the probabilities were built from the random sample's rating distribution. That estimator cannot be used to check itself against the random sample. It can be used, as the evaluation below does, to correct something else with a randomised sample from one group of users and check the correction against the randomised sample of another.

## Which coats, and who rates them

The selection in the Coat data has two sources, and they differ in what it takes to correct them. Some coats are more often chosen than others, and if the popular coats are also the better-liked ones, the self-selected ratings over-represent good coats. That part of the selection depends on which coat is rated, an observed variable, and it can be corrected by reweighting the coats. The other part is that the people who choose a given coat are the ones who like it, so even for the same coat the ratings of its choosers are higher than the ratings of people assigned to it. That part depends on the rating itself.

The data allow the two to be separated. Every coat has at least five ratings of each kind, so each coat's self-selected mean can be compared with its random mean. Weighting every coat equally in the self-selected data, which removes the over-representation of popular coats, lowers the mean from 2.611 to 2.541, a correction of 0.070 points. The remaining 0.313 points are the difference between the choosers' ratings and the random raters' ratings of the same coats, and 73 per cent of the 300 coats lie above the diagonal in the right panel of Figure 2. Most of the bias, about four-fifths of it, is about who rates a coat, not which coats are rated.

That does not make four-fifths of the bias uncorrectable, because some of "who rates it" is observable too. The clearest example in the data is gender. The shop sold 150 men's and 150 women's coats, and 87.4 per cent of the self-selected ratings are of coats of the rater's own gender, against 50.6 per cent of the random ratings. Coats of the other gender are rated lower in both samples, 1.98 against 2.47 among the random ratings. Part of the gap between the samples is simply that random assignment shows men women's coats and women men's coats, and people choosing for themselves rarely look at them. Propensity models that use only observed characteristics show how much of the selection they can absorb. Each is a logistic regression of whether a user-coat pair is rated, fitted to all 87,000 pairs with a ridge penalty on the coefficients, and the self-selected ratings are then reweighted by the inverse of the fitted probability. A model with one effect per coat, the regression version of reweighting the coats, removes 15 per cent of the gap, a little less than the 18 per cent removed by weighting the coats equally, because the penalty shrinks the estimated coat effects and so moderates the weights; without it, the two would coincide. A model with the main effects of the user's characteristics (gender, age group, location, interest in fashion) and the coat's (gender, type, colour, placement on the front page) removes 17 per cent. Adding a single term, the interaction between the user's gender and the coat's gender, raises that to 72 per cent, a reweighted mean of 2.336. Adding every other interaction between a user characteristic and a coat characteristic, which is close to the specification Schnabel and co-authors used for Coat, takes it only to 73 per cent, a mean of 2.333, with fitted propensities between 0.0013 and 0.73. One observable fact, whether the coat was made for someone of the user's gender, accounts for most of what looked like selection on the hidden rating. The remaining 0.104 points, about a quarter of the gap, are what is left after conditioning on everything observed, and as far as these covariates can tell it is selection on the hidden rating itself, or on unobserved tastes closely tied to it.

That sequence is a useful corrective to the way the game is usually taught. The textbook host's selection depends entirely on something the contestant cannot see. Real selection mechanisms are usually mixtures, and the share that depends on observables can be large and can hinge on a single variable that nobody thought to put in the model. A missing-data analysis that starts by asking which observed variables could plausibly drive the selection, and checks how much each one moves the estimate, can discover, as here, that the non-ignorable part is smaller than it first appeared. In the Coat data it does not fall to zero, and a conjecture worth stating as one is that this is the usual outcome: people choose what to rate, buy or report for reasons that no recorded covariate fully captures.

The decomposition is the most practical result in this article, because it says what kind of data each part of a correction needs. Selection on observables, which coat and which kind of person, needs covariates and a propensity model, and covariates are usually available. Selection on the hidden value needs information from outside the selected data, here a randomised sample, and in its absence an assumption about the mechanism, such as a value of γ, that the selected data cannot check. In the game, the first kind of selection would be a host whose behaviour depended on something the contestant could see, and the second is the host of the textbook, whose behaviour depends on where the car is.

## What the selection does to offline evaluation

Most data scientists meet this problem when they evaluate a model, not when they estimate a mean. A recommender is trained on ratings people chose to give, and the standard offline check holds out some of those ratings and measures the error on them. If the held-out ratings were selected in the same way as the training ratings, as they always are when they come from the same log, the check measures accuracy on the kind of item people choose to rate, which is not the accuracy that matters when the model is used to predict ratings for items people have not seen.

The Coat data make it possible to measure how much this matters. In each of 100 independent splits, four of each user's 24 self-selected ratings are held out and the other 20 are used for training; the users are divided at random into two halves, the random ratings of one half are used to estimate the selection probabilities by the naive-Bayes formula above, and the random ratings of the other half are the truth. Six models are fitted to the training ratings: the global mean, a shrunken coat mean, a model with user and coat effects, and a rank-4 matrix factorisation with user and coat effects, the last two both unweighted and weighted by the inverse of the estimated selection probability, which is the debiasing approach of Schnabel and co-authors. Each model is then scored three ways: by its mean squared error on the held-out self-selected ratings (the naive offline evaluation), by the same error reweighted by the inverse selection probabilities and normalised (the self-normalised inverse-propensity estimator, SNIPS), and by its error on the random ratings of the held-out half of the users (the truth).

![Figure 3](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/monty-hall/part3/figures/fig3_evaluation.png)

*Figure 3. Mean squared error of six rating models, averaged over 100 splits with one standard deviation either side: as measured on held-out ratings of coats users chose, the same reweighted for selection, and on ratings of coats assigned at random.*

Figure 3 shows the result. The weighted models are better: averaged over the splits, the weighted user-and-coat model has a true error of 1.139 against 1.262 for its unweighted version, and the weighted factorisation 1.144 against 1.271, a reduction of about 10 per cent, and the weighted model beats its unweighted version in the truth in every one of the 100 splits for both model types. The naive evaluation says the opposite. On held-out self-selected ratings, the unweighted user-and-coat model scores 1.220 and the weighted one 1.291, and the naive evaluation prefers the unweighted model in all 100 splits; for the factorisation it prefers the unweighted version in 85 of them. Asked to pick the best of the six models, the naive evaluation picks the unweighted user-and-coat model in 91 splits, the unweighted factorisation in 8 and a weighted model in 1, while in truth a weighted model is best in every split. The reweighted evaluation agrees with the truth on which version of each model is better in every split, and its average rank correlation (Kendall's tau) with the true ranking is 0.895 against 0.408 for the naive one. Its choice of the single best model matches the truth in 58 per cent of the splits, because the two weighted models are within a few thousandths of each other and trade places from split to split.

The mechanism behind the reversal is visible in the predictions. Trained on self-selected ratings, the unweighted models learn that ratings are fairly high, and they are accurate on more ratings of the same kind; on randomly assigned coats the predictions of the unweighted user-and-coat and factorisation models average between 2.56 and 2.57, against a true mean of 2.23 on those pairs, while the weighted models average 2.31 and 2.34. The weighted models learn the lower ratings that people give to coats they would not have chosen, which costs them accuracy on the chosen coats and gains it everywhere else. An evaluation that only ever looks at chosen coats penalises exactly the correction that makes the model better at its actual job.

Reweighting usually buys its lack of bias with variance, and the cost is visible in Figure 3, where the reweighted estimates spread more from split to split than the naive ones. Here the cost is small, because the selection probabilities vary only modestly between ratings: estimated from the training ratings of a single split, the probability for a five is about two and a half times the probability for a one, a little less than the 2.84 estimated from all the data. By the usual effective-sample-size formula, the 1,160 reweighted held-out ratings are worth on average 1,050 unweighted ones. In data where some items are almost never chosen, the same correction would be far noisier, and estimators that accept a little bias in exchange for a large reduction in variance, such as clipped weights, or doubly robust estimators that add a model of the outcome to the weights, become necessary.

One caveat belongs with this result. The reweighted evaluation worked because a randomised sample from other users was available to estimate the selection probabilities, 2,320 random ratings in each split. Without such a sample, the selection on the hidden rating has to be modelled with an assumption, and the evaluation inherits whatever error that assumption contains. Schnabel and co-authors noted that their naive-Bayes estimator needs only a small randomised sample and studied how its size matters (Schnabel et al., 2016), and the Coat data can say how small it can be here. The script repeats the evaluation 25 times at each of four sizes of randomised sample used to estimate the selection probabilities, from 5 to 50 per cent of the 145 users in the estimation half, with one pseudo-count added to each rating level so that a small sample cannot produce an empty level. With 112 random ratings, the weighted models are better than the unweighted ones in truth in 92 per cent of the comparisons, and the reweighted evaluation agrees with the truth on which is better in 92 per cent. With 224 random ratings both figures are at or near 100 per cent, and with 576 they are exactly 100 per cent; the rank correlation of the reweighted evaluation with the truth rises from 0.86 to 0.91 over the range. A few hundred randomly assigned ratings, a few per cent of the size of the self-selected log, were enough to turn an offline evaluation that picked the wrong model into one that picked the right family. The general lesson is not the particular number, which depends on how strong the selection is and how many rating levels there are, but that the information needed to undo a non-ignorable mechanism can be cheap to collect when it is collected on purpose, and is impossible to recover afterwards from the selected data alone.

## Hosts who announce their rules

The game and the coats are two extremes of the same structure, and most data sit somewhere between them. At one extreme is the host who announces his rule, and the clearest case in data science is the log of a contextual bandit or a randomised recommender that records, for every action it takes, the probability with which it took it. The Open Bandit Dataset, released by Yuta Saito and co-authors with data from the fashion platform ZOZOTOWN, records exactly that, for two logging policies running side by side (Saito et al., 2021). The logging policy is a host who decides which item each user sees, and its rule, a Thompson-sampling policy that does not use the user's features, depends on what it has learned from past clicks on each item, so the items shown are far from a random sample of the items available. But because the probabilities are logged, the mechanism is known, and the inverse-propensity estimator of Horvitz and Thompson (1952), or its doubly robust refinements (Dudík, Langford and Li, 2011), can evaluate a different policy from the logged one without bias and without any assumption about the hidden outcomes, provided every item the evaluated policy can show had a positive probability of being shown by the logging policy and the logged probabilities are correct. In this data set they are themselves estimates, computed by simulating the policy. What this host shares with the textbook one is the feature that settles the answer, a rule that is stated in advance; unlike Monty Hall, he does not look behind the door, since his choice depends on past clicks and not on whether this user would click.

Knowing the rule makes the correction unbiased, but it does not make it cheap, and the small sample of the Open Bandit Dataset that ships with its software shows by how much. In 10,000 impressions logged by the Thompson-sampling policy in the "All" campaign, the logged probabilities of the item shown range from 0.000045 to 0.95, with a median of 0.064, and the five most-shown items take 40 per cent of impressions; under the uniformly random policy every probability is 1/80 and the five most-shown items take 8 per cent. Evaluating the uniform policy from the Thompson log means weighting each impression by (1/80) divided by its logged probability, and the largest weight is 278. The effective sample size of the reweighted log is 340 impressions out of 10,000, about 3 per cent. With 42 clicks in the log, the reweighted estimate of the uniform policy's click-through rate is 0.0023, with a bootstrap interval from 0.0010 to 0.0043, against 0.0038 (0.0027 to 0.0051) measured directly on the uniform policy's own 10,000 impressions; the naive click-through rate of the Thompson log is 0.0042. The correction moves in the right direction but overshoots: the reweighted estimate ends up further below the on-policy value than the uncorrected one was above it, and its interval is wide. At this sample size it is too noisy to be useful; the full data set has some 26 million rows. A host who announces a strongly opinionated rule leaves the contestant able, in principle, to undo his selection without bias, and in practice able to do so only with a great deal of data. The more the logging policy favours some actions, the less the log can say about the actions it avoided.

At the other extreme is the host who chooses freely and records nothing, which is the user rating the coats she chose, the patient who stops coming to follow-up visits when the treatment stops working, or the borrower who is only observed repaying if a lender approved the loan. There the mechanism has to be estimated from separate data, like the random ratings in Coat, or bounded, like the identification region in the game, or assumed, with the assumption stated and its consequences shown, like the curve in the right panel of Figure 1.

## An old remedy for a new problem

The randomised sample that rescues the Coat evaluation has a long ancestry in survey statistics. In 1946 Morris Hansen and William Hurwitz proposed what is now called double sampling for non-response: when a mail survey leaves many households unanswered, draw a random subsample of the non-respondents and pursue them by more expensive means, an interviewer at the door, until all of them answer (Hansen and Hurwitz, 1946). The respondents are a selected sample whose selection may depend on the very quantity being measured, exactly like the coats people chose to rate. The followed-up subsample is a random sample from the people the selection left out, and, provided the follow-up obtains a complete response, combining the two with the right weights gives an unbiased estimate without any assumption about why people did not respond. The random ratings in Coat play a similar role. They are a small random sample of all user-coat pairs, chosen and unchosen alike, rather than of the unchosen ones only, but they too are collected by a mechanism the analyst controls, and that is what lets them measure the mechanism the analyst does not.

What the survey tradition also contributes is the observation that the missing-at-random assumption cannot be tested from the observed data alone. Tests exist for the stronger assumption that data are missing completely at random, by comparing the observed values of respondents with different missingness patterns, but whether the probability of being missing depends on the missing values themselves leaves no trace in the values that were observed, because every distribution of the observed data is consistent with many mechanisms (Little and Rubin, 2019). The only ways to learn about it are the ones this article has used: a sample collected by a known mechanism, knowledge of the process that generated the selection, or a sensitivity analysis that shows how far the conclusion moves as the assumed mechanism moves. In the game, the contestant who wants to know the host's rule cannot learn it from the door he just saw opened. He has to read the rules of the show, or watch the host for a long time, or reason about what the host is trying to achieve.

## The host in other data

The structure of the game appears wherever the process that reveals data knows something about what it reveals, and a few examples show how varied the hosts are. In a survey of household income, if the people who decline to answer are more often at the top or the bottom of the distribution, the observed incomes are a selected sample whose selection depends on the income itself; the standard remedies are covariate-based weighting for the observable part and a stated assumption, often a pattern-mixture shift, for the rest. In a clinical trial, a patient who stops attending follow-up visits because the treatment is not helping turns the last observed value into a host's choice; dropout of this kind is informative, and an analysis that treats it as random can bias the estimated benefit of the treatment, in a direction that depends on which arm loses the patients who are doing badly. In credit scoring, repayment is observed only for applicants who were approved, and the approval rule is the host: the lender knew something about the applicants it turned away, and a model trained on approved loans alone learns the repayment behaviour of people the lender already believed in, which is why the industry has a name, reject inference, for the attempt to reason about the others.

Machine-learning pipelines create hosts of their own. Active learning chooses which examples to send for labelling according to the model's current uncertainty, so the labelled set depends on what the model believed about the unlabelled examples. A content-moderation system sends to human review only the posts its classifier flagged, so the human labels describe the flagged posts, not the stream, and an estimate of the classifier's precision from reviewed posts says nothing about what it missed. An evaluation of a language model that asks human raters to examine only the answers an automatic checker found suspicious has the same shape. In each case the three questions of the next section separate the cases that can be fixed with weights from the ones that cannot.

## Three questions for any data set

The Monty Hall problem reduces to three questions that apply to any data set, and asking them in order is most of what it takes to avoid the error of 1990. Who decided which values would be observed? What did that person or process know about the values when deciding? And was the rule they followed recorded? If the answer to the second question is "nothing that depends on the missing values", the data are missing at random and the selection can be ignored or corrected with observed covariates. If the decision depended on the hidden values but the rule was recorded, the selection can be undone without bias by weighting, provided every value had some chance of being observed. If it depended on the hidden values and nothing was recorded, no analysis of the observed data alone can recover what was hidden, and the honest output is a range, or an estimate conditional on a clearly stated assumption about the mechanism.

In practice, the answer to the third question decides what a report should contain. Where the rule was recorded, the report should state the weights used and their effective sample size, because an exact correction computed from a few hundred effective observations is still a noisy one. Where it was not recorded but a randomised sample exists, the report should say how large that sample is and how the selection was estimated from it, as the evaluation above does. Where neither exists, the report should give the ignorable estimate alongside the estimates under a range of stated mechanisms, in a unit readers can judge, such as the selection slope γ or the shift δ of the unobserved values, and say how strong the selection would have to be to change the decision. That last form is the honest version of what the letters to *Parade* did: they reported one point in the identification region as if it were the only one.

The readers who wrote to *Parade* answered the first question correctly, since the host opened the door, and never asked the second. The Coat data show that the same omission, in an offline evaluation, makes a debiased model look worse than the biased one it improves on. The last article in this series turns from the mathematics to the people who answered one half: the laboratory evidence on why the switch is so hard to accept, and how much experience it takes before someone who plays the game repeatedly learns to switch.
