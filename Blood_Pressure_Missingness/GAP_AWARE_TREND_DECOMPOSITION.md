# Gap-aware decomposition of the systolic time trend

The primary analysis reports a global HC3 systolic trend of about **-4.27 mmHg per 30 days** across the full 64-day calendar window.

That estimate is statistically correct for the observed daily means, but its interpretation needs care because the time axis contains a **32-day interval with no measurements at all**. A single line fitted across the full calendar can combine two different sources of variation:

1. changes **within** periods where measurements were actually observed;
2. a level contrast **between** observation periods on opposite sides of the long gap.

This note separates those components without pretending that the missing interval contains an observed transition.

## Observation episodes

The unique longest internal missing run separates the current public snapshot into:

- a pre-gap episode with 8 observed days;
- a post-gap episode with 17 observed days;
- a 32-day unobserved interval between them.

This split is defined by the observation design. It is **not** an estimated change point.

## Episode-level results

On the current snapshot, the average systolic daily mean is approximately:

- **122.53 mmHg** before the gap;
- **116.26 mmHg** after the gap.

The raw episode mean difference is therefore about **-6.27 mmHg**.

A regression that centers calendar time separately within each episode estimates the post-minus-pre level contrast while identifying the time coefficient only from within-episode variation. On the current snapshot it gives:

- post-minus-pre level difference: **-6.27 mmHg**;
- common within-episode slope: approximately **-9.89 mmHg per 30 days**;
- the HC3 interval for the common within-episode slope crosses zero.

The pre-gap episode itself shows essentially no identifiable linear trend, while the post-gap episode has a negative point estimate but its robust interval also crosses zero.

## Exact decomposition of the global slope

For the two observation episodes, the ordinary least-squares cross-deviation can be written exactly as

\[
S_{xy}=S_{xy}^{\mathrm{within}}+S_{xy}^{\mathrm{between}}.
\]

Dividing both terms by the global

\[
S_{xx}=\sum_i (t_i-\bar t)^2
\]

produces additive contributions to the same global OLS slope.

For the current snapshot, about **87% of the negative global time-pressure covariance** is the between-episode component. Numerically, the within-episode and between-episode slope contributions add exactly to the global estimate of about **-4.27 mmHg per 30 days**.

This does **not** mean that 87% of any physiological change occurred during the gap. It means something much narrower:

> most of the linear association between calendar time and observed systolic pressure is generated by the separation of the two observed episodes, rather than by smooth variation inside the episodes.

## What this changes in the interpretation

The global negative slope remains a valid descriptive summary of the observed daily means. It should no longer be described, even informally, as evidence of a smooth decline through the 32-day missing interval.

The defensible interpretation is:

\[
\boxed{
\text{global decline}
=
\text{within-episode variation}
+
\text{between-episode contrast}
}
\]

with the current data showing that the **between-episode contrast dominates the global covariance structure**.

Because there are no measurements inside the gap, this analysis cannot determine:

- whether the underlying trajectory changed abruptly or gradually;
- when any transition occurred;
- why the two episodes differ;
- whether the difference reflects physiology, context, measurement behaviour, or some combination.

For that reason this module is called a **gap-aware trend decomposition**, not change-point detection or interrupted-time-series analysis.
