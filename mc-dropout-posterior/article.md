# Testing MC Dropout as a Posterior: Concentration, Benchmark Scores and Distribution Shift

## Five experiments on real regression data: what the spread of the stochastic passes measures, where the benchmark log-likelihood comes from, and what to use when a decision needs epistemic uncertainty

A trained network gives one number per input. A decision usually needs two: the prediction and some statement of how far it can be trusted. Monte Carlo dropout became one of the most widely used ways of getting the second number from a network that was not built to provide it. Dropout, the regulariser that switches off a random subset of units on every training step, is simply left switched on at prediction time. The same input is passed through the network T times, each time with a different random subset of units removed, and the T outputs are summarised by their mean, used as the prediction, and their standard deviation, used as the uncertainty. Nothing is retrained, nothing is added to the architecture, and a network trained with dropout for any other reason acquires error bars for the cost of T forward passes.

The justification came from Gal and Ghahramani (2016), who showed that training a network with dropout and weight decay can be read as approximate variational inference: the random masks define a distribution over weights, the training loss is, up to constants, an objective that pulls that distribution towards the posterior of a Bayesian model, and the spread of the stochastic forward passes is therefore an approximation to the spread of the posterior predictive distribution. The method has been used since to put intervals around regression outputs, to send uncertain cases to a person, to decide when a model should abstain and to detect inputs that look unlike the training data.

Each of those uses relies on a property that every honest posterior has and that can be checked with data. As the training set grows, a posterior for the regression function concentrates: under regularity conditions its standard deviation at a fixed input falls like one over the square root of the sample size (the Bernstein–von Mises theorem in its parametric form; van der Vaart, 1998, chapter 10). That is what makes the word "epistemic" mean something. Kendall and Gal (2017), in the paper that popularised the distinction between epistemic and aleatoric uncertainty in deep learning, define the first by exactly this property: it "can be explained away given enough data". Uncertainty about the function should shrink when there is more evidence about the function, and should grow where there is little evidence, whatever the noise in the outcome is doing.

This article tests MC dropout against that property, on real data, in four steps. A linear model where every quantity has a closed form shows the mechanism. A network trained on growing subsets of a large UCI data set shows whether the spread shrinks with data. A rerun of the UCI benchmark that made the method popular shows how much of its score comes from the spread at all. And a power-plant data set with a gap and a tail removed from training shows whether the spread rises where the data stop. A last experiment asks what the spread is still good for when it is used as a score to rank predictions rather than as a probability. The networks and the Gaussian process used as a reference were implemented for the article in NumPy, with scikit-learn used only to fit the Gaussian process hyperparameters; the code is linked at the end.

## What the derivation promises, and what it leaves free

The argument of Gal and Ghahramani is worth stating precisely, because the experiments below test the object it produces rather than the argument. Write each weight matrix of the network as a matrix of learned values multiplied, column by column, by independent Bernoulli variables that keep a unit with probability p and zero it otherwise. That is a probability distribution over weights, a very particular one: every column of every matrix is either its learned value or exactly zero. Treat it as a variational approximation q to the posterior of the weights under a Gaussian prior and a Gaussian likelihood with precision τ. The variational objective, the expected log-likelihood under q minus the Kullback–Leibler divergence from q to the prior, can be approximated by a single sampled mask per data point, and the result is, up to constants, the ordinary dropout training loss: squared error on each example under its own random mask, plus an L2 penalty on the weights. The weight-decay coefficient λ, the prior length-scale l, the keep probability p, the sample size N and the precision τ are tied by τ = p l² / (2 N λ). At prediction time, sampling masks from q and averaging gives the predictive mean; the predictive variance is the sample variance of the T stochastic outputs plus the constant 1/τ.

Two things in this construction decide how the resulting spread behaves, and both are visible before any experiment. The first is the shape of q. Its variance does not come from a scale parameter that the data can shrink. A column that is either w or zero has variance p(1 − p)w², which depends on the learned value w and on p and on nothing else. As N grows, the expected log-likelihood term of the objective grows with N while the Kullback–Leibler term does not, so a flexible q would be pulled towards a point mass, which is what concentration means. This q cannot move towards a point mass unless p moves towards one. In the derivation p is a variational parameter, and Gal, Hron and Kendall (2017) later proposed learning it by gradient descent ("concrete dropout"), noting that as N grows the dropout probability is pushed towards zero. In practice it is a fixed hyperparameter, set by hand or by a search over a short grid, and the spread of the stochastic passes is then a function of p and of the size of the learned weights. Osband, Aslanides and Cassirer (2018) stated the consequence as a lemma: with a fixed rate the dropout distribution does not concentrate with observed data, and it is unchanged if every training point is duplicated. Verdoja and Kyrki (2021) worked through a linear example and found the same independence from the sample size, together with a variance that grows with the square of the target.

The second is that the noise precision τ is not estimated by the method. It enters the predictive variance as an additive constant. In the regression benchmark of the 2016 paper it was tuned by Bayesian optimisation of the validation log-likelihood, with the dropout rate fixed at 0.05 or 0.005; in the public code, revised in 2018 after a reader found that the tuning had leaked test points, τ and the dropout rate are chosen together by grid search on a validation split. A tuned constant can make any predictive distribution well calibrated on average, whether or not the stochastic part of it carries any information. The fair question about MC dropout is therefore not whether the derivation is correct, but what the quantity that practitioners compute, with a fixed p and a tuned τ, actually measures.

Other work has questioned the derivation itself. Hron, Matthews and Ghahramani (2018) showed that the variational objectives behind Bernoulli and Gaussian dropout are ill-posed in their original form, because the approximating distribution is singular with respect to the prior, and proposed a corrected divergence; Le Folgoc and colleagues (2021) showed on regression problems with known posteriors that MC dropout changes the Bayesian model it claims to approximate and can assign no probability to the true function. Those critiques concern whether the method is variational inference at all. The experiments below do not depend on how that question is settled: they measure the behaviour of the number people report, whatever it approximates.

## A linear model where every quantity is exact

The cleanest place to see what the spread measures is a model small enough to solve by hand, and a linear regression with dropout on its inputs is that model. It is less artificial than it looks. The networks in the reference MC dropout experiments drop their inputs as well as their hidden units, and the last layer of any network trained with dropout is a linear regression on its hidden features with dropout applied to them, so the formula below is exactly the contribution of the last layer to the spread, conditional on everything beneath it.

With inverted dropout and keep probability q, each input x_j is multiplied by r_j / q, where r_j is 1 with probability q and 0 otherwise. Averaging the squared error over the masks gives ordinary least squares plus a penalty λ Σ_j w_j² Σ_i x_ij², with λ = (1 − q) / q, which is the ridge-type equivalence Srivastava and colleagues derived for linear regression (2014, section 9.1); for generalised linear models the penalty is weighted by the Fisher information instead (Wager, Wang and Liang, 2013). The trained weights are therefore available in closed form, and so is the MC dropout distribution of the regression function at a new point x: its mean is b + x′w and its variance is

```
Var_dropout[f(x)] = λ · Σ_j x_j² w_j²,     λ = (1 − q) / q
```

No sampling is needed; the code checks the formula against 400,000 stochastic passes, which agree with it to within 0.2%. The posterior of the same regression function under a flat prior and known noise variance σ² has variance σ²(1/n + x′(X′X)⁻¹x), where X holds the centred covariates. The two formulas already contain the whole story. The posterior depends on the sample size through (X′X)⁻¹, which shrinks like 1/n. The dropout variance depends on the sample size only through the fitted weights w, which converge to a fixed limit, and it depends on the size of those weights, squared.

To put numbers on it with realistic covariates, I took the four inputs of the UCI combined-cycle power plant data (ambient temperature, exhaust vacuum, ambient pressure and relative humidity, 9,568 hourly averages), standardised them, and simulated the output from the full-data least-squares fit, so that the true regression function is known. The fitted slopes are −14.74, −2.97, 0.37 and −2.31 megawatts per standard deviation of each input, and the residual standard deviation is 4.56 MW. The temperature and vacuum inputs are correlated at 0.84, which will matter in a moment. For each sample size from 50 to 100,000 rows (resampling covariate rows when n exceeds the data), I drew 200 data sets, fitted the dropout regression at three keep probabilities that span the dropout rates of the reference benchmark grid (0.005, 0.05 and 0.1), and computed the spread of f(x) at 2,000 fixed test points.

![Figure 1](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/mc-dropout-posterior/figures/fig1_linear_floor.png)

*Figure 1. Left: standard deviation of the regression function, averaged over 2,000 test points, against the number of training rows, for the Bayesian posterior and for MC dropout at three dropout rates (exact formulas, 200 simulated data sets per sample size). Dotted lines are the limits as n grows. Right: coverage of the nominal 95% interval for the true regression function.*

The left panel of Figure 1 is the result. The posterior standard deviation falls from 1.46 MW at 50 rows to 0.031 MW at 100,000, a factor of 47, close to the factor of 45 that the square-root law predicts for a 2,000-fold increase in data. The MC dropout spread does not move. At a dropout rate of 0.005 it is 0.93 MW at 50 rows and 0.92 MW at 100,000; at 0.05 it is 2.60 MW throughout; at 0.1 it is 3.46 MW throughout, three quarters of the noise standard deviation, for a four-parameter regression estimated from a hundred thousand rows. The limits computed from the asymptotic weights are 0.920, 2.602 and 3.461 MW, and the simulated curves sit on them from the smallest sample onwards. At 100,000 rows, the dropout spread at rate 0.05 is 84 times the posterior standard deviation.

The centre of the dropout distribution does not converge to the truth either. Multiplicative noise on the inputs is a form of measurement error, and it has the familiar consequences: the temperature slope is attenuated from −14.74 to −11.88 at rate 0.05 and to −10.27 at rate 0.1, and part of the lost effect leaks into the correlated vacuum input, whose slope grows from −2.97 to −4.55 and −5.22. The average absolute bias of the MC mean at the test points converges to 0.12, 0.92 and 1.48 MW at the three rates.

The right panel shows what this does to an interval. At the smallest rate, the nominal 95% interval covers the true regression function 72% of the time with 50 rows, because the spread is narrower than the posterior there, reaches 94% at about 400 rows, and then keeps climbing to 100% from 36,000 rows on, because the posterior has kept shrinking and the dropout spread has not. The interval is right at one sample size and wrong in opposite directions on either side of it. At the larger rates it covers between 96% and 98% at every sample size above a few hundred, even though its centre is biased, because the spread is large enough to swallow the bias. Coverage alone cannot tell a correct interval from one that is too wide and off-centre.

![Figure 2](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/mc-dropout-posterior/figures/fig2_effect_size.png)

*Figure 2. Standard deviation of the regression function at n = 2,000 when every slope of the simulation is multiplied by c, with the noise and the sample size unchanged. The posterior standard deviation does not depend on the size of the effect; the MC dropout spread is proportional to it.*

Figure 2 isolates the second ingredient of the formula. Keep the noise and the sample size fixed at 2,000 rows and multiply every true slope by a constant c. The posterior standard deviation is 0.22 MW whatever c is, because the uncertainty about a regression function depends on the noise and the design, not on how steep the function is. The dropout spread is proportional to c: at rate 0.05 it is 0.055 MW when there is no relationship at all, 2.60 MW at the actual slopes and 7.80 MW at three times the actual slopes. A model that has learned that nothing matters reports near-certainty; a model that has learned a strong effect from the same amount of data reports large uncertainty about it. This is the behaviour Verdoja and Kyrki (2021) found analytically in their linear example, where the variance grows with the square of the target, and it is the first thing a practitioner should know about the number: in the parts of the model the formula covers, it measures the magnitude of what was learned, scaled by the dropout rate.

## A real network on a growing training set

A linear model makes the mechanism visible but leaves open whether a nonlinear network, whose hidden features are themselves learned, behaves the same way. The protein tertiary structure data from the UCI repository are large enough to find out. Each row describes a protein structure by nine physicochemical summaries, and the target is the root-mean-square deviation of the structure from the native one, in ångström. The reference MC dropout experiments use it with a published train/test split, and I kept their architecture for it exactly: one hidden layer of 100 ReLU units, dropout before both dense layers, the weight decay implied by their formula with the noise precision their grid search picked on every protein split.

From the 41,157 training rows of split 0, I drew nested subsets of 500, 1,000, 2,000 and so on up to 32,000 rows, twice with different random orders, and evaluated every model on the same 4,573 test rows. Each subset was split 80/20. The networks were fitted to the first 80% and kept the weights with the lowest validation error among 100 checkpoints, so that no model at any size is a badly overfitted or badly underfitted version of itself. Without that, some of the small-sample networks were worse than predicting the mean, and a comparison of their spreads would have been a comparison of training accidents.

Five kinds of spread were measured at every test point. MC dropout at fixed rates from 0.005 to 0.5. MC dropout with the rate tuned exactly as the reference protocol tunes it, by the best validation log-likelihood over its grid of rates and noise precisions. A bootstrap: ten networks with dropout rate 0.05, each trained on a resample of the fitted rows and evaluated with every unit kept, so that their disagreement is the sampling variability of the fitted regression function, which is the quantity an epistemic uncertainty is supposed to estimate. A deep ensemble of five such networks on the same rows, differing only in initialisation and minibatch order (Lakshminarayanan, Pritzel and Blundell, 2017). And an exact Gaussian-process posterior with an automatic-relevance squared-exponential kernel, whose hyperparameters were estimated once per replicate on 1,500 rows and then held fixed, so that the amount of data is the only thing that changes; it stops at 16,000 rows because the next step, a 25,600 × 25,600 covariance matrix, would not fit in the memory of the machine used.

![Figure 3](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/mc-dropout-posterior/figures/fig3_concentration.png)

*Figure 3. Left: standard deviation of the regression function at a test point, averaged over 4,573 test rows of the protein data, against the size of the training subset; vertical bars span the two replicate subsets. Right: test RMSE of each model's mean prediction.*

The right panel of Figure 3 first establishes that every model learns. The test RMSE of the tuned MC dropout network falls from 5.30 to 4.41 as the subset grows from 500 to 32,000 rows, the bootstrap mean from 5.34 to 4.62, and the GP from 5.49 to 4.68 at 16,000. The one exception is dropout at rate 0.5, the default that Srivastava and colleagues recommended for hidden units in large networks; on a 100-unit network with nine inputs it regularises so heavily that its RMSE is 5.52 at 500 rows and 5.50 at 32,000, and more data teach it nothing.

The left panel is the test. The bootstrap spread falls from 1.35 at 500 rows to 0.48 at 32,000. The GP posterior falls from 4.42 to 1.96 at 16,000. On the log–log scale their slopes above 4,000 rows are −0.22 and −0.27. Those are slower than the −0.5 of a parametric model, as they should be for a flexible function in nine dimensions, where nonparametric rates are slower than the square root; but they are rates, and quadrupling the data takes away a quarter to a third of the uncertainty. The MC dropout spread at rate 0.05 is 1.68 at 500 rows, settles at 1.32 by 4,000 rows, and is 1.31 at 32,000. Its slope above 4,000 rows is +0.003. At rate 0.1 the slope is −0.003, at rate 0.5 it is +0.011, and at rate 0.005 it is −0.009. Above a few thousand rows, eight times more data leaves the MC dropout spread exactly where it was. At 32,000 rows, the spread at rate 0.05 is 2.7 times the bootstrap estimate of how much the fitted function would change on a new sample of the same size.

The small decline below 4,000 rows is not concentration either. At 500 rows the networks with the lowest rates kept checkpoints from less than a tenth of their training steps in both replicates, so they had learned less, and the formula of the previous section says the spread is smaller for a network that has learned less; at rate 0.005 the spread actually rises from 0.65 at 500 rows to 0.93 at 2,000, as the network learns larger weights, and then stays there. What the spread follows is the size of what the network has learned, not the amount of evidence for it.

The tuned curve is the most instructive, because it is what a careful practitioner following the reference protocol would get. At 500 rows the two replicates chose rates of 0.005 and 0.1; at 1,000 rows both chose 0.05; from 2,000 rows on they chose 0.005 or 0.01, and from 8,000 rows on both chose 0.005, the smallest rate the grid offers. The tuned spread falls from 1.21 to 0.89 over the range, a slope of −0.13, but all of that decline comes from the tuning procedure stepping down the grid, and it stops when the grid ends. That is the mechanism by which a learned dropout rate can shrink the spread, and Gal, Hron and Kendall (2017) show the same thing for concrete dropout, whose learned rates fall as the training set grows. It is worth being precise about what it implies. The spread then shrinks because the validation likelihood prefers less noise in the network, not because the posterior is updating, and nothing ties the rate at which the selected dropout rate falls to the rate at which uncertainty about the function falls. The validation data are asking for a more deterministic network; the next section shows that on the benchmark that made the method popular, they ask for it on three data sets out of four.

The deep ensemble deserves a sentence of its own, because it is the usual recommendation in place of MC dropout and because it fails the same test less badly rather than passing it. Its spread falls from 0.71 to 0.39, but almost all of that happens below 4,000 rows; above it, the slope is −0.04. Five networks trained on the same rows disagree because they end in different local optima (Fort, Hu and Lakshminarayanan, 2019, describe ensembles as sampling several modes where dropout stays near one), and that disagreement shrinks only slowly with data. It is also smaller than the bootstrap spread at every size, which means that, on these data, an ensemble that does not resample the training set understates how much the fitted function depends on the particular sample. Neither number is a posterior; the bootstrap is the one that measures what the word "epistemic" promises.

## Where the benchmark score comes from

MC dropout earned its reputation as an uncertainty method largely on one table: test RMSE and test log-likelihood on a set of UCI regression data sets, under the protocol Hernández-Lobato and Adams (2015) introduced for probabilistic backpropagation. Each data set is split 90/10 into training and test rows 20 times (5 times for protein), a network with one hidden layer of 50 units (100 for protein) is trained on each split, and the average over splits is reported. The test log-likelihood is the score that rewards good uncertainty, because it punishes a predictive distribution that is too narrow where the model is wrong and too wide where it is right. Gal and Ghahramani (2016) used it to compare MC dropout with probabilistic backpropagation and variational inference, and the same table, with the same protocol, has been reused in many comparisons since.

The public code was revised in 2018, after a reader found that hyperparameters tuned on some splits had been evaluated on test rows of others. The revised protocol, which is what I reran, works split by split: the first 80% of the training rows train one network for every pair of dropout rate and noise precision τ in a small grid, the last 20% pick the pair with the best MC log-likelihood, and a network with that pair is retrained on all the training rows and scored on the test rows. The grids are files in the repository. Every data set offers the same four dropout rates, 0.005, 0.01, 0.05 and 0.1, and three values of τ chosen per data set. The repository's README says the networks were trained for ten times the listed number of epochs, but its committed result files, the ones whose averages the README reports, are named for a hundred times, and with my implementation only the hundredfold run reaches the reported accuracy on the small data sets. I used a hundredfold, which is 4,000 passes over the data for most sets. I left out the Boston housing data, whose variable B is built from the proportion of Black residents in each town and which scikit-learn removed for that reason.

The rerun matches the published numbers on the four small data sets, all 20 splits each. Yacht hydrodynamics: RMSE 0.66 and test log-likelihood −1.24, against a published 0.67 and −1.25. Concrete strength: 4.85 and −2.95, against 4.82 and −2.93. Energy efficiency: 0.54 and −1.21, against 0.54 and −1.21. Red wine quality: 0.63 and −0.94, against 0.62 and −0.93. Every difference is within about one published standard error, so the networks below are the networks of the benchmark. The four larger data sets take between 8 and 35 minutes per split on a CPU and are not part of this rerun; the largest of them, protein, was studied on its own in the previous section.

The first thing the rerun shows is what the grid search chooses when it is allowed to choose. On yacht it picked the smallest dropout rate, 0.005, on 18 of 20 splits and 0.01 on the other two; on concrete, 0.005 on 16 of 20; on energy, 0.005 on all 20. Wine is the exception, with 0.05 on 12 splits and 0.1 on 6. The noise precision tells the same story from the other side. On yacht and energy the search chose the largest τ in the grid on all 20 splits, which is the smallest noise variance on offer, and on concrete the largest τ on 11 of 20. The original result files show the same choices: τ = 0.75 on every yacht and every energy split. When the validation likelihood decides, it asks for as little dropout noise as it is given and for less observation noise than it is allowed.

The ceiling on τ matters more than it looks. On energy the grid's smallest noise standard deviation, 1/√τ, is 1.15, while the test RMSE of the mean prediction is 0.54, so the predictive distribution is forced to be about twice as wide as the errors. The MC dropout spread then makes it wider still.

![Figure 4](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/mc-dropout-posterior/figures/fig4_benchmark_score.png)

*Figure 4. Left: share of the predictive variance that comes from the spread of the stochastic passes rather than from the tuned constant 1/τ, averaged over test points. Right: test log-likelihood of the MC dropout predictive distribution minus that of a Gaussian without the dropout spread, either with the same tuned variance (circles) or with the validation residual variance of the selected network (squares). Means and 95% intervals over the published splits.*

The left panel of Figure 4 splits the predictive variance into its two parts. Averaged over test points, the dropout spread contributes 21% of the predictive variance on yacht, 24% on concrete, 24% on energy and 12% on wine; the rest is the constant 1/τ, tuned on validation data. At the single test point where the spread matters most, its share reaches 91% on yacht and 52% to 59% on the others. On average, three quarters or more of the uncertainty the benchmark scores is a number picked from a list of three.

The right panel asks whether the quarter that comes from dropout earns its place. Replace the MC dropout predictive distribution, a mixture of T Gaussians centred on the stochastic outputs, by a single Gaussian centred on their mean with the same tuned variance 1/τ, and score both on the same test rows. On energy the Gaussian is better, by 0.040 nats per test point (standard error 0.001): the spread makes the score worse. On yacht the difference is 0.009 (standard error 0.026), indistinguishable from zero. On wine the spread helps by 0.028 (0.006) and on concrete by 0.12 (0.03). The squares make a sharper comparison. A Gaussian whose variance is simply the mean squared validation residual of the selected network, with no dropout spread and no grid, beats MC dropout on energy by 0.38 nats per test point (standard error 0.025) and is within noise of it on yacht; MC dropout keeps an advantage of 0.075 on concrete and 0.018 on wine.

Two more numbers complete the picture. The weight decay that the reference formula implies, l²(1 − p)/(2Nτ) with l = 0.01, is between about 10⁻¹³ and 2 × 10⁻⁶ across the data sets and grid points. In the variational reading, weight decay is the prior; at these values the prior does essentially nothing, and the "posterior" is shaped by the dropout rate and the training procedure. And the τ grids themselves are set per data set in the neighbourhood of the error the networks reach: on concrete and wine the three values bracket it, on yacht and energy they stop short of it, which is why the search lands on the edge there. That is reasonable engineering for a benchmark, but it means the log-likelihood column largely records how well a tuned constant matches the residual variance, and only partly whether the stochastic passes know anything about where the model is wrong.

## Does the spread rise where the data stop?

The use that matters most in practice is the one the benchmark does not test: a model deployed on inputs unlike the ones it was trained on should say so. A posterior does this by construction, because where there are no data there is nothing to shrink it. Foong, Burt, Li and Turner (2020) proved that for networks with a single hidden layer the two most common approximations cannot do it between two clouds of data: for mean-field Gaussian variational inference the variance at a point on the segment between two inputs is bounded by the sum of the variances at the ends, and for MC dropout without input dropout the variance is a convex function of the input, so it cannot have a bump in a gap. Their theorems cover one hidden layer. The reference architecture drops its inputs as well, which their MC dropout theorem excludes, and with two hidden layers a network can in principle express the right behaviour (their universality result for that case also assumes the inputs are not dropped). An experiment on real data settles what happens with the networks people actually train.

The combined-cycle power-plant data (Tüfekci, 2014) have a natural axis of shift: ambient temperature, which ranges from 2 °C to 37 °C over 9,568 hourly averages recorded over six years and drives the plant's output, which ranges from 420 MW to 496 MW and is highest on cold days. I removed two slabs of the data from training. The gap is the band between the 45th and 60th percentiles of temperature, 18.9 °C to 22.8 °C, which sits in the middle of the range. The tail is everything above the 85th percentile, 28.0 °C, the hottest 15% of the hours, which a model trained on the rest has never seen. Of the remaining rows, 15% at random became the in-distribution test set and the other 5,697 the training set. Four models were fitted to the same training rows: MC dropout with one hidden layer of 50 units, rate and τ tuned by the reference protocol; the same with two hidden layers; a deep ensemble of five networks without dropout, with a noise variance estimated on validation rows; and an exact GP. The networks ran with three seeds.

![Figure 5](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/mc-dropout-posterior/figures/fig5_shift.png)

*Figure 5. Epistemic standard deviation at each test row of the power-plant data against ambient temperature. The shaded bands were removed from training. Blue: in-distribution test rows; orange: the gap; green: the tail. One seed per network method.*

The errors confirm that the tail is genuinely harder. Test RMSE in the tail is 4.68 MW for one-layer MC dropout against 3.80 in distribution, 4.51 against 3.60 for two layers, 4.62 against 3.71 for the ensemble and 4.24 against 3.86 for the GP. The gap turns out to be easy for every model, with RMSE within 0.2 MW of the in-distribution value: 3.8 °C of missing temperature is bridged by the other three inputs and by the smoothness of the relationship.

Figure 5 shows what each model's spread does about it. The GP behaves as a posterior should. Its spread is flat across the training temperatures at about 0.33 MW, rises slightly in the gap to 0.43, and climbs steeply into the tail, where it averages 1.37 MW and exceeds 5 MW at the hottest hours: 4.1 times its in-distribution level. Ranked by its spread, a tail row is above an in-distribution row 97% of the time (an AUROC of 0.97). The one-layer MC dropout spread has a different shape altogether. It is U-shaped in temperature, lowest around 17 °C, near the middle of the training temperatures, and rising towards both ends of the range. The cold end, which is well covered by training data, gets as much spread as the unseen hot end; a 5 °C hour inside the training range gets a spread of 2 to 3 MW, about the same as a 32 °C hour outside it. In the gap the spread is lower than on in-distribution rows, by a factor of 0.74, and the AUROC for telling gap rows from in-distribution rows by their spread is 0.31: the gap rows look more familiar to the model than the rows it was trained around.

The U-shape is the formula of the linear section at work. With dropout on the inputs, the spread contains a term proportional to Σ x_j² w_j² in the standardised inputs, which grows with the squared distance of each input from the training mean and has nothing to do with whether training data exist there. That one term reproduces most of what the panel shows: the minimum at the training mean, the symmetric rise, the dip in a gap that happens to sit near the centre. The two-layer network has the same shape, flatter; its tail spread is only 1.11 times its in-distribution spread and the AUROC falls to 0.64. The deep ensemble does better than both, with a ratio of 1.39 in the tail and 1.13 in the gap, but its separation of tail from training rows (AUROC 0.69) is far from the GP's.

Coverage turns the shapes into consequences. On in-distribution rows the nominal 90% intervals cover 91% to 93% for every method, because τ or its equivalent was tuned for exactly those rows. In the tail the GP still covers 93%. The ensemble falls to 86%, one-layer MC dropout to 88% on average, with a seed as low as 81%, and two-layer MC dropout to 83%. A plant operator who used the MC dropout interval to size a reserve margin would be well served on ordinary days and short of cover on precisely the hot days the model had not seen, which are the days when output is lowest and the margin matters most.

## A ranking score, not a probability

A number can fail every test of a posterior and still be useful for a narrower job. The most common job MC dropout does in practice is ranking: send the predictions with the largest spread to a person, abstain on them, or label those inputs next. For that, the scale of the spread is irrelevant and only its order matters, so the fair test is whether discarding the inputs with the largest spread removes the largest errors.

On the benchmark runs above I computed a risk–coverage curve (Geifman and El-Yaniv, 2017, use the same construction for classification): sort the test rows by a score, keep the fraction with the lowest scores, and report the RMSE of the MC dropout prediction on the rows kept, relative to the RMSE on all of them. Four scores rank the same predictions. The MC dropout standard deviation. The spread of a five-member deep ensemble trained alongside on the same rows. The mean distance from the input to its ten nearest training rows in standardised units, which needs no model at all. And the realised absolute error itself, which no score can beat and which shows how much room there is.

![Figure 6](https://raw.githubusercontent.com/DiogoRibeiro7/Medium-Blog/main/mc-dropout-posterior/figures/fig6_risk_coverage.png)

*Figure 6. Risk–coverage curves on the four benchmark data sets: RMSE of the MC dropout predictions on the test rows kept, relative to all rows, when the rows with the highest score are discarded. Means over the 20 published splits.*

The answer, in Figure 6, depends on the data set, and that is the finding. On yacht the MC dropout spread is a good ranking: keeping the 80% of test rows with the lowest spread cuts the RMSE to 0.65 of its full value, against 0.77 for the ensemble spread, 0.79 for distance to the training rows and 0.47 for the unattainable ceiling, and its rank correlation with the absolute error is 0.30. On concrete it carries no information at all. Its rank correlation with the error is −0.01, and keeping the 80% with the lowest spread leaves the RMSE at 0.99 of its full value, which is what discarding rows at random does, while the ensemble spread and the distance to the training rows reach 0.88 and 0.86. On energy and wine all three scores are weak and close together: 0.94, 0.95 and 0.98 on energy, and 0.93, 0.94 and 0.90 on wine. Averaged over the four data sets, the RMSE kept at 80% coverage is 0.88 for MC dropout, 0.89 for the ensemble and 0.88 for distance to the training data, against a ceiling of 0.54.

Concrete is the case worth remembering. On that data set the dropout spread improves the benchmark log-likelihood by more than anywhere else, 0.12 nats per test point over a Gaussian with the same τ, and it ranks the errors no better than chance. The two facts are compatible, and the likely reason is that a spread that varies from point to point turns the predictive distribution into a heavier-tailed mixture, which scores better on a few large errors whichever points they occur at. A better log-likelihood is evidence that the predictive distribution has the right overall shape, not that its uncertainty knows where the model is wrong.

As a ranking, then, the MC dropout spread is sometimes informative, sometimes useless, and on average no better than the distance from the input to the training data, a score that costs nothing to compute and makes no claim to be Bayesian. Which of those it is on a given problem cannot be known from the theory and can be measured in minutes on a validation split.

## What to put in front of a decision instead

None of this makes MC dropout useless, and none of it says the variational derivation is wrong. It says that the number practitioners compute, with a fixed rate and a tuned noise term, is not the uncertainty its name promises, and that the uses that depend on the promise need something else or a check. Five practices follow from the experiments, in the order a project meets them.

The first is to decide which quantity the decision needs. An interval for the next observation, which is what a reserve margin, a price band or a clinical threshold needs, is a statement about y, and most of its width is noise. An interval for the regression function, which is what an estimate of an effect or a comparison of two configurations needs, is a statement about f(x), and its width should shrink with data. MC dropout mixes the two, a tuned constant for the first and a spread for the second, and calibration of the sum on held-out data says nothing about either part. The experiments here show the constant doing most of the work in the benchmark and the spread failing the test that defines the second part.

The second is that if the decision needs an interval for y with a guaranteed coverage, the model's own uncertainty is not the tool. Split conformal prediction (Vovk, Gammerman and Shafer, 2005; Lei, G'Sell, Rinaldo, Tibshirani and Wasserman, 2018) takes any point predictor, measures its absolute residuals on a calibration set, and returns intervals whose coverage on exchangeable new data is guaranteed in finite samples, without any assumption about the model. With a scale estimate in the denominator of the residual it adapts the width to the input. If the MC dropout spread is informative, it can serve as that scale, and the conformal step will expose it if it is not; if the conditional distribution of the errors has to be right across its whole range, recalibration methods such as Kuleshov, Fenner and Ermon (2018) apply. In both cases the guarantee comes from held-out data, not from the network's internal noise, and on shifted inputs neither guarantee holds, which brings back the third practice.

The third is that if the decision needs epistemic uncertainty, the quantity should be checked for the property that defines it. The check is cheap and needs nothing but the training data: refit the model on random halves and quarters of the training set and see whether its reported uncertainty at fixed test points grows as the data shrink. The bootstrap passes this test by construction, because it resamples the data the estimate depends on (Efron, 1979), and so does an exact posterior. On the protein data MC dropout fails it above a few thousand rows, and a deep ensemble without resampling barely passes. When the check fails, the number may still be useful, but it should not be called epistemic and it should not be expected to fall when more data are collected.

The fourth is that if the number will be used to rank inputs, for abstention, review or active learning, it should be evaluated as a ranking on held-out data, against a free baseline. Distance to the nearest training rows costs nothing, makes no claim to be Bayesian, and on the four benchmark data sets above ranks errors about as well as MC dropout does on average. A risk–coverage curve on a validation split, computed before deployment, shows which score earns the abstention budget on the problem at hand; the answer on yacht and the answer on concrete were opposite.

The fifth is to test the shift that deployment will bring, not the shift the benchmark brings. On a random test split every method in the shift experiment looked calibrated. Holding out a region of the input space that matters (the hottest days, the newest customers, the largest orders) and looking at coverage there is the only way to learn whether the uncertainty will be there when it is needed. On the power-plant data it was there for the GP and was not for any of the networks.

## Three conjectures

The experiments support the claims above on the data sets used here. Three further claims seem to follow from them but were not tested, and they are stated as conjectures.

The first concerns learned dropout rates. Concrete dropout lets the rate fall as the data grow, and the protein experiment shows the same effect through grid search. The conjecture is that the speed of that fall is set by the entropy term of the variational objective, which scales with the number of units divided by the number of data points, rather than by the curvature of the likelihood, so that a learned-rate spread will shrink at a rate that matches the posterior only by coincidence. A concentration curve like Figure 3 for concrete dropout, against a bootstrap, would settle it.

The second concerns where dropout sits. The linear formula says that dropout before a layer contributes a spread proportional to the squared size of that layer's inputs times its squared weights. The conjecture is that in large networks with dropout in only a few places, as in transformers, the MC spread is dominated by the norms of the activations and weights at those places, and will therefore track input magnitude, as the U-shape in Figure 5 tracks temperature, more than distance from the training distribution.

The third concerns classification. The arguments here were made for regression with a Gaussian likelihood, where the noise term is a separate constant. In classification there is no τ, and the spread of the softmax outputs carries all of the uncertainty. The conjecture is that the non-concentration persists, because nothing in the mask distribution depends on the sample size, but that its consequences are larger, because there is no tuned constant to absorb the miscalibration; Ovadia and colleagues (2019) found deep ensembles ahead of dropout on most of their shift benchmarks, but dropout ahead on the Brier score of their Criteo click-prediction task at heavy shift, which suggests the answer depends on the data in ways a single benchmark will not reveal.

## What the spread is

Put the experiments together and the spread of MC dropout has a recognisable shape. It is proportional to the size of what the network has learned, through the squared weights; it grows with the distance of the input from the training mean, through the squared inputs; it is scaled by the dropout rate; and it does not depend on how many data points the network was trained on, beyond the effect of the data on the size of the weights. A posterior has none of those properties and has the one this lacks. The benchmark that established the method scores the sum of this spread and a constant tuned on held-out data, and the constant does most of the work; when the tuning is free to choose, it usually reduces the dropout to the smallest rate it is offered.

That is a description of a heuristic, and heuristics can be useful. On some data sets the spread ranks the errors well; on others it ranks them no better than a coin. What a data scientist cannot do with it is the thing it is most often used for: present it as the model's knowledge of its own ignorance, and expect it to shrink when more data arrive or to grow when the inputs drift away from the training data. Both expectations can be tested in an afternoon on the data at hand, and they should be, before the number is put in front of a decision.
