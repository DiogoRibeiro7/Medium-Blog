\title{When Does a More Complex Change-Point Detector Justify Its Cost?\\A Preregistered Monte Carlo Comparison of CUSUM, GLR, and BOCPD}
\author{Diogo Ribeiro}
\date{26 October 2026}

\begin{abstract}
An alarm does not determine whether a model should be retrained. We compare the economic consequences of retraining policies driven by classical CUSUM, a Gaussian generalised likelihood-ratio (GLR) detector, and Bayesian online change-point detection (BOCPD). In the preregistered synthetic experiment, 199 independent null-calibration streams established detector thresholds; 100 evaluation streams per regime, each with 120 deployment observations, were used to assess the policies. Under gradual concept drift, mean economic regret was 0.217649 for CUSUM, 0.217060 for GLR and 0.226625 for BOCPD. The GLR--CUSUM regret difference had an adjusted paired interval of $[-0.004824,0.003344]$, which does not establish the specified minimum improvement of 0.01 regret units. BOCPD had higher regret (difference 0.008977; adjusted interval $[0.003687,0.013818]$). Neither candidate verified the required 10\% false-alarm ceiling. The data do not justify replacing CUSUM under this monitoring score, drift mechanism and cost specification.
\end{abstract}
\section{Introduction}
Sequential detection is often assessed through alarm rates and detection delay. Neither quantity determines whether acting on an alarm improves an economic decision. That depends on the retraining policy, its cost and the actions taken from the resulting predictions.

We compare classical cumulative sum (CUSUM) \cite{page1954}, Gaussian generalised likelihood ratios \cite{basseville1993}, and Bayesian online change-point detection (BOCPD) \cite{adams2007}. Each detector receives the same scalar monitoring signal. We evaluate their associated model-maintenance policies against prespecified economic-regret and false-alarm criteria; no new detection algorithm is proposed.
\section{Predictive and economic model}
Let $X_t\in\mathbb R^{16}$ and write $m_t(x)=\mathbb E[Y_t\mid X_t=x]$ for the synthetic conditional mean, with $\widehat m_t$ denoting the deployed regression estimate. The policy takes an action at cost $c$ when $\widehat m_t(X_t)>c$; refitting at time $t$ incurs cost $q$. Relative to an oracle with access to the complete conditional mean, one-step regret is
\begin{equation}
R_t=(m_t(X_t)-c)_+-(m_t(X_t)-c)\mathbf1\{\widehat m_t(X_t)>c\}+q\mathbf1\{\text{refit at }t\}.
\end{equation}
Regret accounts for action errors relative to the oracle and for model-maintenance costs. We use the true conditional mean only to evaluate simulated decisions. Neither the regression fit nor the detector observes it; the specification does not identify a causal treatment effect.

Eight independent Gaussian latent factors generate 16 predictors through correlated measurement pairs, with independent measurement-error standard deviation $0.12$. The response innovation has standard deviation $0.65$. In the primary mechanism the initial conditional mean is $10(X_{t1}-X_{t2})$. The coefficient remains fixed over the first third of deployment, then decreases linearly to its negative initial value at the final step. This controlled mechanism is not fitted to observational data.
\section{Sequential detectors}
A frozen reference regression $\widehat m_0$ is fitted on 160 observations. For a released label indexed by $j$, we compute $s_j=-(Y_j-\widehat m_0(X_j))\widehat m_0(X_j)/\widehat\sigma_\varepsilon$, with $\widehat\sigma_\varepsilon$ the standard deviation of reference-model fitting residuals. The detector input is $Z_j=(s_j-\bar s_{\mathrm{cal}})/\widehat\sigma_{s,\mathrm{cal}}$, where the location and scale are estimated from 80 separate predeployment calibration observations. With zero additional feedback delay, the label at $j$ becomes available before the prediction at $j+1$. Every detector receives this same scalar sequence. Consequently, the experiment does not compare general multivariate change detection.
\subsection{CUSUM}
For CUSUM, we use the one-sided recursion
\begin{equation}
G_t=\max\{0,G_{t-1}+Z_t-\kappa\}.
\end{equation}
The allowance is $\kappa=0.2$. Positive increments accumulate disagreement with the reference relation. Optimality results for classical CUSUM depend on the observation model and change hypothesis; they do not transfer automatically to this residual-derived score \cite{page1954,lorden1971}.
\subsection{Generalised likelihood ratio}
For the one-sided Gaussian GLR, we scan recent suffixes with lengths $m\in[m_{\min},m_{\max}]$:
\begin{equation}
\mathrm{GLR}_t=\max_m\frac{\left(\max\{0,\sum_{j=t-m+1}^t Z_j\}\right)^2}{2m}.
\end{equation}
The scan uses $m_{\min}=4$ and $m_{\max}=32$. This maximises the Gaussian log likelihood ratio over an unknown positive mean shift under a working unit-variance model. That variance is assumed after empirical standardisation rather than known from the generating mechanism. Dependence or scale misspecification can change the null distribution.
\subsection{Bayesian online change-point detection}
BOCPD updates the posterior run-length distribution $r_t$ using hazard $h=0.03$, a conjugate normal prior for the segment mean with precision $0.25$, and unit observation variance \cite{adams2007}. Following a 15-score warmup, the alarm statistic is the posterior mass on run lengths shorter than five. Its interpretation is conditional on this likelihood, hazard and prior; it is not a model-free probability of physical change.
\section{Preregistered comparison}
For each detector we computed the maximum statistic over the fixed monitoring horizon on 199 independent null streams. Given $n_{\mathrm{null}}$ maxima and target $\alpha$, the corrected threshold is their $k$th order statistic, $k=\lceil(n_{\mathrm{null}}+1)(1-\alpha)\rceil$. Here $n_{\mathrm{null}}=199$ and $\alpha=0.10$. The marginal false-alarm statement requires exchangeability of complete null-stream maxima over the same horizon; it does not extend to unrestricted sequential monitoring. Calibration and evaluation used disjoint simulated streams.

The primary protocol fixed a low-variance signal, gradual drift, immediate feedback, action cost $c=0.6$, refit cost $q=0.05$ and 120 deployment steps. Scheduled retraining occurs every 15 steps. After an alarm, the detector-triggered policy refits on the latest 100 eligible labelled observations and applies a 15-step cooldown. We evaluate 100 independent streams per regime, comparing GLR and BOCPD separately with CUSUM. The minimum economically meaningful improvement was 0.01 regret units per observation; the false-alarm ceiling was 10\%. We divided the nominal 5\% family error budget between paired economic comparisons and false-alarm bounds, adjusting for the two candidates.

For candidate $A$, define the stream-paired effect
\[
\Delta_r(A)=\overline R_r(A)-\overline R_r(\mathrm{CUSUM}).
\]
A negative $\Delta_r(A)$ favours the candidate. Each $\overline R_r$ averages 120 deployment steps. The paired bootstrap resamples independent stream-level differences, not adjacent observations. Its Monte Carlo intervals condition on the realised detector thresholds and omit repeated-calibration variability.

\section{Discussion and limitations}
GLR's average numerical advantage was smaller than the stipulated material-gain threshold, and its adjusted interval included zero. Scheduled retraining produced regret close to CUSUM. The aggregated results do not identify which alarm timings or refits explain those differences; attributing them to one detector mechanism would exceed the experiment.

The findings depend on one monitoring score, one response mechanism, the specified costs and the deployment horizon. Their applicability to multivariate or alternative change scores is unknown. Gradual coefficient reversal has no single abrupt change time against which detection latency can be evaluated.

The parameter settings were fixed, not optimised across mechanisms. We cannot separate the properties of BOCPD as a family from the suitability of the specified likelihood, hazard and alarm rule. Tuning on these evaluation streams would require a fresh confirmatory sample. Uncertainty from recalibrating the thresholds is also absent from the bootstrap limits. The economic payoff is synthetic and does not identify a causal intervention benefit.
\section{Conclusion}
Neither GLR nor BOCPD met the declared replacement criteria in this synthetic gradual-drift experiment. A more complex detector did not establish a material net-regret advantage at the specified costs. This result does not establish general optimality of CUSUM.
